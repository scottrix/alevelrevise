Atomic Structure and the Periodic Table

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC/Eduqas, CCEA

A* revision for atomic structure: isotopes, time-of-flight mass spectrometry, electron configuration and ionisation energy trends, mapped to AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC and CCEA specifications with exam-style questions.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Describe subatomic particles and use atomic number and mass number to deduce numbers of each particle
  • Define isotopes and calculate relative atomic mass from mass spectra of elements
  • Explain the four stages of time-of-flight mass spectrometry and perform time-of-flight calculations
  • Write electron configurations including transition metal ions, applying the 4s-removed-first rule and Cr/Cu exceptions
  • Explain trends in first ionisation energy across a period and down a group in terms of nuclear charge, distance and shielding
  • Interpret successive ionisation energy graphs as evidence for electron shells and deduce the group of an element
  • Predict and explain 3:1, 9:6:1 and 1:1 isotopic peak patterns for chlorine and bromine

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Isotope: Atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons (same Z, different A).
Relative atomic mass (Ar): The weighted mean mass of an atom of an element compared with 1/12 of the mass of one atom of carbon-12.
Relative isotopic mass: The mass of one atom of an isotope compared with 1/12 of the mass of one atom of carbon-12.
Orbital: A region within an atom that can hold up to two electrons with opposite spins.
Sub-shell: A group of orbitals of the same type (s, p, d or f) within a shell; s holds 2, p 6, d 10 electrons.
First ionisation energy: The enthalpy change when one mole of gaseous 1+ ions is formed from one mole of gaseous atoms.
Successive ionisation energy: The energy required to remove each further electron from a gaseous ion; a large jump indicates electron removal from a new inner shell.
Shielding: The repulsion of outer-shell electrons by inner-shell electrons, reducing the effective nuclear attraction they experience.

Fundamental Particles, Isotopes and Relative Atomic Mass

Atoms consist of a nucleus of protons and neutrons surrounded by electrons in orbitals. Protons and neutrons each have a relative mass of approximately 1 (the neutron is very slightly heavier at 1.0087), while the electron has a relative mass of 1/1836 — effectively all the mass sits in the nucleus. Protons carry a charge of +1, electrons carry −1 and neutrons are neutral, so an atom is neutral because the number of protons equals the number of electrons.

The atomic (proton) number Z defines the element and fixes its position in the periodic table; the mass number A is protons plus neutrons. Isotopes are atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons — same Z, different A. Isotopes have identical chemical properties (same electron configuration, so same bonding and reactions) but different physical properties such as mass, density and diffusion rate. Radioactive isotopes additionally have unstable nuclei.

Relative atomic mass (Ar) is defined as the weighted mean mass of an atom of an element compared with 1/12 of the mass of one atom of carbon-12. Relative isotopic mass is the mass of one isotope compared with the same standard — for light isotopes this equals the mass number. Relative molecular mass (Mr) is the sum of the relative atomic masses in a molecule; relative formula mass is used for ionic compounds.

Mass spectrometry of elements: a time-of-flight (TOF) instrument works in four stages. (1) Ionisation: in electron impact (EI) the vaporised sample is bombarded with high-energy electrons which knock one electron off each atom to give M+(g) ions; in electrospray ionisation the sample is dissolved and protonated to MH+, a gentler method suited to fragile biological molecules. (2) Acceleration: an electric field accelerates all ions to the same kinetic energy. (3) Ion drift: ions enter a field-free tube where lighter ions travel faster — time of flight is inversely proportional to √(mass), since KE = ½mv². (4) Detection: ions strike a detector producing a current proportional to their abundance. Exam calculations often ask you to find a time of flight ratio or the Ar: take each isotopic mass, multiply by its relative abundance (signal area), total these and divide by total abundance.

Electron Configuration, Orbitals and Sub-shell Notation

Electrons occupy orbitals — regions of space that can hold a maximum of two electrons with opposite spins. An s orbital is spherical; there are three p orbitals (px, py, pz) shaped like dumbbells at right angles; there are five d orbitals and seven f orbitals. Sub-shells group orbitals of the same type: s holds 2, p holds 6, d holds 10 electrons. Sub-shell energies increase in the order 1s < 2s < 2p < 3s < 3p < 4s < 3d < 4p, so the 4s orbital fills before 3d.

Two rules govern filling: the Pauli exclusion principle (no orbital holds more than two electrons, and they must have opposite spin) and Hund's rule (within a sub-shell electrons occupy orbitals singly with parallel spins before pairing up, minimising repulsion). Two exceptions must be known: chromium is [Ar]3d54s1 and copper is [Ar]3d104s1, because a half-filled or filled d sub-shell is more stable than the expected arrangement.

For ions, remember the rule: 4s electrons are removed first. Although 4s fills before 3d, once the 3d sub-shell is occupied it drops below 4s in energy, so the 4s orbital is emptied first during ionisation. Fe (Z=26): 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁶3d⁶4s²; Fe²⁺: 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁶3d⁶; Fe³⁺: 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁶3d⁵ (a stable half-filled d sub-shell, which is why Fe³⁺ is more stable than Fe²⁺). Other examples: Cl⁻ is 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁶ (isoelectronic with Ar); Ca²⁺ is [Ar]; Na⁺ is [Ne].

Writing configurations from a noble gas core saves time: Al = [Ne]3s²3p¹. Exam tip: when asked for the full electron configuration of a transition metal ion, always write the 3d before 4s in the final answer even though 4s filled first — most boards penalise 3d written after 4s for ions.

Ionisation Energies: Trends and Evidence for Shells

First ionisation energy is the enthalpy change when one mole of gaseous 1+ ions forms from one mole of gaseous atoms: Na(g) → Na⁺(g) + e⁻, ΔH = +496 kJ/mol. It is always endothermic — energy must be supplied to overcome the attraction between the nucleus and the electron. Successive ionisation energies measure removal of electrons one after another from the same atom, and each is larger than the last because the same positive charge attracts fewer electrons more strongly and the ion is increasingly positive.

Three factors control ionisation energy. Nuclear charge: more protons pull electrons harder. Distance: the further the outer electron is from the nucleus, the weaker the attraction. Shielding: inner shells repel outer electrons and screen them from the nuclear charge.

Trends: ionisation energy increases across a period — nuclear charge rises while shielding stays roughly constant because electrons enter the same outer shell, so Zeff increases. Small dips break the trend and are standard A*/explain questions: aluminium (3s²3p¹) has a lower first IE than magnesium (3s²) because the highest-energy electron is in the higher-energy 3p sub-shell, which is easier to remove; sulfur (3p⁴) has a lower first IE than phosphorus (3p³) because sulfur's paired 3p electrons repel each other, so one is easier to remove. Ionisation energy decreases down a group — each element has an extra full inner shell, so atomic radius and shielding both increase and outweigh the rise in nuclear charge.

Graphs of log(successive IE) against electron number provide the classic evidence for shells: within one shell the increase is gradual, but a large jump occurs when the next electron comes from a new, inner shell because it is much closer to the nucleus and far less shielded. Counting the number of electrons before the first big jump tells you the number of outer-shell electrons, hence the group. For example, an element with a huge jump after the fourth ionisation energy is in Group 4.

Mass Spectra of Elements and Isotopic Calculations

A mass spectrum of an element shows one line per isotope: the position on the m/z axis gives the isotopic mass and the height gives the relative abundance. From this you calculate Ar. Worked example: chlorine gives peaks at m/z 35 (75.0%) and m/z 37 (25.0%), so Ar = (35 × 75.0 + 37 × 25.0) / 100 = 35.5.

Two diatomic patterns are examined constantly. Chlorine (³⁵Cl 75%, ³⁷Cl 25%) gives molecular-ion peaks for Cl₂⁺ at m/z 70, 72 and 74 in the ratio 9:6:1 (0.75² : 2 × 0.75 × 0.25 : 0.25²), while any fragment containing one Cl atom (e.g. CH₃Cl⁺) appears as a doublet at m/z 49/51 or 35/37 in a 3:1 ratio. Bromine (⁷⁹Br 50%, ⁸¹Br 50%) gives 1:1 doublets, and Br₂⁺ peaks at m/z 158, 160, 160, 162 in the ratio 1:2:1. These ratios let you deduce how many halogen atoms a fragment contains — a favourite AS-level question.

TOF calculation style: two ions of the same charge given the same kinetic energy — the heavier ion flies more slowly. If ion A (mass mA) takes time tA and ion B takes tB, then tB/tA = √(mB/mA). Rearrange to find the unknown mass, then identify the isotope. Always work in kg for mass (divide by 1000 from gmol⁻¹ × 10³ to match SI joules) unless the question asks for a ratio.

Exam Technique and Practicals: Spectra and Ionisation Data

Mass spectra and ionisation energy graphs reward precise method writing. For TOF calculations, state the principle first — same charge, same kinetic energy, so velocity depends on mass — then apply tB/tA = √(mB/mA). For Ar calculations from abundance data, show the weighted-mean expression with all terms before evaluating; the expression itself carries method marks even if arithmetic slips. When asked to identify an element from successive ionisation energies, count the electrons before the large jump, state the number of outer-shell electrons, then name the group — three separate reasoning steps, three marks.

For ionisation energy trend questions, the mark scheme expects the three factors named explicitly: nuclear charge (number of protons), distance of the outer electron from the nucleus, and shielding by inner shells. 'More protons attract more strongly' alone scores one mark; adding 'same shell so shielding unchanged, so effective nuclear charge increases' scores the rest. For discontinuity explanations, name the sub-shells of both elements compared — Mg 3s² vs Al 3s²3p¹, or P 3p³ vs S 3p⁴ — before giving the reason (higher-energy sub-shell, or paired-electron repulsion).

Electron configuration answers: write full configurations in the order asked (AQA asks ascending order 1s first; for transition metal ions, 3d before 4s in the final answer), use superscript notation correctly, and double-check the electron count against the charge — an Fe²⁺ answer with 24 electrons is a silent error that costs a mark. Common traps: writing 4s² in a transition-metal ion's configuration; forgetting the Cr/Cu exceptions; treating isotopes as chemically different; giving ionisation energy as exothermic. Practise writing the causal chain — extra inner shell → more shielding and greater distance → weaker attraction → lower IE — because chained explanations are what separate A* scripts from A/B.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: The first ionisation energies of magnesium and aluminium are 738 and 578 kJ mol⁻¹ respectively. Explain why the first ionisation energy of aluminium is lower than that of magnesium despite aluminium having a higher nuclear charge.
Mark-scheme logic: Aluminium's outer electron is in the 3p sub-shell ([Ne]3s²3p¹) whereas magnesium's is in the 3s sub-shell ([Ne]3s²). The 3p orbital is higher in energy and slightly further from the nucleus than 3s, and the 3s² pair shields the 3p electron. This makes the outer electron in aluminium easier to remove, outweighing the extra proton, so less energy is needed (578 vs 738 kJ mol⁻¹). Mark points: identify sub-shells; state 3p higher in energy/greater distance; state shielding; conclude easier removal. (AQA 7405 3.1.1.6; OCR 3.1.1.1)

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Write the full electron configuration of Fe²⁺ (Z = 26) and explain why the 4s electrons are lost before the 3d electrons.
Answer: Fe: 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁶3d⁶4s², so Fe²⁺: 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁶3d⁶. Once the 3d sub-shell is occupied it lies below 4s in energy, so during ionisation the 4s orbital is emptied first. Mark points: correct Fe configuration; correct ion; explanation of energy order after occupation.
Predict the shapes and bond angles of NH₃, CO₂ and SF₆.
Answer: NH₃: 3 bonding pairs + 1 lone pair on N → trigonal pyramidal, 107° (lone pair repels more strongly than bonding pairs, compressing from 109.5°). CO₂: two double-bond regions on C → linear, 180°. SF₆: 6 bonding pairs on S → octahedral, 90°. (Cross-links to Bonding.)
An element has successive ionisation energies (kJ/mol): 578, 1817, 2745, 11577. Identify the group and explain your reasoning.
Answer: The very large jump is between the 3rd and 4th ionisation energies (2745 → 11577), so the first three electrons are outer-shell and the fourth comes from a new inner shell. Three outer-shell electrons means Group 3 (boron/aluminium family; the values match aluminium).
Chlorine has isotopes ³⁵Cl (75.0%) and ³⁷Cl (25.0%). Calculate the relative atomic mass and predict the m/z values and ratio of the Cl₂⁺ molecular ion peaks.
Answer: A_r = (35 × 75 + 37 × 25)/100 = 35.5. Cl₂⁺ peaks at m/z 70, 72, 74 in the ratio 9:6:1, from the combinations ³⁵Cl–³⁵Cl, ³⁵Cl–³⁷Cl (twice) and ³⁷Cl–³⁷Cl.
In a TOF mass spectrometer, a ⁷⁹Br⁺ ion takes 1.02 × 10⁻⁵ s to drift. Calculate the drift time of an ⁸¹Br⁺ ion with the same kinetic energy.
Answer: t_B/t_A = √(m_B/m_A) = √(81/79) = 1.0126. t_B = 1.0126 × 1.02 × 10⁻⁵ = 1.03 × 10⁻⁵ s. Same charge and KE, so only mass differs.
Explain why the first ionisation energy of sulfur is lower than that of phosphorus.
Answer: Phosphorus is 3s²3p³ with each 3p orbital singly occupied; sulfur is 3s²3p⁴, so one 3p orbital holds a pair. The paired electrons repel each other, so one is easier to remove, lowering the first IE of sulfur despite its higher nuclear charge.
