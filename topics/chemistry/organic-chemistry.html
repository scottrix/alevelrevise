Organic Chemistry: Mechanisms

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC/Eduqas, CCEA

A* revision for organic mechanisms: free-radical substitution, electrophilic addition, SN1/SN2 nucleophilic substitution, elimination and nucleophilic addition, described step by step with curly-arrow conventions for AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC and CCEA.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Name organic compounds and identify functional groups, structural/E-Z/optical isomerism
  • Describe the free-radical substitution mechanism with initiation, propagation and termination steps
  • Describe electrophilic addition mechanisms step by step, applying Markovnikov's rule and carbocation stability
  • Compare SN1 and SN2 mechanisms with curly-arrow conventions, rate laws and stereochemical outcomes
  • Describe the elimination mechanism with ethanolic KOH and the oxidation sequence of alcohols including required practical apparatus
  • Describe nucleophilic addition to carbonyls with HCN and NaBH₄, and aromatic substitution (nitration, Friedel-Crafts) including electrophile generation

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Nucleophile: An electron-pair donor that attacks electron-deficient (δ+) carbon atoms, e.g. OH⁻, CN⁻, NH₃.
Electrophile: An electron-pair acceptor attracted to electron-rich regions such as C=C bonds, e.g. H⁺, NO₂⁺, Br₂.
Homolytic fission: Bond breaking in which each atom takes one electron, forming two radicals.
Heterolytic fission: Bond breaking in which both electrons go to one atom, forming a cation and an anion.
Carbocation: A positively charged carbon ion; stability increases with substitution (3° > 2° > 1°) due to alkyl inductive donation.
SN1 mechanism: Stepwise nucleophilic substitution: C–X bond breaks first (rate-determining) forming a planar carbocation, then nucleophile attacks — racemic product, rate = k[halogenoalkane].
SN2 mechanism: Concerted nucleophilic substitution: nucleophile attacks 180° to the leaving group as C–X breaks — inversion of configuration, rate = k[halogenoalkane][nucleophile].
Markovnikov's rule: In electrophilic addition to an unsymmetrical alkene, H adds to the carbon with more hydrogens, so the more stable carbocation forms.
Elimination (dehydrohalogenation): Loss of HX from a halogenoalkane using hot ethanolic KOH, forming an alkene.
Arenium (Wheland) intermediate: The carbocation formed when an electrophile bonds to benzene, with the positive charge delocalised over five carbons; stability restored by deprotonation.

Nomenclature, Functional Groups and Isomerism

IUPAC naming: find the longest carbon chain containing the highest-priority functional group, number from the end giving the lowest locants, name substituents as prefixes in alphabetical order and the functional group as a suffix. Priority order (high to low): carboxylic acid > ester > acyl chloride > amide > nitrile > aldehyde > ketone > alcohol > amine > alkene > alkane. Examples: CH₃CH₂CH(OH)CH₃ is butan-2-ol; CH₃CH₂COOCH₂CH₃ is ethyl propanoate (acid part as base name, alkyl part as prefix).

Functional groups and their reactions: alkanes (free-radical substitution); alkenes (electrophilic addition); halogenoalkanes (nucleophilic substitution, elimination); alcohols (substitution, elimination, oxidation, esterification); aldehydes/ketones (nucleophilic addition, oxidation of aldehydes); carboxylic acids (esterification, reduction); esters (hydrolysis); amines (bases, nucleophiles); nitriles (reduction, hydrolysis). Skeletal formulae show bonds as lines with vertices as carbons — be fluent converting between displayed, structural and skeletal forms.

Isomerism: structural isomers have the same molecular formula but different atom ordering (chain, position, functional-group isomerism). E/Z (geometric) isomerism requires a C=C bond with restricted rotation and two different groups on each carbon: Z when the highest-priority groups (CIP rules — higher atomic number first) are on the same side, E when opposite. But-2-ene has cis (Z) and trans (E) forms. Optical isomerism: a chiral carbon (four different groups) gives non-superimposable mirror images (enantiomers) rotating plane-polarised light equally in opposite directions; racemic mixtures are 50:50 and optically inactive. SN1 products are racemic (planar carbocation attacked from both sides) — a classic A* link between mechanisms and stereochemistry.

Free-Radical Substitution and Electrophilic Addition

Free-radical substitution (alkanes + halogens, UV light): three stages. Initiation: UV supplies energy for homolytic fission of the Cl–Cl bond — each atom takes one electron, forming two radicals: Cl₂ → 2Cl•. Propagation: Cl• + CH₄ → CH₃• + HCl (radical abstracts H, new radical forms), then CH₃• + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + Cl• (radical regenerated — chain reaction). Termination: any two radicals combine: Cl• + Cl• → Cl₂; CH₃• + Cl• → CH₃Cl; CH₃• + CH₃• → C₂H₆. Curly-arrow convention: radicals use single-headed 'fish-hook' arrows showing one-electron movement; a dot represents the unpaired electron. Limitations to quote: further substitution (poly-substitution giving CH₂Cl₂ etc.) and mixture of products at termination — limiting CH₄ excess favours monosubstitution. Ozone depletion mechanism (CFCl₃ → Cl• + CFCl₂•; Cl• + O₃ → ClO• + O₂; ClO• + O → Cl• + O₂) uses the same radical chain logic.

Electrophilic addition (alkenes): the C=C π bond is electron-rich and attracts electrophiles. Mechanism for propene + HBr, step by step: (1) the π electron pair forms a bond to the H of H–Br; simultaneously (or immediately after) the H–Br bond breaks heterolytically — both electrons go to Br. Curly arrows: from the π bond to the H, and from the H–Br bond to Br. (2) A carbocation forms on one alkene carbon; Markovnikov's rule: H adds to the carbon already carrying more hydrogens, so the positive charge sits on the more substituted carbon (secondary/tertiary carbocations are stabilised by electron-donating alkyl groups via the inductive effect). (3) The Br⁻ lone pair donates to the positive carbon, forming the C–Br bond: product 2-bromopropane (major). Minor product 1-bromopropane arises via the primary carbocation.

With Br₂ no polar solvent needed — Br₂ is polarised as it approaches the electron-rich π bond. Exam tip: always draw the intermediate carbocation with the positive charge, and justify the major product by carbocation stability. Hydration of alkenes (H₃PO₄ catalyst, steam) follows the same electrophilic-addition mechanism to make alcohols industrially.

Nucleophilic Substitution: SN1 and SN2 in Detail

Nucleophiles are electron-pair donors (OH⁻, CN⁻, NH₃, H₂O) attacking electron-deficient δ+ carbon bonded to a halogen (C–X is polar: X more electronegative). SN2 — substitution nucleophilic bimolecular — is concerted, one step: (1) the nucleophile's lone pair attacks the δ+ carbon from the side opposite (180° to) the leaving group; (2) simultaneously the C–X bond breaks heterolytically, both electrons to X. Curly arrows: from nucleophile lone pair to carbon, and from C–X bond to X. A transition state shows partial bonds C···Nu and C···X with a dashed wedge — the 'umbrella turning inside out'. Rate = k[halogenoalkane][nucleophile]; favoured by primary halogenoalkanes (little steric hindrance, no stable carbocation) and polar aprotic solvents. Inversion of configuration occurs at a chiral centre.

SN1 — substitution nucleophilic unimolecular — is stepwise: (1) the C–X bond breaks first, heterolytically, forming a planar carbocation intermediate — rate-determining step; (2) the nucleophile attacks the planar carbocation from either side equally, giving a racemic mixture if the carbon was chiral. Rate = k[halogenoalkane] only. Favoured by tertiary halogenoalkanes (carbocation stabilised by three alkyl groups' inductive effect; steric hindrance blocks backside attack). Standard exam comparison: 2-bromo-2-methylpropane reacts by SN1, 1-bromobutane by SN2 — justify by carbocation stability AND steric hindrance for full marks.

Conditions: hydrolysis with warm aqueous NaOH (or water, slower) gives alcohols; with KCN in ethanol gives nitriles (extends chain by one C); with excess ethanolic NH₃ gives amines (excess NH₃ minimises further substitution). Bond enthalpy trend: C–I weakest, hydrolyses fastest — rate measured by AgNO₃/ethanol (AgI precipitate fastest, AgCl slowest), a required OCR/AQA rate comparison. Nucleophilicity order in polar protic solvents: I⁻ > Br⁻ > Cl⁻ (weaker bond, more polarisable).

Elimination, Alcohol Oxidation and Required Practical

Elimination (dehydrohalogenation): halogenoalkane + hot, concentrated ethanolic KOH (reflux) → alkene + KBr + H₂O. Step-by-step mechanism (E2): (1) the hydroxide lone pair abstracts a β-hydrogen (on the carbon adjacent to the C–X carbon); (2) the C–H bond electron pair forms a C=C π bond between the α and β carbons; (3) simultaneously the C–X bond breaks heterolytically to X⁻. Curly arrows: OH⁻ lone pair to the β-H, C–H bond to the C–C gap (forming π), C–X bond to X. Aqueous KOH gives substitution (alcohol); ethanolic KOH gives elimination — condition determines mechanism, standard comparison question. With asymmetric halogenoalkanes, the major alkene has more alkyl groups (more stable — Zaitsev-type preference).

Oxidation of alcohols with acidified K₂Cr₂O₇ (orange → green, Cr³⁺): primary alcohols → aldehydes (distil immediately, to prevent further oxidation) → carboxylic acids (reflux with excess oxidant); secondary → ketones; tertiary resist oxidation (no C–H on the carbinol carbon, so no hydrogen to remove). Oxidation mechanism concept: loss of two H (one from O–H, one from C–H) forming the new C=O. Distinguishing tests: aldehydes reduce Tollens' reagent ([Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺) giving a silver mirror and Fehling's gives brick-red Cu₂O; ketones give no reaction. 2,4-DNP (Brady's reagent) gives an orange precipitate with any C=O, confirming carbonyl presence but not distinguishing aldehyde from ketone. Distinguishing the alcohol classes: oxidise then test, or use iodoform (CH₃CHOH– and CH₃CO– give yellow CHI₃ precipitate with I₂/NaOH).

Required practical (AQA RP5 / OCR PAG5): distillation of a primary alcohol oxidation product. Apparatus: round-bottom flask, still head with thermometer, condenser, receiver. Thermometer bulb level with the side-arm so it reads the vapour temperature; collect the fraction boiling at the aldehyde's boiling point. Sealing: anti-bumping granules prevent violent boiling. Then test the distillate with Tollens'/Fehling's to show incomplete oxidation produced aldehyde, or reflux further to convert to the acid. Preparation of purified organic solids (recrystallisation) is covered under Titrations and Analytical Techniques.

Carbonyl Chemistry and Aromatic Mechanisms

Nucleophilic addition to carbonyls: the C=O is polarised (O more electronegative → C δ+), so nucleophiles attack the carbon. Mechanism for carbonyl + HCN (NaCN/H₂SO₄ catalyst), step by step: (1) the CN⁻ lone pair attacks the δ+ carbonyl carbon; the π bond pair moves onto the oxygen — curly arrow from CN⁻ lone pair to C, from C=O π bond to O — forming an alkoxide intermediate with O⁻; (2) the alkoxide oxygen abstracts a proton from HCN: arrow from O⁻ lone pair to H, from H–CN bond to CN⁻ (regenerating CN⁻ — catalyst). Product: hydroxynitrile (extends chain by one carbon, creates a chiral centre when the two groups on C differ — racemic mixture because planar C=O is attacked from both sides). Ketones react more slowly than aldehydes: two alkyl groups donate electron density, reducing the δ+ character, and sterically hinder attack.

Reduction: NaBH₄ provides H⁻ (hydride) — same nucleophilic-addition mechanism: aldehydes → primary alcohols, ketones → secondary alcohols. Carboxylic acids need LiAlH₄ (stronger). Esterification: carboxylic acid + alcohol, conc. H₂SO₄ catalyst, reflux → ester + water; mechanism involves protonating the C=O, alcohol addition, then loss of water. Ester hydrolysis: hot aqueous acid (reversible, gives acid + alcohol) or hot aqueous alkali — saponification, irreversible, gives carboxylate salt + alcohol (basis of soap-making from fats).

Aromatic chemistry: benzene's delocalised π-system (6 electrons over 6 carbons) makes it stable — evidence against Kekulé: hydrogenation enthalpy −208 kJ/mol vs expected −360 kJ/mol for cyclohexa-1,3,5-triene; equal C–C bond lengths (0.139 nm, between single and double); reluctance to undergo addition. Nitration mechanism, step by step: (1) electrophile generation: HNO₃ + 2H₂SO₄ ⇌ NO₂⁺ + 2HSO₄⁻ + H₃O⁺ (conc. acids, 50–60 °C); (2) attack: the π-electron pair of one C=C in benzene bonds to NO₂⁺, forming the arenium (Wheland) intermediate — a carbocation with the positive charge delocalised over five carbons (horseshoe); (3) deprotonation: HSO₄⁻ removes the H⁺ from the sp³ carbon, the C–H bond electrons restore the delocalised ring — nitrobenzene + H₂SO₄ (catalyst regenerated). Higher temperatures promote dinitration; −NO₂ deactivates the ring (electron-withdrawing), so further substitution needs harsher conditions.

Friedel-Crafts acylation: RCOCl + AlCl₃ → RCO⁺ (acylium ion) + AlCl₄⁻; the acylium ion attacks the ring as in nitration; AlCl₃ is regenerated on deprotonation (catalyst). Products: aryl ketones (e.g. benzene + CH₃COCl → phenylethanone). Acylation is used in synthesis because it prevents multiple acylation (the ketone deactivates the ring) and fixes chain length. Directing effects: electron-donating groups (−OH, −NH₂, alkyl) activate the ring and direct 2,4-substitution; electron-withdrawing (−NO₂, −COR) deactivate and direct 3-substitution. Phenol is more reactive than benzene (O lone pair delocalised into the ring, increasing electron density) and reacts with bromine water without a catalyst (white precipitate of 2,4,6-tribromophenol).

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: Propose a mechanism for the reaction of 2-methylpropene with HBr and explain the major product.
Mark-scheme logic: Electrophilic addition. Step 1: the π electron pair forms a bond to the H of H–Br while the H–Br bond breaks heterolytically (both electrons to Br) — curly arrow from the π bond to H and from the H–Br bond to Br. Step 2: the carbocation forms on the more substituted carbon (Markovnikov) — a tertiary carbocation (CH₃)₃C⁺ stabilised by three electron-donating alkyl groups — rather than a primary carbocation. Step 3: the Br⁻ lone pair attacks the positive carbon forming the C–Br bond. Product: 2-bromo-2-methylpropane (major). Mark points: correct heterolytic fission; carbocation on tertiary carbon; justification by carbocation stability; curly-arrow description. (AQA 3.3.3.1-3.3.3.2)

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Write the initiation, propagation and termination steps for the reaction of methane with chlorine in UV light.
Answer: Initiation: Cl₂ → 2Cl• (homolytic fission by UV). Propagation: Cl• + CH₄ → CH₃• + HCl; CH₃• + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + Cl•. Termination (any two radicals combine): Cl• + Cl• → Cl₂; CH₃• + Cl• → CH₃Cl; CH₃• + CH₃• → C₂H₆.
Explain the difference between SN1 and SN2, and why 2-bromo-2-methylpropane reacts by SN1 but 1-bromobutane by SN2.
Answer: SN1 stepwise: C–Br breaks first to a carbocation intermediate (rate depends only on [halogenoalkane]); SN2 concerted: nucleophile attacks 180° to the leaving group as C–Br breaks (rate depends on both concentrations). 2-bromo-2-methylpropane is tertiary: carbocation stabilised by three alkyl groups and steric hindrance blocks backside attack → SN1. 1-bromobutane is primary: backside attack easy, primary carbocation unstable → SN2.
Describe chemical tests to distinguish propanal from propanone.
Answer: Warm each with Tollens' reagent ([Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺): propanal is oxidised to propanoic acid giving a silver mirror; propanone no reaction. Fehling's: brick-red Cu₂O precipitate with the aldehyde only. (2,4-DNP gives orange precipitate with both — confirms C=O but does not distinguish.)
Describe the SN2 hydrolysis of bromoethane with aqueous NaOH, using curly-arrow conventions in words.
Answer: One-step concerted mechanism: the OH⁻ lone pair attacks the δ+ carbon from the side opposite the C–Br bond (180° backside attack) while the C–Br bond breaks heterolytically — both electrons to Br⁻. Curly arrows: OH⁻ lone pair → carbon; C–Br bond → Br. Products: ethanol + Br⁻. A transition state with partial C–O and C–Br bonds is drawn; configuration inverts at a chiral centre.
Outline the mechanism for the nitration of benzene to nitrobenzene.
Answer: 1. Electrophile generation: HNO₃ + 2H₂SO₄ → NO₂⁺ + 2HSO₄⁻ + H₃O⁺ (conc. acids, 50-60 °C). 2. Attack: benzene π-electrons bond to NO₂⁺ forming the arenium carbocation (horseshoe, delocalised over 5 carbons). 3. Deprotonation: HSO₄⁻ removes H⁺ from the sp³ carbon, restoring the aromatic ring: nitrobenzene + H₂SO₄ (catalyst regenerated).
Why do ketones react more slowly with nucleophiles than aldehydes?
Answer: Two alkyl groups on the carbonyl carbon donate electron density by the inductive effect, reducing the δ+ character of the carbon (weaker attraction for the nucleophile), and the extra alkyl group sterically hinders attack. Aldehydes have only one (or no) alkyl group, so the carbon is more positive and more accessible.
