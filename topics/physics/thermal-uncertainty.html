Thermal Physics and Uncertainty

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC/Eduqas, CCEA

A-Level Physics revision: internal energy, specific heat capacity and latent heat, the first law of thermodynamics, gas laws, ideal gas equation, kinetic theory derivation and uncertainty/percentage error calculations - AQA 7408 3.1/3.6.2, OCR A H556 5.1, Edexcel 9PH0 Topic 9.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Distinguish internal energy, temperature and heat; use Q = mcΔθ and Q = mL (AO1/AO2) - AQA 7408 3.6.2.1, OCR A H556 5.1
  • Apply the first law ΔU = Q + W with correct sign conventions and interpret p-V diagrams (AO2) - AQA 7408 3.6.2.1
  • Use the gas laws and ideal gas equation pV = nRT = NkT; convert temperatures to kelvin (AO2) - AQA 7408 3.6.2.2
  • Derive pV = ⅓Nm(c_rms)^2 from kinetic theory and use c_rms = √(3kT/m) (AO2/AO3) - AQA 7408 3.6.2.3
  • Calculate combined uncertainty, percentage error and analyse practical measurements (AO3) - AQA 7408 3.1.2, Edexcel 9PH0 Topic 1, OCR Module 1

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Internal energy (U): The sum of the random kinetic and potential energies of all the molecules in a system.
Temperature: A measure of the mean random kinetic energy per molecule; proportional to it (in kelvin).
Specific heat capacity (c): The energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 K: Q = mcΔθ, in J kg^-1 K^-1.
Specific latent heat (L): The energy needed to change the state of 1 kg of a substance with no temperature change: Q = mL, in J/kg (fusion and vaporisation).
First law of thermodynamics: ΔU = Q + W - the increase in internal energy equals energy supplied by heating plus work done ON the system; energy conservation.
Absolute zero: 0 K (-273 °C): the temperature at which molecules have minimum kinetic energy and the pressure of an ideal gas falls to zero.
Root mean square speed (c_rms): The square root of the mean of the squares of molecular speeds: c_rms = √(3kT/m) = √(3RT/M).
Boltzmann constant (k): The gas constant per molecule: k = R/N_A = 1.38 × 10^-23 J/K.
Random uncertainty: Uncertainty that varies unpredictably between repeats - reduced by repeating and averaging; shown by scatter in results.
Systematic uncertainty: A constant offset in every reading (zero error, calibration error, parallax) - repeats do NOT reduce it; found by comparison or calibration.

📌 Key Points

Internal energy U = Σ(KE + PE) of molecules; temperature ∝ mean KE per molecule.
Q = mcΔθ (no phase change); Q = mL (phase change); L_fusion, L_vaporisation.
First law: ΔU = Q + W; W positive when done ON the gas; Q positive into the system.
Gas laws: Boyle p ∝ 1/V (isothermal), Charles V ∝ T (isobaric), pressure law p ∝ T (isochoric); ideal gas pV = nRT = NkT.
Kinetic theory: pV = ⅓Nm(c_rms)^2, so c_rms = √(3RT/M) = √(3kT/m).
Uncertainty: single reading ± half resolution; repeats: half the range. Add/subtract: add absolute uncertainties. Multiply/divide: add percentage uncertainties. Power n: multiply percentage uncertainty by n.
Percentage error = |measured - accepted|/accepted × 100%.

Internal energy, specific heat capacity and latent heat

Internal energy is the sum of the random kinetic and potential energies of all the molecules (AQA 7408 3.6.2.1; OCR A H556 5.1). Temperature measures the mean random KE per molecule - raising temperature raises internal energy and changes state. Distinguish the three: temperature is the measure, heat (Q) is energy transferred because of a temperature difference, internal energy is the store - the standard definition-matching question. Increasing the temperature of a body increases its internal energy (more molecular KE); a state change at constant temperature changes internal energy through the PE term (bonds/stretching between molecules).

Specific heat capacity: Q = mcΔθ - the energy to change the temperature of mass m by Δθ; c in J kg^-1 K^-1 (water: 4180 J kg^-1 K^-1 - large, so water is a good coolant). Note Δθ is the same in K and °C because the intervals are equal.

Specific latent heat: Q = mL - energy to change state of mass m with no temperature change (L_fusion for melting, L_vaporisation for boiling; water: L_f = 3.34 × 10^5 J/kg, L_v = 2.26 × 10^6 J/kg - vaporisation is much larger because separating molecules completely needs more energy than loosening them). Cooling curves show plateaus at state changes - the temperature stays constant while the substance changes state because the energy input goes into the latent heat, not into KE.

Worked example: heating and melting

Find the total energy to melt 0.50 kg of ice at 0 °C and heat the water to 25 °C (L_f = 3.34 × 10^5 J/kg, c = 4180 J kg^-1 K^-1).

Line 1: Melting (no temperature change): Q_1 = mL_f = 0.50 × 3.34 × 10^5 = 1.67 × 10^5 J (M1 A1).

Line 2: Heating: Q_2 = mcΔθ = 0.50 × 4180 × 25 = 5.2 × 10^4 J (M1 A1).

Line 3: Total = 1.67 × 10^5 + 0.52 × 10^5 = 2.19 × 10^5 J ≈ 219 kJ (A1: stages added separately).

Practical (AQA RP11): determine specific heat capacity with an electrical heater: energy supplied E = IVt, temperature rise Δθ measured; c = IVt/(mΔθ). Insulate the block (reduce heat loss - the main systematic error making c too high), use a lid, stir liquids, and extrapolate the cooling curve back to correct for the heating delay.

First law of thermodynamics and p-V diagrams

First law: ΔU = Q + W - the increase in internal energy equals energy supplied by heating (Q, positive into the system) plus work done ON the system (W, positive when the gas is compressed) (AQA 7408 3.6.2.1). Sign conventions are the examinable trap: W = -pΔV for a gas at constant pressure (compression: ΔV negative, W positive - work done on the gas). Some boards write ΔU = Q - W with W the work done BY the gas - read the question's convention.

Work done by/on a gas: W = ∫p dV - the area under the p-V curve. Expansion does work by the gas on the surroundings (gas loses internal energy if no heat supplied); compression does work on the gas (internal energy rises).

p-V diagrams (described in words): isothermal (constant T) - a curve falling as p ∝ 1/V (Boyle); ΔU = 0 for an ideal gas, so Q = -W: all heat supplied is converted to work. Isobaric (constant p) - a horizontal line; W = pΔV. Isochoric/isovolumetric (constant V) - a vertical line; no work done, so ΔU = Q. Adiabatic (no heat exchange, Q = 0) - a steeper curve than isothermal; ΔU = W: the gas cools when it expands (work done at the expense of internal energy). The area inside a closed cycle on a p-V diagram is the net work done per cycle - the basis of engine analysis.

Worked example: first law bookkeeping

A gas absorbs 500 J of heat and does 200 J of work by expanding. Find ΔU, and state what happens if instead the gas is compressed by 200 J with the same heat input.

Line 1: ΔU = Q + W with W = -200 J (work done BY the gas): ΔU = 500 - 200 = 300 J (M1 A1: sign conventions).

Line 2: Compressed: W = +200 J: ΔU = 500 + 200 = 700 J (M1 A1).

Line 3: Compression adds to internal energy - the gas heats more (A1).

Gas laws and the ideal gas equation

The empirical gas laws (AQA 7408 3.6.2.2; Edexcel 9PH0 Topic 9; OCR A H556 5.1), all for a fixed mass of ideal gas, all with temperature in kelvin (T = °C + 273): Boyle's law p ∝ 1/V at constant T (isothermal - pV = constant); Charles's law V ∝ T at constant p (isobaric); pressure law p ∝ T at constant V (isochoric). Combining: pV/T = constant, and for n moles the ideal gas equation pV = nRT (R = 8.31 J mol^-1 K^-1), or pV = NkT for N molecules (k = R/N_A = 1.38 × 10^-23 J/K).

Absolute zero: extrapolating any gas law graph to V = 0 or p = 0 gives T = -273 °C - the same intercept for every gas, defining the kelvin scale (molecules have minimum KE; pressure falls to zero when molecular motion stops).

Practicals: (AQA RP10; Edexcel and OCR gas-law practicals) - Boyle's law: trapped air column in a sealed tube with oil, pumped to vary pressure; plot p against 1/V - straight line through the origin confirms the law. Charles's law: capillary tube trapped air over a bead of concentrated sulfuric acid in water baths; plot V against T(°C) - extrapolate to V = 0 for absolute zero. Pressure law: fixed-volume flask in water baths; plot p against T(°C) - same extrapolation. Errors: temperature lag between bath and gas (wait for equilibrium, stir), volume/pressure reading resolution, and the assumption that the gas is dry and ideal at room temperatures.

Worked example: ideal gas equation

One mole of an ideal gas occupies 0.022 m^3 at 27 °C. Find its pressure.

Line 1: T = 27 + 273 = 300 K (M1: kelvin conversion - the classic mark loss if missed).

Line 2: pV = nRT, so p = nRT/V (M1).

Line 3: p = (1 × 8.31 × 300)/0.022 = 2493/0.022 = 1.13 × 10^5 Pa (A1) - about atmospheric pressure, as expected for molar volume at room temperature.

Kinetic theory: deriving pV = ⅓Nm(c_rms)^2

The kinetic theory model derives the gas equation from molecular collisions (AQA 7408 3.6.2.3) - the full derivation is a standard six-mark question, so learn each step and the assumptions.

Assumptions: molecules are identical point particles; they move in random directions with a distribution of speeds; collisions (with each other and the walls) are perfectly elastic; the time of a collision is negligible compared with the time between collisions; the gas is dilute so intermolecular forces act only during collisions; Newtonian mechanics applies.

Derivation, step by step: (1) Consider one molecule of mass m moving with velocity component u_x in a box of length l, width/height giving face area A. (2) It strikes the end wall elastically, reversing u_x: change in momentum per collision Δp = 2mu_x. (3) The time between collisions with the same wall is the round trip: Δt = 2l/u_x. (4) Force on the wall from this molecule: F = Δp/Δt = 2mu_x/(2l/u_x) = mu_x^2/l. (5) Sum over all N molecules: F = m(u_1x^2 + u_2x^2 + ...)/l = Nm/l, where is the mean square x-component. (6) Random directions mean = ⅓, where is the mean square speed (three perpendicular directions share it equally). (7) Pressure = force/area: p = F/A = Nm/(3lA) = Nm/(3V), since V = lA. So pV = ⅓Nm.

Linking to temperature: compare with pV = NkT: ⅓Nm = NkT gives ½m = (3/2)kT - the mean kinetic energy per molecule is (3/2)kT, proportional to absolute temperature. Hence the root mean square speed c_rms = √(3kT/m) = √(3RT/M) (M = molar mass in kg/mol - watch kg, not g). Equipartition: each quadratic degree of freedom holds (1/2)kT per molecule - monatomic gases have 3 translational degrees (mean KE (3/2)kT), diatomic add 2 rotational at room temperature (5 degrees, (5/2)kT) - explaining why diatomic molar heat capacities are larger.

Worked example: rms speed

Find the rms speed of nitrogen molecules (M = 0.028 kg/mol) at 300 K.

Line 1: c_rms = √(3RT/M) (M1: equation with molar mass in kg/mol).

Line 2: c_rms = √(3 × 8.31 × 300/0.028) = √(267,107) (M1 A1).

Line 3: c_rms = 517 m/s (A1) - comparable to the speed of sound in the gas (sound travels through molecular motion).

Uncertainty calculations and percentage error

Measurement uncertainty (AQA 7408 3.1.2; Edexcel 9PH0 Topic 1 'Working as a physicist'; OCR Module 1): random uncertainty varies unpredictably between repeats - reduce by repeating and averaging (or a best-fit line); shown by scatter. Systematic uncertainty is a constant offset in every reading (zero error, calibration error, parallax, heat loss in thermal experiments) - repeats do not reduce it; find it by calibration or comparison with a known value. The uncertainty of a single reading is at least ± half the resolution; for repeated readings use ± half the range.

Combining uncertainties: adding or subtracting measured quantities - add the absolute uncertainties (Q = mcΔθ: absolute uncertainties in m and Δθ add; c is a constant). Multiplying or dividing - add the percentage uncertainties (E = IVt: three percentage uncertainties add). Raising to a power n - multiply the percentage uncertainty by n (V = πr^2l: the r^2 term doubles the radius's percentage uncertainty). Constant multiples - percentage uncertainty unchanged (½m: factor ½ contributes nothing).

Percentage error against an accepted value: |measured - accepted|/accepted × 100% - used to conclude evaluations. Gradients and intercepts: use best-fit lines (steepest and shallowest lines through the error bars give the gradient's uncertainty); a large y-intercept where zero is expected signals a systematic error.

Worked example: specific heat capacity with uncertainty

An aluminium block: E = IVt = 6.0 V × 2.0 A × 120 s with V ± 0.1 V, I ± 0.05 A, t ± 1 s; m = 1.000 ± 0.001 kg; Δθ = 15.0 ± 0.5 K.

Line 1: c = IVt/(mΔθ) = (6.0 × 2.0 × 120)/(1.000 × 15.0) = 96 J kg^-1 K^-1 (M1 A1).

Line 2: Percentage uncertainties: V: 0.1/6.0 = 1.7%; I: 0.05/2.0 = 2.5%; t: 1/120 = 0.8%; m: 0.1%; Δθ: 0.5/15 = 3.3% (M1: each computed).

Line 3: Total (all multiplied/divided) = 1.7 + 2.5 + 0.8 + 0.1 + 3.3 = 8.4%, so Δc = 0.084 × 96 = 8 J kg^-1 K^-1 (A1).

Line 4: c = 96 ± 8 J kg^-1 K^-1; accepted value ≈ 900 J kg^-1 K^-1 - the measured value is far too low: the dominant error is heat loss (systematic - insulation, lid, shorter heating), not random scatter (A1: evaluation - systematic vs random is the discriminator).

Exam evaluation logic for thermal practicals: identify the largest uncertainty (percentage terms), classify it random or systematic, and propose the fix (repeats for random; insulation/calibration/zeroing for systematic). The mixture method (hot metal into known water, energy conservation: m_metal c_metal Δθ_metal = m_water c_water Δθ_water) and the continuous-flow method are the alternative routes - know which errors each minimises.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: A student determines the specific heat capacity of an aluminium block electrically: heater 6.0 V, 2.0 A for 120 s, block mass 1.000 kg, temperature rise 15.0 K. The accepted value is 900 J kg^-1 K^-1. Calculate the experimental c, the percentage error, and explain the main source of error and how to reduce it. (AQA 7408-style, 6 marks)
Mark-scheme logic: Energy supplied: E = IVt = 6.0 × 2.0 × 120 = 1440 J (M1 A1: electrical energy). c = E/(mΔθ) = 1440/(1.000 × 15.0) (M1: definition rearranged) = 96 J kg^-1 K^-1 (A1). Percentage error = |96 - 900|/900 × 100% = 89% (A1: percentage error). The experimental value is far too low because much of the electrical energy is lost to the surroundings (heat loss from the block) rather than heating the aluminium - a systematic error (A1: correct identification). Reduce it: insulate the block (lagging and lid), heat for a shorter time with a higher power, and correct using a cooling-curve extrapolation (A1: valid remedies). Repeating and averaging would not help, because systematic errors are not reduced by repeats.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Calculate the energy needed to raise the temperature of 500 g of water from 20 °C to 80 °C. (c = 4180 J kg^-1 K^-1)
Answer: Q = mcΔθ = 0.500 × 4180 × (80 - 20) (M1: mass in kg) = 0.500 × 4180 × 60 = 1.25 × 10^5 J = 125 kJ (A1).
One mole of an ideal gas occupies 0.022 m^3 at 27 °C. Calculate its pressure. (R = 8.31 J mol^-1 K^-1)
Answer: T = 27 + 273 = 300 K (M1: kelvin conversion). pV = nRT gives p = nRT/V (M1) = (1 × 8.31 × 300)/0.022 = 1.13 × 10^5 Pa (A1) - about atmospheric.
Calculate the rms speed of nitrogen molecules (M = 0.028 kg/mol) at 300 K, and the mean kinetic energy per molecule. (k = 1.38 × 10^-23 J/K)
Answer: c_rms = √(3RT/M) = √(3 × 8.31 × 300/0.028) (M1) = 517 m/s (A1). Mean KE per molecule = (3/2)kT = 1.5 × 1.38 × 10^-23 × 300 (M1) = 6.2 × 10^-21 J (A1).
Calculate the total energy to melt 0.50 kg of ice at 0 °C and heat the water to 25 °C. (L_f = 3.34 × 10^5 J/kg, c = 4180 J kg^-1 K^-1)
Answer: Melting: Q_1 = mL_f = 0.50 × 3.34 × 10^5 = 1.67 × 10^5 J (M1 A1: no temperature change during melting). Heating: Q_2 = mcΔθ = 0.50 × 4180 × 25 = 5.2 × 10^4 J (M1 A1). Total = 2.19 × 10^5 J ≈ 219 kJ (A1: stages added).
A gas absorbs 500 J of heat while being compressed so that 200 J of work is done ON it. Calculate ΔU and state the sign convention.
Answer: First law ΔU = Q + W with Q = +500 J (into the system) and W = +200 J (work done on the gas) (M1: signs correct). ΔU = 700 J (A1). Convention: Q positive into the system, W positive when done ON the gas; if the question defines W as work done BY the gas, ΔU = Q - W = 300 J (A1: convention stated).
An aluminium block experiment: E = IVt with V = 6.0 ± 0.1 V, I = 2.0 ± 0.05 A, t = 120 ± 1 s, m = 1.000 ± 0.001 kg, Δθ = 15.0 ± 0.5 K. Calculate c and its percentage uncertainty.
Answer: c = IVt/(mΔθ) = 1440/15.0 = 96 J kg^-1 K^-1 (M1 A1). Percentage uncertainties: V 1.7%, I 2.5%, t 0.8%, m 0.1%, Δθ 3.3% (M1: each computed). Total = 8.4% (A1), so c = 96 ± 8 J kg^-1 K^-1; the Δθ and I terms dominate - stir and use a higher-resolution thermometer/ammeter.
