Kantian Ethics

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA, OCR, WJEC/Eduqas, CCEA

Kant's good will, categorical imperative (universalisability, humanity as an end), summum bonum and postulates, with W.D. Ross' response - strengths, weaknesses and A* essay technique for AQA 7062 and OCR H573.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain Kant's good will, duty and the distinction between hypothetical and categorical imperatives (AO1)
  • State the first formulation of the categorical imperative (universalisability) and apply it to a case (AO1)
  • State the second formulation (humanity as an end in itself) and apply it to euthanasia and business cases (AO1)
  • Explain the third formulation (kingdom of ends), the summum bonum and Kant's postulates of freedom, God and immortality (AO1)
  • Evaluate strengths (dignity, universalisability) and weaknesses (conflicts of duty, cold formalism, lying to the murderer) (AO2)
  • Explain W.D. Ross' prima facie duties as a response to Kant's rigidity and weigh it in a verdict (AO2)
  • Write an evaluative essay with a sustained argument and justified verdict (AO2/AO3)

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

good will: Kant: the only thing good without qualification - a will that acts from duty out of respect for the moral law, not from inclination or consequence.
duty: The necessity of an action done from respect for the law; an action has moral worth only when done from duty, not merely in accordance with it.
hypothetical imperative: A conditional command: if you want X, do Y (instrumental reason); Kant - such commands bind only given a goal, so they are not moral law.
categorical imperative: Kant's unconditional moral command, binding on all rational agents: act only on maxims you can will as universal law.
universalisability: First formulation: act only according to a maxim you could rationally will everyone to follow; a maxim that cannot be universalised (e.g. lying) is wrong.
humanity as an end in itself: Second formulation: treat humanity, in yourself and others, always as an end and never merely as a means; persons have dignity, not price.
kingdom of ends: Third formulation: act as a law-making member of a community of rational agents who legislate universal laws for all.
summum bonum: The highest good: virtue rewarded with happiness; Kant argues morality demands it, which grounds his postulates of God and immortality.
postulates of practical reason: Kant: freedom, God and immortality must be assumed for morality to be coherent - freedom for duty, God/immortality for the summum bonum.
prima facie duties: W.D. Ross: conditional duties (fidelity, reparation, gratitude, justice, beneficence, self-improvement) that can conflict; judgement decides which binds in a case.

Good will, duty and the categorical imperative

Kant (Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals, 1785): the good will is the only thing good without qualification - 'it is impossible to conceive anything at all in the world... which can be taken as good without qualification, except a good will'. Talents, wealth and happiness can be used for evil; a good will cannot. Moral worth comes from acting from duty, not merely in accordance with it: the shopkeeper who is honest only for reputation acts in accordance with duty but not from it, so has no moral worth. Consequences are irrelevant to moral worth - outcomes are beyond our control; only the will is within it.

Hypothetical vs categorical imperatives: a hypothetical imperative is conditional - 'if you want to pass, revise' - it binds only given a goal, and is merely instrumental. The moral law must be categorical: unconditional, binding on all rational agents regardless of desire. Kant offers three formulations, which he holds are the same law viewed from different angles.

First formulation (universalisability): 'act only according to that maxim through which you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law.' Test for a maxim (e.g. 'I will make a false promise to get money'): (1) formulate the maxim; (2) universalise it; (3) check for contradiction. If everyone made false promises when convenient, promise-keeping itself would collapse - the maxim contradicts itself, so lying is wrong. Kant also uses perfect duties (never permissible: lying, suicide) vs imperfect duties (sometimes permissible: helping others, developing talents).

Autonomy and the postulate of freedom: duty is possible only if the will is free; Kant's determinism problem - if actions are determined by natural causes, morality collapses; the postulate of freedom is a practical necessity, not a theoretical proof. O'Neill's refinement (Constructions of Reason, 1989): universalisability requires two tests - consistency in conception (the universalised maxim must be thinkable) and consistency in the will (it must be willed by all rational agents); this answers the 'absurd maxim' objection by tightening the test beyond mere contradiction.

Humanity as an end, kingdom of ends, summum bonum and postulates

Second formulation (humanity as an end in itself): 'act in such a way that you treat humanity, whether in your own person or in the person of any other, never merely as a means to an end but always at the same time as an end.' Persons have dignity (beyond price), not market value; objects have price. Application: lying to a person uses their rationality as a mere instrument - it is wrong for that reason as well as failing universalisability; business exploitation (misleading advertising, child labour) treats workers and consumers merely as means; voluntary euthanasia is complex - the patient may will their own end, but Kant still holds suicide is a perfect duty violation because it uses the person as a means to escape suffering.

Third formulation (kingdom of ends): act as a law-making member of an ideal community of rational agents legislating universal laws for all - each member is both sovereign and subject. This unifies autonomy and law: moral agents give the law to themselves, so duty is not external coercion.

Summum bonum and postulates: morality demands that virtue be rewarded with happiness - the summum bonum. Since we cannot guarantee this in this life, practical reason must postulate: freedom (for duty to be possible the will must be free), immortality (endless progress toward complete virtue), and God (who harmonises virtue and happiness). These are not proofs of God's existence but conditions of moral coherence - 'I have found it necessary to deny knowledge in order to make room for faith.' Kant uses this to reject the moral argument as demonstration while retaining it as practical necessity.

Strengths of duty: reason is universal - the moral law binds all rational agents regardless of culture or emotion; autonomy: each agent legislates the law for themselves, so morality is not imposed; dignity: persons are ends, which grounds human rights.

Applying the second formulation further: sexual ethics - prostitution and deception use a person merely as a means; consenting adults who respect each other treat each other as ends (Kant's framework has limited but real application to sexual ethics); business ethics - CSR grounded in dignity: workers are never merely labour costs; the murderer at the door revisited: Kant's own essay (On a Supposed Right to Lie from Philanthropy, 1797) insists on truth-telling even here - 'to be truthful in all declarations is... a sacred and unconditionally commanding law of reason' - the standard AO2 exhibit for the rigidity objection.

Strengths, weaknesses and Ross' response

Strengths: (1) universalisability matches moral conviction - fairness and consistency are core to ethics; (2) dignity of persons grounds modern human rights and business ethics (workers as ends, not instruments); (3) motive matters - rewards and consequences cannot make an act moral, which fits moral intuition; (4) autonomy and reason make the law universal, not sectarian; (5) the postulates give a rational account of why morality seems to demand a moral universe.

Weaknesses: (1) conflicts of duty - Kant holds perfect duties admit no exceptions, but cases exist where duties conflict (lying to the murderer at the door: truth-telling vs protecting life); Kant's own reply (tell the truth even then) is widely judged indefensible - if the murderer uses your truthful answer, you are implicated; (2) cold formalism - the theory ignores consequences and emotion; a rigid Kantian outcome (returning a weapon to a madman) is monstrous (Hegel: the theory is 'empty formalism'); (3) universalisability may be too permissive - absurd maxims ('everyone wear odd socks') universalise without contradiction, so the test needs extra conditions; (4) consequentialists (Bentham, Mill) argue ignoring outcomes is irrational - morality exists to make lives go better; (5) the summum bonum argument relies on dubious claims about what morality 'demands'.

W.D. Ross (The Right and the Good, 1930): prima facie duties - conditional duties (fidelity, reparation, gratitude, justice, beneficence, non-maleficence, self-improvement) that can conflict; in a case, judgement decides which duty binds. This is a deontological response to Kant's rigidity: duties are real but not exceptionless. Evaluation: Ross resolves conflicts of duty and fits moral experience, but 'judgement' is unexplained and risks intuitionism without criteria; A* essays use Ross to moderate Kant and weigh whether the price of rigidity is worth the security of exceptionless duties.

Further evaluation: (6) the moral argument as postulate - Kant's summum bonum reasoning grounds God and immortality in practical necessity, which religious ethicists (Newman, C.S. Lewis' moral argument tradition) develop into a moral argument for God's existence: objective moral law points to a moral lawgiver; Kant himself denies this is demonstration - A* essays should distinguish Kant's use from the natural-law moral argument; (7) autonomy as strength - each agent legislates the law for themselves, so Kantian ethics avoids both divine command theory (morality is not arbitrary) and relativism (the law is universal) - the standard answer to 'is Kant's ethics religious?'.

Evaluation and exam technique: applications

Spec coverage: AQA 7062/1A section 3.2.1.4 (Kantian ethics: good will, duty, categorical imperative, summum bonum, postulates; comparison with religious ethics); OCR H573/02 (Kantian ethics: duty, categorical imperative, universalisability, humanity as an end, applications to euthanasia, business ethics, sexual ethics).

Applications to have ready: euthanasia (Kant: suicide is a perfect duty violation; the patient's rationality may not be used to end their own life - contrast with Singer's preference utilitarianism), business ethics (workers and consumers as ends, not means - misleading advertising and exploitative wages are wrong regardless of profit; contrast with utilitarian cost-benefit), sexual ethics (using a person merely as a means is exploitation; but Kant's framework says little about marriage as an institution). Name one case per application.

Exam structure: AQA Paper 1 Section B: each question = 25 marks - 10-mark AO1 'Explain...' then 15-mark AO2 'Critically discuss...'; two questions answered. OCR H573/02: extended 'Critically discuss' essays plus AO1 components. Plan for AO2: (1) state the claim; (2) strongest support; (3) strongest critique; (4) reply and weigh (Ross as moderator); (5) justified verdict.

  • Pitfalls: confusing hypothetical and categorical imperatives; treating consequences as irrelevant to the rightness of the maxim but asserting Kant ignores outcomes entirely (he holds outcomes are morally irrelevant to worth - the distinction matters); presenting the postulates as proofs of God's existence; missing the humanity-as-an-end formulation in applied answers (examiners expect it); asserting Kant's lying-to-the-murderer reply without stating his reasoning; ignoring Ross.
  • Examiner tips: quote 'good without qualification' and 'never merely as a means'; work through the four-step universalisability test on one maxim in AO1; distinguish perfect and imperfect duties; name Hegel's 'empty formalism' and the murderer case in AO2; end with a justified verdict - e.g. Kantian ethics secures dignity and universalisability but Ross's prima facie duties better handle conflicts, so the second formulation survives better than the first.

Kant and religious ethics: comparison

Comparison with natural law: both are deontological and rule-based; both hold some acts intrinsically wrong. But NL grounds precepts in human nature's telos (union with God) knowable by reason; Kant grounds the moral law in pure practical reason alone - the law is legislated by the rational will, not discovered in nature. Kant's universalisability is formal (any rational agent can test it); Aquinas' precepts are substantive (they depend on what human nature is for). Critique: Kant's formal test may be too thin to generate substantive duties (Hegel's 'empty formalism'); Aquinas' substantive precepts may be too thick for a secular age (the telos is contested).

Comparison with Christian ethics: Kant's ethics is not divine command theory - morality is grounded in reason, not God's will; God is postulated only to secure the summum bonum. Christian ethics (agape - Fletcher; revelation - Barth) grounds morality in love and revelation: agape is unconditional but situational; Kantian duty is unconditional and rule-bound. Bonhoeffer's critique: ethics is not about principles (Kantian or otherwise) but about responsible action in concrete situations - his responsibility ethics rejects principles-based accounts generally while sharing Kant's seriousness about the will. Weighing point: Kantian ethics and Christian ethics converge on the dignity of persons (imago Dei; humanity as an end) but diverge on grounding (reason vs revelation) - A* essays use this convergence-divergence pattern across applied topics (euthanasia, business, sexual ethics).

Applications for this section: euthanasia (Kant: perfect duty vs NL: sanctity of life - converge on prohibition but for different reasons); business (Kant: dignity vs utilitarian: outcomes); sexual ethics (Kant: never merely as a means vs agape: love decides). Name the contrast in each application.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: 'Kantian ethics provides a convincing approach to moral decision-making.' Critically discuss this claim. [AQA 7062 15-mark AO2 essay / OCR H573 extended essay]
Mark-scheme logic: AO1 required: good will (only thing good without qualification); duty vs inclination (shopkeeper example); hypothetical vs categorical imperatives; first formulation (universalisability, four-step test); second formulation (humanity as an end, dignity vs price); third formulation (kingdom of ends); summum bonum and postulates (freedom, God, immortality); optionally Ross' prima facie duties. AO2 required: (1) strengths - universalisability matches fairness convictions, dignity grounds human rights, motive matters; (2) weaknesses - conflicts of duty (lying to the murderer at the door) and Kant's indefensible reply; Hegel's 'empty formalism'; absurd universalisable maxims show the test is too permissive; (3) consequentialist reply - ignoring outcomes is irrational (Bentham/Mill); (4) Ross: prima facie duties resolve conflicts but 'judgement' is unexplained; (5) weigh - e.g. Kantian ethics secures dignity but rigidity over conflicts is decisive, so Ross' moderated deontology is more convincing; conclude with a justified verdict.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Explain Kant's first formulation of the categorical imperative. [10 marks AO1]
Answer: AO1 answer: Kant (Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals, 1785) - the good will is the only thing good without qualification; moral worth comes from acting from duty, not inclination. The first formulation: 'act only according to that maxim through which you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law.' Four-step test for a maxim ('I will make a false promise to get money'): (1) formulate the maxim; (2) universalise it; (3) check for contradiction - if everyone made false promises when convenient, promise-keeping itself would collapse; (4) conclude the maxim cannot be willed as universal law, so lying is wrong. Distinguish perfect duties (never permissible: lying, suicide) from imperfect duties (sometimes permissible: helping others). Marks awarded for: the formulation quoted, the four-step test applied, and the perfect/imperfect distinction.
'Kantian ethics provides a convincing approach to moral decision-making.' Critically discuss. [15 marks AO2]
Answer: AO1 anchor: good will, categorical imperative (universalisability, humanity as an end), postulates. AO2 required: (1) strengths - universalisability matches fairness convictions; dignity of persons grounds human rights; motive matters - consequences cannot make an act moral; (2) conflicts of duty - lying to the murderer at the door: Kant's reply (tell the truth even then) is widely judged indefensible; (3) cold formalism - Hegel: 'empty formalism'; ignoring outcomes is monstrous in hard cases (returning a weapon to a madman); (4) too permissive - absurd maxims ('everyone wear odd socks') universalise without contradiction; (5) consequentialist reply (Bentham, Mill) - morality exists to make lives go better; (6) Ross - prima facie duties resolve conflicts but 'judgement' is unexplained; (7) weigh - e.g. Kantian ethics secures dignity and universalisability but rigidity over conflicts is decisive; conclude with a justified verdict, e.g. the second formulation (humanity as an end) survives better than the first.
Explain Kant's second formulation (humanity as an end in itself) and one application. [10 marks AO1]
Answer: AO1 answer: 'act in such a way that you treat humanity, whether in your own person or in the person of any other, never merely as a means to an end but always at the same time as an end.' Persons have dignity (beyond price), not market value. Application: misleading advertising uses consumers' rationality as an instrument - deception fails this formulation as well as universalisability; exploitative wages and child labour use workers merely as means; in euthanasia, Kant holds suicide is a perfect duty violation because it uses the person as a means to escape suffering. Marks awarded for: the formulation quoted, the dignity vs price distinction, and one accurate application (business or euthanasia).
'Kant's reply to the murderer at the door is indefensible.' Critically assess. [15 marks AO2]
Answer: AO1 anchor: Kant's case - a murderer asks where your friend is hiding; Kant holds you must tell the truth (lying is a perfect duty violation), even if the murderer uses your answer. AO2 required: (1) Kant's reasoning - universalising lying collapses the practice of truth-telling; we cannot control outcomes, only the will; if you lie and the friend escapes anyway, the lie was still wrong; (2) critique - if the murderer uses your truthful answer, you are implicated; a rigid outcome (handing over the friend) is monstrous; (3) Ross - prima facie duties (fidelity vs beneficence) can conflict; judgement decides which binds - this resolves the case where Kant's exceptionless duties cannot; (4) consequentialist reply - the calculus clearly favours the lie; ignoring outcomes is irrational; (5) defence of Kant - the case may be constructed so the lie is certain to fail and the truth certain to succeed, when truth-telling is rational; (6) weigh - the reply is indefensible in the standard case but the exceptionless-duty framework has value; conclude with a justified verdict.
Explain the summum bonum and Kant's postulates of practical reason. [10 marks AO1]
Answer: AO1 answer: the summum bonum is the highest good - virtue rewarded with happiness. Morality demands it, but we cannot guarantee it in this life, so practical reason must postulate: freedom (duty is possible only if the will is free), immortality (endless progress toward complete virtue), and God (who harmonises virtue and happiness). These are not proofs of God's existence but conditions of moral coherence - 'I have found it necessary to deny knowledge in order to make room for faith.' Kant uses this to reject the moral argument as demonstration while retaining it as practical necessity. Marks awarded for: the summum bonum defined, all three postulates named, and the distinction between postulates and proofs.
'Ross' prima facie duties improve on Kant.' Critically discuss. [15 marks AO2]
Answer: AO1 anchor: Ross (The Right and the Good, 1930) - conditional duties (fidelity, reparation, gratitude, justice, beneficence, non-maleficence, self-improvement) that can conflict; judgement decides which binds. AO2 required: (1) improvement - Ross resolves conflicts of duty (lying to the murderer at the door) where Kant's exceptionless perfect duties cannot; fits moral experience - duties are real but not exceptionless; (2) cost - 'judgement' is unexplained and risks intuitionism without criteria; (3) Kantian reply - exceptionless duties give the moral law its authority; Ross's flexibility risks eroding it into a rule of thumb; (4) Ross still retains Kantian core - duty, dignity, universalisability as a default; (5) weigh - Ross better handles conflicts while keeping the deontological core; conclude with a justified verdict, e.g. Ross improves on Kant's rigidity, but the unexplained 'judgement' means his account is a modification of, not a replacement for, the categorical imperative.
