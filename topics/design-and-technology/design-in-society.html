Design in Society

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA

A* A-level Product Design revision for AQA 7552 Paper 2: design influences and consumer choice, inclusive design, the product life cycle, circular economy and six Rs, standards, legislation, ethics and intellectual property (3.1.8-3.1.13, 3.2.3, 3.2.8, 3.2.10).

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain the importance of marketing and brand identity - customer identification, labelling, packaging, corporate identification and global marketing including social media and viral marketing (AQA 7552 3.1.13)
  • Discuss how socio-economic influences and major technological developments shape design, and describe the product life cycle stages (AQA 7552 3.2.3, 3.2.3.4)
  • Explain the development of inclusive products usable by a wide range of users including disabled people, children and the elderly (AQA 7552 3.1.8)
  • Discuss responsible design: the circular economy, the six Rs, design for manufacture, maintenance, repair and disassembly, product miles and packaging impact (AQA 7552 3.2.8, 3.1.11)
  • Explain consumer protection: legislation, the BSI and safety standards, and national/international standards including ISO, WEEE, ROHS, energy ratings and eco-labelling (AQA 7552 3.1.9, 3.2.10)
  • Discuss the ethical responsibilities of designers and manufacturers, including Fairtrade, culturally acceptable products and open design (AQA 7552 3.2.3, 3.1.10)

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Inclusive design: Designing products that can be used by the widest possible range of users - including disabled people, children and the elderly - without needing adaptation.
Circular economy: An economic system in which products, components and materials are kept in use through repair, reuse, remanufacture and recycling, designing out waste rather than following take-make-dispose.
Six Rs of sustainability: Design strategies for reducing environmental impact: reduce, reuse, rethink, recycle, repair, refuse.
Product life cycle: The stages of a product's commercial life: introduction, evolution/growth, maturity, decline and replacement.
Product miles: The distance a product and its components travel from raw material source through manufacture to the consumer; global manufacturing increases them and the associated emissions.
Fairtrade: A certification system ensuring producers in developing countries receive fair prices and decent working conditions; it influences both design decisions and consumer demand.
British Standards Institution (BSI): The national standards body whose kitemark certifies that a product has been independently tested to meet safety and quality standards.
Registered design: Intellectual property protecting a product's visual appearance - its shape, configuration, pattern or ornament - through formal registration.
Open design: Developing products for the common good of society with designs freely shared, e.g. humanitarian projects and file sharing for 3D printing.
WEEE directive: Waste Electrical and Electronic Equipment directive - places responsibility on producers for the collection, recycling and safe disposal of electrical products at end of life.

Consumer choice, marketing and the life cycle (3.1.13, 3.2.3)

Consumer choice is shaped deliberately (3.1.13). Marketing and brand identity work through: customer identification - defining exactly who the product targets; labelling and packaging - the legal and persuasive layer of the product that carries branding, information and polymer codes; corporate identification - consistent logos, colours and design language across a product range so consumers recognise and trust the brand; and the concept of global marketing - promoting and advertising products worldwide using new technologies such as social media and viral marketing, where content spreads peer-to-peer at almost no media cost. Product costing and profit, and awareness of the role of entrepreneurs, complete the section: entrepreneurs spot unmet needs and take design risks to serve them, and collaboration between designers (virtual and face-to-face) drives innovation.

Socio-economic influences (3.2.3) explain why design looks the way it does: post-WW1 the Bauhaus developed furniture for mass production, making functional design affordable; WW2 rationing produced utility products - austere, material-efficient design under government constraint; contemporary times show fashion cycles and demand for mass-produced furniture alongside decorative design. Major developments in technology - microelectronics, new materials, new methods of manufacture, advancements in CAD/CAM - continually reshape what is possible and what consumers expect.

The product life cycle (3.2.3.4): introduction (launch, low sales, high promotion cost), evolution/growth (sales rise as the market adopts), maturity (peak sales, competition, refinement), decline (sales fall as tastes or technology move on) and replacement. The exam wants examples of how designers refine and re-develop products to extend maturity - iterative smartphone updates, cars facelifted between generations, the VW Beetle re-launched as the New Beetle to harvest nostalgia. Link each stage to a marketing response: price cuts and relaunches fight decline; brand extensions stretch maturity.

Deepen the global marketing bullet with the mechanism: social media and viral marketing spread content peer-to-peer at negligible media cost, so brand awareness scales fast - but is less controlled than traditional advertising. Corporate identification (consistent logo, colour and design language) builds recognition and trust across a range; packaging and labelling carry both the legal information and the persuasive layer. Entrepreneurs spot unmet needs, take design risks and secure the investment to serve them; collaborative working between designers - virtual and face-to-face - is how new and innovative products are developed, and market research data is interpreted to calculate costs, prices and profit (3.1.13 maths links: profit = revenue - costs; price set against market research on willingness to pay).

Add life cycle marketing responses to complete 3.2.3.4: promotion is heaviest at introduction (building awareness); growth relies on widening distribution; maturity is defended by refinement, facelifts, brand extensions and updated specifications; decline is met with price cuts, relaunches or harvesting before replacement. Quote one product refined across its life cycle - iterative smartphone updates or the VW Beetle relaunched as the New Beetle - and link the design refinement to the stage: each iteration extends maturity by re-engaging demand.

Inclusive design and fitness for purpose (3.1.8)

Inclusive design (3.1.8) means developing products that can be used by a wide range of users including the disabled, children and the elderly. The principle: design out the barriers rather than design a separate specialist product. Classic evidence to quote: lever taps usable by arthritic hands where crosshead taps are not; OXO Good Grips-style oversized soft handles that suit everyone, not just users with reduced grip strength; high-contrast controls and audible feedback for users with impaired vision; step-free access and adjustable-height features.

Anchor the answer in aesthetics, ergonomics and anthropometrics (3.1.8): ergonomic data - posture, reach, grip strength, force required - shapes the interaction; anthropometric data with percentiles sizes the product for the 5th to 95th percentile user; aesthetics ensure the inclusive product is not stigmatising - the OXO lesson is that designing for the extreme user produced a mainstream commercial success. Perceptible information (visible, audible, tactile cues) and tolerance for error complete the checklist for exam answers.

Questions typically ask you to explain how a given product could be made more inclusive or discuss why designers have a responsibility to design inclusively. Structure: identify the excluded user group and the barrier; propose the design modification (control type, size, force, feedback, adjustability); link to ergonomic/anthropometric data; finish with the commercial and ethical argument - the population is ageing, inclusive products reach bigger markets, and legislation (Equality Act obligations on service provision) increasingly expects accessibility. Avoid the common error of describing accessibility features without naming which user they include - the mark is for the user link.

Build the evidence bank with a worked example chain. OXO Good Grips: designed for users with arthritis (reduced grip strength and dexterity) - oversized, cushioned, non-slip handles - and succeeded commercially with everyone, demonstrating that designing for the extreme user produces mainstream products (inclusive design is not niche design). Road signage (Margaret Calvert, 3.2.2): legible typeface and consistent pictograms usable by the widest population at speed - inclusive communication design. Everyday adaptations: lever taps instead of crosshead (arthritic hands), high-contrast controls with tactile and audible feedback (impaired vision), adjustable-height furniture (children to adults), lightweight materials and easy-open packaging (elderly users with reduced strength).

Link to fitness for purpose (3.1.8): a product is only fit for purpose if the actual user range can operate it - hence anthropometric percentiles (5th-95th) and ergonomic data drive dimensions, control forces and feedback. The exam phrase is that products must be developed to be inclusive 'so that they can be used by a wide range of users including the disabled, children and the elderly' - name the excluded group, name the barrier, name the modification. The commercial close: ageing populations and legislation on accessibility widen the market for inclusive products, so inclusion is a business argument, not only an ethical one.

Sustainability and responsible design (3.2.8, 3.1.11)

Responsible design (3.2.8) rests on the circular economy: products designed to conserve energy, materials and components, with minimum environmental impact across raw material extraction, consumption, ease of repair, maintenance and end of life. Sustainable manufacturing uses alternative energy and methods to minimise waste, and considers the waste, surplus and by-products of manufacture itself - reuse of off-cuts, chemicals, heat and water - plus the cost implications of dealing with waste and the impact of global manufacturing on product miles.

Design for manufacture, maintenance, repair and disposal (3.1.11) turns the principle into concrete design decisions - learn these as a checklist because discussion questions reward applied specifics:

  • Ease of manufacture: ribs and webbing to reduce material thickness; snap fittings to remove fixings/adhesives; internal moulded screw posts for self-tapping screws; pre-made and standardised components; moulded-in texture to cut process count; self-finishing polymers.
  • Maintenance and repair: temporary and integral fixings; standardised parts; allowing for service and replacement of parts; ability to upgrade with software downloads.
  • Disposal: labelling of materials to aid separation for recycling (polymer codes); making products easy to disassemble or separate; application of the six Rs - reduce the quantity of materials, toxic materials, damaging materials and associated energy use; reuse components; rethink by using eco-friendly alternatives; recycle into new products (plus repair and refuse).
  • Active disassembly: using smart materials such as shape memory alloys so a casing releases itself at end of life, and biodegradable parts that separate naturally.

Packaging is a set-piece exam context (3.2.8): the environmental impact of excessive packaging and plastics - design minimal, mono-material, labelled packaging that still protects and sells. Weigh the trade-offs to reach the top band: snap fittings speed assembly and enable recycling but can weaken the joint; reducing material risks product damage in transit, which wastes more than it saves if failure rates rise. Responsible design is a balance, and saying so is what earns the evaluation marks.

Structure life cycle thinking as the exam wants it (3.2.8): minimum environmental impact across raw material extraction, consumption, ease of repair, maintenance and end of life - an assessment of the whole life, not just manufacture. Conservation of energy and resources: products designed to conserve energy, materials and components; sustainable manufacturing using alternative energy and methods to minimise waste; the impact of waste, surplus and by-products created in manufacture - reuse of material off-cuts, chemicals, heat and water - plus the cost implications of dealing with waste and the impact of global manufacturing on product miles. A 6-mark answer moves through the stages: extraction (recycled or certified-sustainable inputs), manufacture (waste reuse, alternative energy), use (energy-efficient operation, repairability), end of life (disassembly, labelled materials for recycling).

For design for manufacture, maintenance, repair and disposal (3.1.11), add the 'why' behind each feature: ribs and webbing allow thinner walls, cutting material quantity and moulding cycle time; snap fittings remove fixing operations and make separation possible at end of life; internal moulded screw posts accept self-tapping screws without separate nuts; moulded-in texture removes a finishing process; self-finishing polymers need no coating at all; temporary and integral fixings plus standardised parts allow service, repair and part replacement; software-download upgrades extend service life without hardware replacement. Labelling materials (polymer codes) aids separation for recycling, and active disassembly - casings released by smart materials such as shape memory alloy when triggered - automates end-of-life separation. Every feature is an argument that ends 'fewer processes, less material, or easier recovery' - that closing link is the mark.

Standards, safety, ethics and intellectual property (3.1.9, 3.1.10, 3.2.10)

Consumer protection (3.1.9): legislation used to protect consumers and its impact on product design - the Consumer Rights Act (2015) (goods must be as described, of satisfactory quality and fit for purpose) and the Sale of Goods Act (1979) predecessor; the British Standards Institute (BSI) and how specific products are tested to meet safety standards (the kitemark); measures to ensure toy safety such as the Lion Mark; and advice to consumers through manufacturer instructions, safety warnings and aftercare advice. Designers also work within the Health and Safety at Work Act (1974) and COSHH when specifying materials, adhesives and finishes that are hazards.

National and international standards (3.2.10) - learn the list with what each does: ISO (International Organisation for Standardisation) international quality/system standards; ROHS directive restricting hazardous substances in electronics; the battery directive (removable/recyclable batteries); polymer codes for identification and recycling; packaging directives (producer responsibility for packaging waste); WEEE directives for e-waste; energy ratings of products; and eco-labelling - the Mobius Loop, the European Eco-label, the EC energy label, the Energy Efficient label and logo, the Forest Stewardship Council (FSC) mark for sustainable timber, and the EPA Energy Star.

Intellectual property (3.1.10): copyright and design rights (automatic protection of original work); patents (protecting an invention's function for a fixed term); registered designs (protecting appearance); trademarks and logos (protecting brand identity). Balance with open design: developing products for the common good of society with designs freely shared - humanitarian projects and file sharing for 3D printing. Ethical issues (3.2.3) tie it together: products made using sustainable materials and ethical production methods; products that are culturally acceptable and not offensive to people of different race, gender or religious belief; inclusive products; design addressing social problems such as poverty, health and wellbeing, migration and housing; the impact of Fairtrade on design and consumer demand; and designing to the six Rs. The top band links the ethic to a concrete design decision, not a generic list.

Consolidate IP with the differences examiners test: copyright/design right is automatic protection of original work (drawings, designs) without registration; patents protect how an invention works for a fixed term (typically 20 years) and must be applied for; registered designs protect appearance and must be registered; trademarks and logos protect brand identity and can be renewed indefinitely. The designer's stake: protecting IP preserves the commercial value of innovation - which is why open design (3.1.10) is a deliberate exception, developing products for the common good of society with designs freely shared, e.g. humanitarian projects and file sharing for 3D printing.

For the ethics bullet (3.2.3), structure the responsibilities: products made using sustainable materials and ethical production methods; products that are culturally acceptable and not offensive to people of different race, gender or religious belief; inclusive products; products that could assist with social problems - poverty, health and wellbeing, migration and housing; the impact of Fairtrade on design and consumer demand; and designing with the six Rs. The top-band link pairs each ethic with a decision: sourcing Fairtrade-certified materials changes the supply chain, not just the label; culturally acceptable design means testing with representative users. Standards close the loop - ISO systems, BSI testing and eco-labels (Mobius Loop, FSC, Energy Star) are how ethical claims become verifiable, which is what makes the consumer trust them.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: AQA 7552 Paper 2 (Designing and making principles) style - extended response: A vacuum cleaner manufacturer wants to improve the sustainability of its best-selling product. Discuss how the principles of the circular economy and design for disassembly could be applied to the vacuum cleaner, and how relevant directives would affect its design. [12 marks]
Mark-scheme logic: Mark-scheme logic (12 marks = balanced discussion applying principles to the product, with directives): (1) CIRCULAR ECONOMY APPLIED - design for repair with standardised, serviceable parts (brushes, filters, belts) and software upgrades; use recycled and recyclable polymers; energy-efficient motor reduces in-use impact; consider a take-back/refurbishment scheme. (2) SIX RS APPLIED - reduce material through ribs and webbing in the casing; reuse components across models; rethink with eco-friendly materials; recycle with labelled polymer parts. (3) DESIGN FOR DISASSEMBLY - snap fittings and integral fixings instead of adhesives so the casing separates; polymer codes moulded into parts to aid sorting; active disassembly using shape memory alloy fasteners; minimal mono-material packaging. (4) DIRECTIVES - WEEE makes the producer responsible for end-of-life collection and recycling, so design must enable separation; ROHS restricts hazardous substances in the electronics; the battery directive requires removable batteries; energy ratings inform consumer choice and pressure efficiency. CONCLUSION (AO3) - weigh trade-offs (snap fittings vs joint strength; material reduction vs durability in transit) and conclude that disassembly plus labelling delivers the most recyclability at acceptable risk. Full-mark answers apply each principle specifically to the vacuum cleaner and balance the arguments.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

State what is meant by inclusive design. [2 marks]
Answer: Designing products that can be used by as wide a range of users as possible (1) including disabled people, children and the elderly, without needing adaptation or separate specialist versions (1).
Explain how the British Standards kitemark and the Lion Mark are used when designing a child's toy. [4 marks]
Answer: The BSI kitemark shows the toy has been independently tested to meet a published safety standard (e.g. toy safety standard EN 71) (1); it gives consumers and retailers confidence the toy is safe, so it influences purchasing (1); the Lion Mark is the toy industry's safety mark, used to show the toy meets safety and age-grading expectations (1); both push the designer to test materials, small parts, cords and finishes against safety standards before sale (1).
Explain two ways a designer can make a product easier to disassemble at end of life. [4 marks]
Answer: Use snap fittings or integral fixings instead of adhesives so parts separate without tools or force (1) - adhesives make separation and material sorting almost impossible (1). Label every polymer part with its polymer code and use mono-materials where possible (1) so components can be separated and sorted for recycling at end of life (1). (Also accept: standard screws instead of security fixings; active disassembly with shape memory alloy fasteners that release when heated.)
Discuss the impact of global manufacturing on product miles and the environment. [6 marks]
Answer: Global supply chains move components and products across the world several times before sale, so product miles and transport emissions rise (1); outsourced manufacture may use energy from fossil-fuel grids with weaker environmental regulation (1); JIT and global logistics cut inventory but not transport distance (1); counter-arguments: consolidated shipping is efficient per unit, and local sourcing shortens miles but may raise unit cost (1); consumers increasingly factor product miles and eco-labelling into choice, pressuring brands to localise or offset (1); answers must discuss both impact and response, tied to environmental consequences, not just describe globalisation (1).
Explain how the WEEE directive affects the design of an electric drill. [4 marks]
Answer: WEEE places responsibility on the producer for collection, recycling and safe disposal of the electrical product at end of life (1); so the drill must be designed for disassembly - snap fittings/integral fixings rather than adhesives, standard screws (1); parts must carry polymer codes and avoid mixed materials so they can be separated and sorted for recycling (1); hazardous substances must be minimised in the electronics, and any battery must be removable in line with related directives (1).
