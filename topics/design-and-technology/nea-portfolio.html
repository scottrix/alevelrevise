Non-Exam Assessment (NEA) Portfolio

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA

A* NEA guidance for AQA 7552: the five assessment criteria A-E with mark allocations and AO mapping, evidence requirements, the 45-page rule, feedback rules and top-band strategies for the design-and-make portfolio (worth 50% of the A-level).

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Outline the NEA structure: a substantial design-and-make task worth 100 marks (scaled x2 to 200), 50% of the A-level, with five criteria A-E mapped to AO1, AO2 and AO3 (AQA 7552 4.2, 4.4, 4.5)
  • Plan Criterion A: justify the chosen context and user, and combine primary and secondary investigation, practical experimentation and disassembly (AQA 7552 4.5.1)
  • Produce a challenging design brief and a live, measurable design specification with integrated project management (AQA 7552 4.5.2)
  • Develop innovative design proposals justified against the brief and specification, with modelling, a third-party manufacturing specification and contingency planning (AQA 7552 4.5.3)
  • Manufacture a high-quality prototype with justified material selection, QA/QC evidence and modifications driven by user trials and testing (AQA 7552 4.5.4)
  • Evaluate throughout using focused testing, user/client involvement and consideration of different production methods, within the portfolio evidence rules (AQA 7552 4.5.5, 4.4.2)

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Context: The broad area, selected by the student, from which a design possibility is developed; it must offer the scope and complexity for work worthy of an A-level.
Design brief: A clearly stated and challenging statement of the problem to be solved, developed by the student from the context and addressing the needs of a user, client or market.
Live document: A design specification that is constantly referenced and updated throughout the project, rather than written once and forgotten.
Iterative process: The portfolio is developed through repeated loops of designing, making, testing and evaluating; the five criteria are not a linear checklist to complete in order.
Primary investigation: First-hand research: questionnaires, surveys, interviews, focus groups, visits, product disassembly and practical experimentation.
Candidate Record Form (CRF): The official form recording supervision, use of specialist processes outside school, and any individual support given; marks must be adjusted to reflect assistance.
Manufacturing specification: A fully detailed set of instructions, referencing quality control checks, that allows a third party to accurately manufacture the prototype.
Generic feedback: The only teacher feedback permitted during development: oral/written advice on focus and resources; never corrections, templates, model answers or writing frames.
Best-fit marking: Holistic band assessment: highest mark if the work fully meets all statements, mid-band if mostly, lowest if it just meets the majority.
Quality assurance (QA): The procedures and policies put in place before and during manufacture to ensure products are produced accurately and within acceptable tolerances.

How the NEA is marked (4.2, 4.4, 4.5)

The NEA is a substantial design-and-make task producing a final prototype based on a context and design brief developed by the student. It is worth 100 marks, scaled by a factor of 2 to 200, and contributes 50% of the A-level. The brief must be of appropriate complexity and contain a degree of uncertainty of the outcome so an iterative process of designing, making, testing and evaluating is genuinely possible.

CriterionWhat it assessesMarksAO
A - Identifying and investigating design possibilitiesContext rationale, investigation, first concepts20AO1
B - Producing a design brief and specificationChallenging brief, live measurable spec, project management10AO1
C - Development of design proposal(s)Justified, innovative development; modelling; manufacturing spec25AO2
D - Development of design prototype(s)Justified making; skill, precision, QA/QC, modification25AO2
E - Analysing and evaluatingOngoing evaluation, testing, user involvement, final critique20AO3

Total: AO1 30, AO2 50, AO3 20 = 100 raw marks. Memorise the split - it dictates where to spend time. Critical rules: the five criteria are not a linear process and criteria should be cross-referenced throughout (work is rewarded wherever it appears in the portfolio); bands are viewed holistically; no work or work below A-level standard scores zero. The folder should be concise - no more than 45 pages is recommended; exceeding it self-penalises by losing focus, and falling short self-penalises by under-covering the criteria. AOs overall for the qualification: AO1 15%, AO2 25%, AO3 25%, AO4 35% - the NEA carries all of AO1 and AO2, which is why 50 marks of the 100 sit on designing and making.

Budget your 45 pages against the marks: Criteria A and B (30 marks) need roughly 8-10 pages of focused context rationale and investigation plus a tight brief/spec; Criterion C (25 marks) is the biggest single block - give it 12-15 pages of developed, justified proposals with modelling evidence; Criterion D (25 marks) is evidenced mainly through photographs of making with brief annotations - 8-10 pages; Criterion E (20 marks) is spread through the portfolio but needs a strong final evaluation of 3-4 pages. Because bands are awarded holistically and work is rewarded wherever it appears, keep evaluation and user involvement visible in every section rather than quarantined at the end.

Two marking mechanics matter for strategy. First, best-fit: where work fully meets all statements the highest mark is awarded; mostly meets - mid-band; just meets the majority - lowest mark. So completeness of evidence, not brilliance in one area, decides the band. Second, the zero rule: no work for a criterion, or work below A-level standard, scores zero - so every criterion needs visible, A-level-worthy evidence, and an undeveloped area drags more than a strong area lifts.

Criteria A and B: investigating and specifying (4.5.1, 4.5.2)

Criterion A (20 marks) starts with the context. The top band (16-20) demands an excellent rationale for the context selected, with continuous reference throughout the project to the end user and the constraints - not a rationale written once and abandoned. The investigation must employ a comprehensive range of strategies: both primary methods (visits, surveys, questionnaires, interviews) and secondary methods (internet, books, magazines), plus practical experimentation and disassembly where relevant. All sources fully referenced is an explicit top-band statement - keep a source log from day one. First concepts must be fully relevant to the context and feasible for further development, communicated through a fully appropriate variety of methods; at this stage they demonstrate initial thinking and stimulate later, more considered proposals.

Criterion B (10 marks) is small but quick to secure. The brief must be clearly stated and challenging, addressing the context and the needs and wants of the intended user. The specification must be fully detailed and well explained, informed by the investigations, with specific measurable outcomes as well as qualitative statements - 'the product must weigh under 2kg', not 'lightweight'. It is a live and working document, constantly referenced throughout. The top band also requires a detailed project management approach - time management and determining quantities and costs of materials - fully integrated into the specification: put a Gantt-style plan and a costed materials list inside the spec, not in an appendix.

Common losses at Criterion A: investigation that does not relate directly to the context; issues identified but not addressed; single-strategy research (one questionnaire). At Criterion B: briefs that lack challenge, specs with no measurable statements, and project management bolted on rather than integrated.

Make the investigation comprehensive by pairing methods with purposes: a questionnaire quantifies preferences across a sample (present results as graphs or charts); interviews and focus groups explain the why behind the numbers; a visit or observation captures the real context and constraints; product analysis of an existing solution benchmarks features and costs; disassembly reveals how competitors solve joints, fixings and assembly; practical experimentation tests whether a technique or material actually works before you commit a design decision to it. Every method should end in a documented conclusion that a later design decision references - that chain (method → finding → decision) is what the top-band descriptor 'issues are identified and fully addressed' means in practice.

For the specification, write measurable and qualitative statements side by side and make them auditable: 'the prototype must support a user weighing up to 110kg without visible deflection' is measurable; 'the product should feel comfortable in one-handed use' is qualitative but testable through a user trial. Include appearance/ aesthetics, function, user requirements, cost of materials, size and weight, safety, manufacture methods and timescale - then integrate the project management: a schedule of activities, quantities and costs of materials, and contingency time. The top band explicitly wants time management and quantities/costs fully integrated into the specification, not appended.

Criteria C and D: developing and making (4.5.3, 4.5.4)

Criterion C (25 marks) - development of design proposals. The top band (19-25) requires the rationale for design decisions to be clearly documented and fully justified with constant reference to the design brief, specification and investigations; originality, creativity and a willingness to take design risks; excellent use of a variety of modelling techniques - part modelling, practising manufacturing and finishing techniques, scale models, material experimentation; and ongoing development through exploration of and experimentation with different materials, techniques and processes. You must also produce a comprehensive, fully detailed manufacturing specification that makes specific reference to relevant quality control checks and allows fully accurate interpretation by a third party, plus project management that allows for contingency planning. Working drawings, plans and patterns are expected; CAD is encouraged but must not be the only form of design communication.

Criterion D (25 marks) - development of the prototype. The top band requires excellent justification for selection of materials, components, techniques and processes, demonstrating excellent understanding of material properties (link this to the materials content of Paper 1); significant complexity or challenge throughout production; excellent manufacturing skills combined with excellent understanding of dimensional accuracy and precision; selection and use of appropriate tools, machinery and equipment - including CAM where required, though CAM must not be the only form of manufacturing used; and modifications made in a fully considered manner in light of feedback from user trials and third-party feedback and as a result of testing and evaluation carried out against earlier iterations. Quality assurance must be evident throughout, with it clear where planned quality control checks were applied, and evidence throughout that appropriate health and safety processes were considered and employed.

The mechanism is identical in both criteria: justify → evidence → modify. Every design decision is justified against the brief/spec; every justification is evidenced (photos, annotations, test data); every test result produces a documented modification. Photograph every stage of making - Criterion D can only be evidenced through the portfolio plus photographic evidence of the final prototype.

Evidence quality control checks in the manufacturing specification by name, because 'makes specific reference to relevant quality control checks' is a top-band statement: measuring with vernier callipers and micrometers against tolerances; go/no-go gauges for repeated holes and slots; laser or probe scanning to check profiles; templates and jigs to guarantee repeatable features; visual checks against the working drawing at each stage; and non-destructive testing (x-ray or ultrasound) where a weld or bond must be verified without destroying the part (3.2.9). Write each check into the plan at the stage where it applies - that is what allows a third party to manufacture accurately, the explicit purpose of the manufacturing specification.

In Criterion D, structure the photographic evidence like a test log: each stage photographed with a caption naming the process, the check applied and the outcome; each prototype iteration dated and compared against the previous one with what changed and why. Justify material and process selection against working properties (linking to Paper 1 knowledge), record the QC checks as they are applied, note health and safety controls (guards, extraction, PPE) as they are used, and log every modification with its trigger - user trial feedback, third-party comment, or a test result against an earlier iteration. The top band wants modifications made 'in a fully considered manner' - a dated, triggered, justified modification log is exactly that evidence.

Criterion E and the evidence rules (4.5.5, 4.4.2)

Criterion E (20 marks) has one vital instruction: evidence for analysing and evaluating can take place in any part of the NEA - it is not a final chapter. Students should be constantly analysing and recording thoughts to explain their thinking; ongoing evaluation must be seen to inform the decision-making process, particularly by bringing about modifications to design proposals and prototype development. Central to the top band is close and regular involvement of the proposed client/user(s) - ensuring the prototype is fit for purpose for them, not just for the student. The top band (16-20) requires: comprehensive analysis and evaluation throughout; focused and comprehensive testing with clear evidence of how results were used to inform the design and any modifications; a well-reasoned critical analysis of the final outcome linked clearly to the design brief and specification, with full justification of the extent the prototype is fit for purpose; and a comprehensive critical evaluation identifying how modifications could improve the outcome, with full justification and full consideration of how the prototype could be developed for different production methods (one-off → batch → mass).

Evidence and administration rules (4.4.2): the portfolio is written or digital, clearly evidencing how the assessment criteria have been met, with photographic evidence of the final manufactured prototype; it should not exceed 45 pages. Time limits are not specified because the portfolio is iterative and independent, while the final prototype is made under supervision in school/college; specialist processes or equipment beyond school may be used but must be documented on the Candidate Record Form (CRF). Feedback rules: students may redraft before final submission; teachers may give generic feedback (oral/written advice on focus, resources, key sections) but cannot correct work, provide templates, model answers or writing frames, or give specific improvement advice against the criteria. Once work is submitted for final assessment it cannot be revised. Any support given to an individual that was not given to the whole class must be recorded on the CRF and the mark adjusted to represent unaided achievement - so record what help you received, and never let assistance inflate work you cannot justify as your own.

Structure the final evaluation in four moves, mirroring the top band: (1) critical analysis against brief and spec - go through the specification point by point and judge with evidence how far the prototype satisfies each; (2) fitness for purpose for the client/user - user trial results and third-party feedback decide this, not your opinion; (3) justified modifications - identify how the outcome could be improved, with a full justification for each modification, not a wish list; (4) different production methods - consider how the prototype would be developed for one-off, batch and mass production, changing processes, materials and assembly for the scale. A conclusion that weighs trade-offs and commits to a recommendation completes the AO3 argument.

Finally, work the 45-page rule as a discipline: because the band is best-fit against documented evidence, dense justified pages beat sprawling description - every page should end in a decision, a finding or a justification that a marker can point to. Keep sources referenced as you go (all sources fully referenced is an explicit Criterion A top-band statement), keep the spec and project plan live, and remember the CRF: any help beyond generic class feedback must be recorded and the mark adjusted - plan to need none, and record any you do use.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: AQA 7552 NEA-style question - written paper variant: Explain how ongoing evaluation and user involvement should inform the development of a design portfolio in the AQA 7552 NEA. [10 marks]
Mark-scheme logic: Mark-scheme logic (10 marks = five developed points mapping to Criterion E descriptors): (1) ONGOING, NOT FINAL - evaluation must take place in any part of the NEA and be seen to inform the decision-making process, not sit as a final chapter; annotations record thinking as it happens. (2) TESTING → MODIFICATION - testing is carried out in a focused and comprehensive way, with clear evidence of how the results were used to inform the design and modifications to the prototype against earlier iterations. (3) USER/CLIENT INVOLVEMENT - close and regular involvement of the proposed client/user(s) ensures the prototype is fit for purpose and meets their needs rather than just the student's; user trials and third-party feedback drive fully considered modifications. (4) CRITICAL ANALYSIS OF THE OUTCOME - a well-reasoned critical analysis links the final prototype clearly to the design brief and specification and justifies the extent it is fit for purpose. (5) FUTURE DEVELOPMENT - the evaluation identifies justified modifications to improve the outcome and considers how the prototype could be developed for different production methods (one-off, batch, mass). Top-band answers are comprehensive, focused and evidenced throughout the portfolio.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

State the five NEA assessment criteria and their mark allocations. [5 marks]
Answer: A - identifying and investigating design possibilities: 20 marks; B - producing a design brief and specification: 10; C - development of design proposal(s): 25; D - development of design prototype(s): 25; E - analysing and evaluating: 20. Total 100 marks (AO1 30, AO2 50, AO3 20).
State two rules about teacher feedback in the 7552 NEA. [2 marks]
Answer: Any two of: feedback must be generic only - teachers cannot correct work or provide templates, model answers or writing frames (1); once work is submitted for final assessment it cannot be revised (1); any support given to an individual that was not given to the whole class must be recorded on the Candidate Record Form and the mark adjusted accordingly (1).
Explain why the design specification should be treated as a live document. [4 marks]
Answer: AQA requires a specification that is constantly referenced throughout the process (1); investigations continue to reveal information, so the spec is updated rather than fixed, keeping it a working guide to design thinking (1); it must contain measurable outcomes and qualitative statements, which are only useful if kept current against testing (1); the top band of Criterion B requires a detailed project management approach - time management and quantities/costs - fully integrated into the specification, which only works as a live document (1).
Explain what is meant by complexity and challenge in Criterion D, and give two examples of how a student could demonstrate it. [4 marks]
Answer: The brief must contain a degree of uncertainty of the outcome and the prototype must be worthy of A-level assessment - significant complexity or challenge involved throughout its production (1); it means the making cannot be a simple, low-risk task (1). Examples (any two): a compound-curved laminated or steam-bent component; a working mechanism or moving part; combining materials with different working properties; using CAM alongside hand processes where required; integral fixings or moulded screw posts requiring precise dimensioning (2).
A student photographs every prototype iteration but writes no annotations. Explain why this will limit their Criterion C and E marks. [6 marks]
Answer: Criterion C requires the rationale for design decisions to be clearly documented and fully justified with reference to the brief, specification and investigations - photographs alone document what changed, not why (1); annotations and notes are the evidence of design thinking that markers look for (1); without justification the development reads as refinement without reasoning, capping the band (1); Criterion E requires testing with clear evidence of how results informed the design and modifications (1); photographs without annotations do not link test results to decisions, so the modification loop is unevidenced (1); ongoing evaluation must be seen to inform decision-making - written analysis is the mechanism, so its absence caps both criteria (1).
