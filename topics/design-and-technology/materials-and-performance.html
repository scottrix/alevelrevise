Materials and Performance

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA

A* A-level Product Design revision for AQA 7552 Paper 1: classification, physical and mechanical properties, performance characteristics of papers, boards, woods, metals and polymers, plus enhancement methods and selection justification (3.1.1-3.1.3).

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Classify materials - metals (ferrous, non-ferrous, alloys), woods (hardwoods, softwoods, manufactured boards), polymers (thermoplastics, thermosets, elastomers), papers and boards, composites, smart and modern materials - and name examples of each (AQA 7552 3.1.1)
  • Select materials for given applications with justified reference to physical and mechanical properties, function, aesthetics, cost, manufacture and disposal (AQA 7552 3.1.1)
  • Describe workshop and industrial tests for tensile strength, toughness, hardness, malleability, corrosion and conductivity, and analyse the data they produce (AQA 7552 3.1.1)
  • Describe the performance characteristics of papers and boards, polymer based sheet and film, woods, metals and polymers, and explain why specific named materials suit specific applications (AQA 7552 3.1.2)
  • Explain how additives, lamination, resins and heat treatment enhance polymers, woods and metals (AQA 7552 3.1.3)
  • Construct extended AO4 justification answers linking property to function to context, including simple calculations of quantities, sizes and costs (AQA 7552 3.1.1, maths links)

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Physical property: A characteristic measured without changing the composition of the material, e.g. density, thermal conductivity, electrical conductivity.
Mechanical property: How a material responds to an applied force, e.g. tensile strength, compressive strength, hardness, toughness, malleability, elasticity.
Toughness: The ability of a material to absorb sudden shock or impact without fracturing; the opposite of brittleness.
Hardness: Resistance of a surface to scratching, indentation and wear.
Malleability: The ability of a material to be deformed by compression (hammering, rolling, pressing) without cracking.
Thermoset polymer: A polymer that cures permanently when heated or mixed with a hardener and cannot be remelted, e.g. epoxy resin, urea formaldehyde.
Elastomer: A polymer that can be stretched and returns to its original shape, e.g. natural rubber, neoprene, silicone, TPE.
Composite: Two or more materials combined so the resulting properties are enhanced beyond those of the individual components, e.g. CFRP, GRP, reinforced concrete, glulam.
Smart material: A material that responds to an external stimulus - a change in temperature, light level or pressure - e.g. shape memory alloy (Nitinol), thermochromic pigment.
Case hardening: A heat treatment that adds carbon to the surface of a low carbon steel component, producing a hard, wear-resistant skin over a tough core.

Classification and selection of materials (3.1.1)

Paper 1 expects instant, accurate classification. Learn the categories and one or two examples each - examiners award marks for named materials, not vague ones.

Selection is never a property list on its own. The mark scheme wants a justified explanation referencing six factors: physical and mechanical properties (working characteristics), product function, aesthetics, cost, manufacture and disposal. A 9-mark question on why a material is suitable needs three developed property-function links, each tied to the product's use.

Testing (3.1.1) questions ask how a test is set up, what is measured and how data is compared: tensile - specimen clamped in jaws, incremental load applied until fracture, stress-strain graph produced to find yield point and UTS; toughness - pendulum impact test, energy absorbed on fracture measured; hardness - indentation test, depth or diameter of the indent measured; malleability - controlled bending or rolling until cracking occurs; corrosion - exposure to salt spray or weathering, with condition and mass compared against a control; conductivity - current or heat passed through a standardised sample and measured. State that industrial tests are standardised and results are compared against published data so materials can be selected consistently.

Two classification nuances earn easy marks. First, why alloy: alloying deliberately mixes metals to enhance properties - duralumin (aluminium plus copper) trades a little corrosion resistance for a large gain in tensile strength, which is why it was developed for aircraft structures; brass (copper plus zinc) gives low friction for fittings; pewter is a low-temperature casting alloy. Second, ferrous selection logic: ferrous metals rust, so low carbon steel is chosen where cost and weldability matter more than corrosion resistance (frames, structures - then protected by coating or galvanising), while stainless steel is chosen where hygiene or appearance under water matters more than cost.

On testing data, be ready to analyse rather than describe: destructive tests (tensile, impact) sacrifice specimens to find limits, so they are used on samples, not finished products; results are compared against a control specimen and against published standard data; multiple repeats are averaged to eliminate outliers, and the comparison informs which material is selected. A 3-mark analysis point: the material whose stress-strain curve shows the higher yield point before fracture is selected for the load-bearing application.

Performance characteristics by material category (3.1.2)

This is the highest-yield section of Paper 1. Learn the characteristic list and the application pairings - questions routinely give an application and ask which material and why.

Know stock forms per category because selection questions often name the form: woods as rough sawn, PSE, PAR, mouldings and manufactured boards; metals as sheet, plate, bar (flat, round, square, hexagonal), tube (round, square, rectangular) and structural sections (H beam, I beam, tee, channel, angle); polymers as sheet, film, granules, rod and other extruded forms, foam, powder. Two economic points are repeatedly examined: efficient 2D net design reduces waste when constructing containers, and material selection should allow recyclability, biodegradability and stability in use - a corrugated card net nests on the sheet with minimal off-cut waste, and PLA film degrades under UV, water or soil enzymes where LDPE does not.

Four further 3.1.2 sub-sections sit inside this topic and appear in Paper 1 regularly. Elastomers (natural rubber, polybutadiene, neoprene, silicone, TPE): suitability rests on the ability to be stretched and then return to the original shape, plus texture, self finishing and non-toxicity - used to enhance products such as over-moulded grips for improved ergonomics. Biodegradable polymers (corn starch polymers, Potatopak, biopol bio-batch additive, PLA, PHA, water-soluble Lactel and ecofilm): they can be moulded into 3D products or film and degrade under UV rays, water or enzymes present in soil - quote the degradation trigger, not just biodegradable.

Composites (CFRP, GRP, tungsten carbide, aluminium composite board, reinforced concrete, fibre cement, glulam): materials are combined so properties are enhanced - mouldable into varied 3D forms, higher strength-to-weight, and for some uses easier to manufacture against traditional materials. Concrete is strong in compression but weak in tension, so steel reinforcement is added - the classic enhanced-property explanation. Smart materials (Nitinol SMA, thermochromic, phosphorescent and photochromic pigments, electroluminescent wire, piezo electric material): defined by the stimulus they respond to - change in temperature (thermochromic, SMA), light levels (photochromic, phosphorescent) or pressure/force (piezo). Finally modern materials (Kevlar, precious metal clay, high density modelling foam, polymorph) - recently developed materials whose suitability must be explained for given applications, e.g. Kevlar where high impact and cut resistance at low weight is needed.

Enhancement of materials (3.1.3)

Enhancement questions ask you to describe the method and explain its suitability for a specific product. Learn one enhancement per category plus the mechanism behind it.

  • Polymer enhancement - additives. UV stabilisers prolong the life of polymers used outdoors (garden furniture becomes brittle in sunlight without them); bio-batch additives such as biopol encourage biodegradability in carrier bags and food packaging so they break down after disposal. Pigments added at the moulding stage can also count as enhancement.
  • Wood enhancement - resins, lamination and preservatives. Combining natural timber with resins and laminating (glulam, marine plywood) increases strength and dimensional stability - laminated sections counteract warping and shrinkage because opposing grain directions are glued together. Pressure treating with chemical preservatives and applying oils or varnishes extends outdoor service life.
  • Metal enhancement - heat treatment. Case hardening: low carbon steel is carburised then heat treated, giving a hard, wear-resistant outer skin while the core stays tough - ideal for gear wheels and cam followers subject to surface wear plus shock loading. Hardening and tempering: medium/high carbon steel is heated to critical temperature and quenched (very hard but brittle), then reheated to around 200-300 degrees C and cooled to remove brittleness while retaining hardness - used for chisels, lathe tools and springs.

Composites and elastomers can also be framed as enhancement routes: CFRP and GRP mould a high strength-to-weight material into complex 3D forms impossible in plain polymer, and over-moulding a TPE grip onto a rigid substrate enhances ergonomics - a favourite context in Paper 1 extended questions. Tie each method back to the six selection factors; the describe command word earns the method, explain suitability earns the application link.

Exam focus: extended justification answers (AO4)

AQA 7552 Paper 1 (2h30m, 120 marks, 30% of A-level) uses a mixture of short answer and extended response. The extended material questions are where A* candidates separate themselves. Structure every justification in three layers:

  1. Name plus property. Name the specific material and the specific property - polypropylene has excellent fatigue resistance, not plastic is strong.
  2. Property to function link. State what the property does in this product: the fatigue resistance allows an integral living hinge to flex thousands of times without failing.
  3. Context - cost, manufacture, disposal. Add the commercial layer: PP is cheap and injection moulds at high volume with zero finishing (self finishing), and is recyclable under polymer code 5, aiding disposal.

Where the question offers a comparison (why X rather than Y), score by contrasting one decisive property - choose stainless over low carbon steel for a kitchen sink because corrosion resistance matters more than cost where water is always present. Where a calculation appears (3.1.1 maths links), show working for quantities of material, areas and costs: area = length x width for sheet coverage, cost = quantity x price per unit, and percentages for waste allowances. Marks are lost through generic answers, through properties quoted without a function, and through forgetting the disposal/recycling angle - build all three layers into every practice answer.

Common examiner traps
  • Writing strong and durable with no named property or application link.
  • Confusing toughness (impact) with hardness (surface wear) - they frequently oppose each other.
  • Ignoring stock form: selection for a pressed lamp body implies sheet or extruded form, not granules.
  • Missing scale of production: injection moulding only pays off at volume, so a one-off justification should not rely on it.

Paper 1 runs 2h30m for 120 marks, so budget roughly one minute per mark plus checking time. Short-answer questions (1-3 marks) need one precise fact per mark - a named material, a named property, one feature of a test. The 9-mark extended questions reward structure and linkage, and typically appear as 'explain why material X is suitable for product Y' or as a comparison. Plan the three layers (property, function, context) in 30 seconds of bullet points before writing - the mark scheme descriptors reward developed links, and a planned answer avoids the generic-property trap that caps marks at 4-5. Where the question says 'justify', a comparison with a rejected alternative is expected; where it says 'explain', the property-to-function chain is enough, but adding cost or disposal still differentiates top answers.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: AQA 7552 Paper 1 (Technical principles) style - extended response: A manufacturer produces premium desk lamps with a pressed metal casing and an over-moulded carry handle. Explain why aluminium is a suitable material for the casing and Thermoplastic Elastomer (TPE) is suitable for the handle. [9 marks]
Mark-scheme logic: Mark-scheme logic (9 marks = three developed justifications, each linking property to function): (1) ALUMINIUM CASING - high thermal conductivity dissipates heat from the lamp head; low density keeps the lamp light and portable; forms a protective oxide layer so resists corrosion and can be anodised for a premium finish; malleable so it press-forms into accurate casing profiles; self finishing appearance suits a premium product. (2) TPE HANDLE - elasticity: stretches slightly then returns to shape, giving a firm, comfortable grip (ergonomics); thermal and electrical insulation makes the handle safe to touch near the lamp; self finishing and non-toxic; can be over-moulded directly onto the metal casing in one process. (3) COMMERCIAL LAYER - both materials suit batch/mass production: aluminium extrusion and pressing are fast and repeatable; TPE injection moulding or over-moulding eliminates separate fixing operations, reducing manufacture cost; both are recyclable at end of life. Full-mark answers name both materials, link at least three properties each to the product's function, and add cost/manufacture reasoning.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

State three mechanical properties and, for each, name a material with a high value of that property. [3 marks]
Answer: 1 mark per correctly matched pair, e.g. tensile strength - high speed steel or CFRP; hardness - hardened tool steel or tungsten carbide; malleability - copper or low carbon steel; toughness - HDPE or ABS. Accept any accurate pairing; property and material must both be correct to score.
Describe how a tensile test is carried out in industry and state what data it produces. [4 marks]
Answer: A standardised specimen is clamped in the jaws of a tensile testing machine (1); an increasing tensile load is applied until the specimen fractures (1); extension is measured against load to produce a stress-strain graph (1); the graph yields the yield point, ultimate tensile strength and stiffness, which are compared against published data for material selection (1).
Explain why corrugated card is suitable for packaging a flat-screen television, but MDF is more suitable for a domestic speaker cabinet. [6 marks]
Answer: Corrugated card: the fluted structure gives high impact resistance and cushioning relative to its weight (1), protecting the screen in transit (1); it is lightweight so reduces transport costs, is cheap for single-use packaging and is recyclable/biodegradable (1). MDF: it is a dense, rigid, dimensionally stable panel that does not warp, so the cabinet will not flex or resonate (1); its uniform, grain-free surface machines cleanly and takes veneer or paint to a high quality finish (1); it is cost-effective for batch production of flat-pack cabinets (1).
Explain how case hardening improves the performance of a low carbon steel gear wheel. [4 marks]
Answer: Low carbon steel cannot be through-hardened effectively, so the gear would wear quickly on its teeth (1). In case hardening the surface is carburised (carbon added) and then heat treated (1), producing a hard, wear-resistant outer skin that resists abrasion where the teeth mesh (1); the core remains tough, so the gear absorbs shock loads without fracturing (1).
A packaging designer must choose between bleached card and moulded paper pulp for a premium confectionery box. Justify the selection of bleached card. [6 marks]
Answer: Bleached card has a smooth, bright white surface with excellent print quality, essential for premium graphics and branding (1); it scores, folds and glues accurately into a net, so it forms a stiff, rigid box at high speed (1); it can take foil blocking, embossing or laminating to further enhance shelf appeal (1); its stiffness protects the contents in transit (1); commercially it suits batch/mass production of identical printed nets at low unit cost (1); it is widely recyclable, supporting responsible disposal - although pulp would outperform it on biodegradability, print quality is the decisive factor for a premium product (1).
