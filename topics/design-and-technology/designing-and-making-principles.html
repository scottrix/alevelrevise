Designing and Making Principles

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA

A* A-level Product Design revision for AQA 7552 Paper 2: iterative and user-centred design, anthropometrics, design movements and designers, prototype development, accuracy and evaluation (3.2.1-3.2.7) - with 12-mark model answers.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain iterative, user-centred design approaches and how primary and secondary research (market research, interviews, focus groups, product analysis) informs design decisions (AQA 7552 3.2.1)
  • Use anthropometric data and percentiles, and ergonomic data, to justify product dimensions and user interaction (AQA 7552 3.2.1)
  • Discuss key design movements - Arts and Crafts, Art Deco, Modernism (Bauhaus), Post-modernism (Memphis) - and the work of Starck, Dyson, Calvert, Rams, Charles and Ray Eames and Marianne Brandt (AQA 7552 3.2.2)
  • Discuss how socio-economic change and major technological developments shape design, and describe the product life cycle (AQA 7552 3.2.3, 3.2.3.4)
  • Describe prototype development from design proposal to manufacture, and select appropriate tools, equipment and processes for the scale of production (AQA 7552 3.2.4, 3.2.6)
  • Explain how accuracy is achieved through jigs, fixtures, templates and testing, and how evaluation uses third-party feedback (AQA 7552 3.2.5, 3.2.7)

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Iterative design: A cyclic process of designing, modelling, testing and modifying in which each loop informs the next; never a single linear pass through the stages.
User-centred design: Designing from the needs, wants and values of the user, keeping them closely and regularly involved throughout the process.
Anthropometrics: The measurement of human body dimensions; percentiles (commonly 5th to 95th) are used to size products so most users are accommodated.
Ergonomics: The study of the interaction between user and product: comfort, posture, reach, grip and ease of use (human factors).
Design specification: A set of clear, unambiguous and measurable criteria the design must satisfy; treated as a live working document throughout the project.
Prototype: A full-size working model of the final design, used to test, evaluate and develop the proposal before commercial manufacture.
Jig: A guiding device that holds the workpiece or tool to ensure accuracy and repeatability, e.g. a drilling jig with hardened bushes.
Fixture: A holding device that secures the workpiece in a fixed position during a machining or assembly operation.
Template: A pattern or guide used to mark out or check a shape repeatedly, ensuring consistency across identical parts.
Product life cycle: The stages a product passes through: introduction, evolution/growth, maturity, decline and replacement.

Design methods, iteration and user-centred design (3.2.1, 3.2.4)

The specification is explicit that there is no single design process: good design always addresses needs, wants or values, and different approaches to user-centred design exist. What matters in the exam is that you can explain how investigation informs decisions. Primary data - market research, interviews, human factors observation, focus groups, product analysis and disassembly - is collected first-hand; secondary data comes from existing sources such as published reports, books and databases. Questions often ask why both are needed: primary data is specific to the actual user group and context, while secondary data is broader, cheaper and benchmarks against what already exists.

Anthropometrics and percentiles are examined almost every year. Designing for the 5th to 95th percentile accommodates about 90% of users: a doorway or chair seat sized for the 95th percentile (largest users) prevents exclusion of tall people, while reach distances based on the 5th percentile (smallest users) keep controls within grasp of most. Adjustable features (seat height, straps, headrests) widen the range further. Ergonomic data then shapes interaction - posture, grip, control placement, force required - and is justified against the user group: a child's scissors have smaller, softer grips sized to child hand anthropometrics.

The full design process stages (3.2.4) must be known for the NEA and for discussion questions: investigations and analysis; use of inspiration materials such as mood boards; ideas generation; illustration; development of a design specification; modelling; planning; evaluating and testing. In industrial and commercial contexts, iteration is cyclic and collaborative - design teams work with users, clients and expert practitioners, responding to feedback, and commercial design loops continuously between prototype testing and refinement because the cost of failure at volume makes early testing essential.

Be precise about what good design addresses (3.2.1): designing to meet needs, wants or values; investigations that inform the use of primary and secondary data; product analysis and evaluation; the development of a design proposal; the planning and manufacture of a prototype solution; and evaluation of the prototype to inform further development. The specification also expects you to discuss representation of data - how market research and testing data are presented (graphs, tables, charts) to inform design decisions and evaluate outcomes - a small but reliable question type.

A classic discussion question contrasts iterative design in school vs industry. In school/NEA the loop is student-driven and documented in a portfolio; in industry it is team-driven, collaborative and cyclic across design and manufacture - feedback comes from users, clients and expert practitioners, and because commercial failure at volume is catastrophic, extensive testing and refinement happen before launch. The mark is in the link: both are iterative, but industry adds collaborative working, cost of failure and cross-functional teams. Avoid claiming industry follows a fixed linear process - the specification explicitly rejects that.

Design theory: movements, designers and change (3.2.2, 3.2.3)

Learn each movement with its principles, dates and one signature product or designer - Paper 2 questions ask you to discuss how movements shaped product design, not just describe them.

MovementPrinciplesEvidence to quote
Arts and Crafts (c.1860-1900)Reaction against industrial mass production; honest use of materials, craftsmanship, simplicity, decoration from constructionWilliam Morris; hand-crafted furniture; influenced later designers to value material honesty
Art Deco (1920s-30s)Geometric ornament, luxury materials, streamlining, symmetryChrysler Building; Bakelite radios; streamlined 1930s product styling
Modernism (e.g. Bauhaus, 1919-1933)Form follows function; rejection of ornament; design for mass production; honest materials; unity of art and technologyMarianne Brandt's metalwork, Wagenfeld lamp; post-WW1 Bauhaus furniture designed for mass production
Post-modernism (e.g. Memphis, 1980s)Playful rebellion against modernist rules; colour, pattern, contradiction, decoration returnsMemphis Group (Sottsass) furniture; Philippe Starck's decorative yet functional products

Named designers (3.2.2): Philippe Starck - post-modern, playful commercial design (Juicy Salif lemon squeezer, styled over function); James Dyson - iterative engineering development (5,127 prototypes for the dual cyclone vacuum), function-driven innovation; Margaret Calvert - graphic/communication design (UK road signage with Jock Kinneir), clarity for the user; Dieter Rams - Braun, functionalist modernism, his ten principles of good design (innovative, useful, aesthetic, honest, unobtrusive and so on); Charles and Ray Eames - moulded plywood and plastic furniture, experimentation with materials for democratic mass production; Marianne Brandt - Bauhaus metalwork and lighting, designed for industrial manufacture.

Socio-economic influences (3.2.3): post-WW1 the Bauhaus developed furniture for mass production (cheap, functional, democratic); WW2 brought rationing and utility products (austere, material-efficient design under the Utility scheme); contemporary design reflects fashion and demand for mass-produced furniture plus decorative design. Major technology developments shaping design: microelectronics (product miniaturisation), new materials, new methods of manufacture and advancements in CAD/CAM. Finally, the product life cycle (3.2.3.4): introduction, evolution, growth, maturity, decline and replacement - quote how designers refine and re-develop products to extend maturity (e.g. iterative smartphone updates, the VW Beetle re-launched as the New Beetle).

Sharpen the designer profiles with facts you can deploy in 6-mark discuss answers. Dieter Rams (Braun): functionalist modernism; his ten principles - good design is innovative, makes a product useful, is aesthetic, makes a product understandable, is unobtrusive, is honest, is long-lasting, is thorough down to the last detail, is environmentally friendly, involves as little design as possible. James Dyson: 5,127 prototypes for the dual cyclone vacuum - the set-piece example of iterative development and design risk. Margaret Calvert (with Jock Kinneir): the UK road sign typeface and pictograms - clarity, legibility and user-centred graphic design for everyone. Charles and Ray Eames: experiments with moulded plywood and later plastic - material experimentation enabling well-designed furniture for mass production. Marianne Brandt: the only woman in the Bauhaus metal workshop - ashtrays and lighting designed geometrically for industrial manufacture. Philippe Starck: Juicy Salif lemon squeezer - post-modern styling and form over obvious function, a design-as-statement product that divided opinion.

For the socio-economic bullet, structure by era: post-WW1 Bauhaus furniture for mass production (functional, affordable, democratic); WW2 rationing and utility products (austere, efficient, standardised); contemporary fashion cycles and demand for mass-produced furniture alongside decorative post-modern design. Link each era to why design responded as it did - economics and materials scarcity shape style, which is exactly the analytical link the discuss questions reward.

Prototype development and manufacture (3.2.4, 3.2.6)

Prototype development questions ask you to discuss and demonstrate how a prototype grows from design proposals. Expect a narrative chain: design proposal → concept modelling (card, foam, styrofoam, 3D print) → testing against the specification → modification → part-models or technique practice for critical elements → working drawings and plans → final prototype manufacture. Every stage must show that knowledge of the user and specification is driving decisions - AQA states this knowledge should influence NEA development so students make high-quality products that meet the needs of identified users.

Selecting tools, equipment and processes (3.2.6) links the prototype to production reality. Key exam points: designs are developed from a single prototype into batch and mass produced products; the need for batch and mass manufacture changes the manufacturing process (one-off hand skills give way to jigs, fixtures, CNC and moulding); the most appropriate process must be selected to realise a design proposal; and safe working practices apply throughout - correct tools for the task, protection of self and others, and in commercial settings workforce training and national safety standards. A strong answer contrasts scales: a one-off birch plywood stool cut by hand and router becomes, at volume, a CNC-nested flat-pack product assembled with KD fittings - same design intent, different process chain chosen by scale.

Model with purpose: be ready to justify why a modelling technique suits its stage - card and foam for fast cheap form exploration, 3D printing for accurate functional parts, part-models to prove a single joint or mechanism before committing material to the full prototype. This justification habit is exactly what the NEA Criteria C and D reward, so practicing it in written answers directly lifts coursework marks.

Add the commercial chain to your prototype answers: single prototype → batch → mass production changes everything downstream. At batch, jigs and fixtures guarantee repeatability; at mass, processes switch to moulding, pressing and CNC with standardised bought-in components, and the design itself is modified to suit the process (draft angles for moulding, nesting for CNC, knock-down fittings for flat packing). The specification phrase is 'the effect on the manufacturing process brought about by the need for batch and mass manufacture' - be able to name three concrete effects.

On safe working (3.2.6): correct tools and equipment for the specific task; ensuring your own safety and that of others in the workshop; and in commercial settings, workforce training plus national safety standards. A 4-mark answer links the hazard to the control: rotating machinery (guards, extraction, eye protection), dust and fumes (LEV extraction, masks under COSHH), and manual handling (technique, aids). Health and safety reasoning also feeds NEA Criterion D evidence, so practicing this link in written answers lifts coursework too.

Accuracy, testing and evaluation (3.2.5, 3.2.7)

Accuracy in design and manufacture (3.2.7) is examined whatever the scale of production. Learn the mechanism chain: testing eliminates error; measuring aids - templates, jigs and fixtures - ensure consistency and reduce human error; datum points and geometry are used when setting out design drawings and creating templates. Batch and mass production amplify the value of jigs: a jig is made once but guarantees every identical part afterwards is accurate, so the cost per part of the jig falls as volume rises. Be able to give a concrete example - a drilling jig with hardened bushes positions holes identically in every shelf of a flat-pack run.

Critical analysis and evaluation (3.2.5) must cover both your own products and commercial products, leading to possible improvements or modifications of the original idea. Commercial products are required to undergo rigorous testing before becoming available for sale - be ready to name testing methods (user trials, endurance and safety testing, standards testing to BSI specifications). Third-party feedback is a favourite question: the views of other interested parties ensure the evaluation is objective and unbiased - the designer is biased towards their own ideas, and users, clients and expert practitioners spot issues the designer cannot see. Feedback then informs future modification and development, completing the iterative loop. Structure evaluation answers as: test → data → judgement against specification → justified modification. That four-step chain mirrors both the written mark scheme and the NEA Criterion E descriptors, so it earns marks in both places.

Flesh out commercial testing before sale (3.2.5): products are required to undergo rigorous testing before becoming commercially available - user trials with target users, endurance/fatigue testing beyond normal service conditions, safety testing to BSI standards, and batch sampling during production. Third-party feedback is examined because of objectivity: the views of other interested parties (users, clients, independent testers) ensure evaluation is objective and unbiased, informing future modification and development. A 6-mark answer names the parties, explains the bias they remove, and links the feedback loop to a concrete improvement.

For accuracy, add the measurement chain: datum points (a fixed reference edge or face from which all dimensions are taken) prevent cumulative error; geometry creates templates that reproduce curves identically; testing eliminates error before volume manufacture, when the cost of an error multiplies by the batch size. The exam link: an error caught at prototype stage costs one part; the same error at mass production costs thousands - which is why 3.2.7 insists accuracy matters whatever the scale, but the economics justify the jigs and testing at volume.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: AQA 7552 Paper 2 (Designing and making principles) style - extended response: A furniture start-up is developing a new dining chair using an iterative design process. Explain how user-centred design and iterative prototyping should be used to develop the chair from initial concept to finished prototype. [12 marks]
Mark-scheme logic: Mark-scheme logic (12 marks = four developed points, each linking method to outcome): (1) USER-CENTRED INVESTIGATION - primary research (questionnaires, interviews, focus groups, human factors observation) identifies needs, wants and values of the intended users; anthropometric data and percentiles (5th-95th) set seat height, width and back support so the chair fits most users; ergonomic data shapes comfort, posture and control of any adjusters. (2) ITERATIVE PROTOTYPING - concept models in card/foam allow cheap, fast exploration of form; each prototype is tested against the design specification and by users; documented modifications feed the next iteration - the cyclic loop continues until criteria are met (the spec is a live document). (3) DEVELOPMENT TO MANUFACTURE - part-models and technique practice prove joints and finishing before committing material; working drawings with datums guide accurate manufacture; materials and processes are justified against scale of production. (4) EVALUATION INFORMS DECISIONS - third-party feedback (users, clients, practitioners) provides objective, unbiased evaluation; results are used to modify the design, and commercial context (collaborative working, cyclic commercial design) keeps the chair viable for the market. Full-mark answers explain the methods (AO1), apply them to the chair (AO2) and evaluate how the loop improves the outcome (AO3).

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

State what is meant by an iterative design process. [2 marks]
Answer: A cyclic process of designing, modelling/prototyping, testing and modifying (1) in which the results of each loop inform the next, rather than following a single linear path (1).
Explain why anthropometric data is used with percentiles when sizing a school dining chair. [4 marks]
Answer: Anthropometric data gives measured human body dimensions (1); using the 5th to 95th percentiles accommodates about 90% of the user population (1); seat height and width set for the range prevent smaller and larger students being excluded (1); adjustable features such as seat height can widen the range further, and ergonomic data ensures comfort and posture for the age group (1).
Discuss how the Bauhaus influenced furniture design after World War One. [6 marks]
Answer: The Bauhaus (founded 1919) united art and technology, rejecting ornament in favour of form following function (1); post-WW1 it developed furniture explicitly for mass production, making good design affordable and democratic (1); standardised, honest materials such as tubular steel replaced carved decoration (e.g. Marianne Brandt's metalwork) (1); its rational, functional aesthetic defined modernism and still shapes flat-pack and office furniture today (1); however, hand-craft traditions (Arts and Crafts) were displaced, showing the movement's decisive shift from craft to industry (1); answers must discuss impact, not just describe - link principles to what changed in furniture manufacture (1).
Explain the difference between a jig and a fixture, and why both are used in batch production. [4 marks]
Answer: A jig guides the tool or workpiece so each operation is positioned identically, e.g. a drilling jig with bushes (1); a fixture only holds the workpiece securely in position during the operation (1); in batch production both ensure consistency of accuracy and repeatability across every identical part (1); they reduce human error and cost per part, since the aid is made once and then guarantees accuracy for the whole batch (1).
Explain why third-party feedback is important when testing and evaluating a new product before it is sold commercially. [6 marks]
Answer: The designer is biased towards their own ideas, so feedback from other interested parties makes the evaluation objective and unbiased (1); users reveal real-world issues with usability, comfort and ergonomics the designer cannot anticipate (1); clients confirm commercial requirements such as cost and branding (1); expert practitioners identify technical or safety flaws before rigorous pre-sale testing is complete (1); feedback informs future modification and development, closing the iterative loop and improving fitness for purpose (1); commercially, products are required to undergo rigorous testing before sale, and third-party evidence supports standards compliance and consumer confidence (1).
