The Large Data Set

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC/Eduqas, CCEA

The AQA and Edexcel large data sets - weather and vehicle data, sampling methods, cleaning data, outliers and how the exam tests them - A-Level Maths statistics (Edexcel 9MA0 Topics 11-12, AQA 7357 K-L) revised with worked examples and mark-scheme logic.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Know the prescribed large data set for your board - its context, variables and structure - and use it to interpret statistical results in context (AO2/AO3) - Edexcel 9MA0 Topics 11-12, AQA 7357 K1-L1
  • Select and justify an appropriate sampling method, and criticise unsuitable methods for bias (AO2/AO3) - Edexcel 9MA0 Topic 11, AQA 7357 K1-K2
  • Take a random, systematic, stratified, quota or opportunity sample from a large data set using a sampling frame (AO1) - Edexcel 9MA0 Topic 11, AQA 7357 K2
  • Clean data: handle n/a entries, trace values and outliers using the 1.5 x IQR rule, justifying decisions (AO2) - Edexcel 9MA0 Topic 12, AQA 7357 L1
  • Compare two distributions from the data set in context, commenting on location, dispersion and skewness (AO2) - Edexcel 9MA0 Topic 12, AQA 7357 L1-L2
  • Calculate summary statistics from data set samples and interpret them in the context of the data (AO1/AO2)

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Large data set: The prescribed data set each board expects students to have explored during the course; exam questions assume familiarity with its context and variables.
Census: Measuring every member of the population; accurate but expensive, slow and hard to carry out for large populations.
Sample: A subset of the population measured to draw conclusions about the whole; cheaper and quicker, but subject to sampling variation and bias.
Sampling frame: A numbered list of all members of the population from which a sample is drawn, such as the days of a year or a list of vehicles.
Simple random sample: Every member has an equal chance of selection, chosen with random numbers; unbiased but needs a full sampling frame.
Systematic sample: Selecting every kth member of an ordered list after a random start, with k = N/n; simple and quick but risks periodicity in the list.
Stratified sample: The population is split into strata differing in a relevant way, each sampled in proportion to its size: n_h = (N_h/N) x n.
Opportunity (convenience) sample: Taking whichever members are available; quick but prone to bias towards whichever members are easiest to reach.
Outlier: A value far from the rest of the data; the standard test flags values beyond 1.5 x IQR outside the quartiles.
Trace (tr): In the Edexcel weather data set, a daily total rainfall recorded as tr means a trace: rainfall below 0.05 mm.

📌 Key Points

Edexcel LDS: weather data from 8 stations - 5 UK (Heathrow, Hurn, Leeming, Leuchars, Camborne) and 3 overseas (Beijing, Jacksonville, Perth) - for 1987 (May to October) and 2015 (full year).
Edexcel variables: daily mean temperature, total rainfall, total sunshine, mean windspeed and direction (Beaufort), mean cloud cover (oktas), mean pressure (hPa), mean gust.
AQA LDS: vehicles registered in Great Britain - make, engine size, fuel type, engine power and CO2 emissions among the recorded variables.
Data cleaning: tr means trace rainfall (below 0.05 mm); n/a means not available; outliers flagged by the 1.5 x IQR rule.
Sampling: census (all), simple random (random numbers), systematic (every kth after a random start), stratified (proportional), quota and opportunity (non-random, prone to bias).

What the large data set is - and why it exists

Each board prescribes a large data set (LDS) that students are expected to have explored during the course (Edexcel 9MA0 Topics 11-12; AQA 7357 K1-L1; OCR A, WJEC/Eduqas and CCEA test the same sampling and data-handling skills but without a shared national data set, so their questions supply the extract you need). The exam does not print the whole data set: questions quote extracts, summary statistics or small samples taken from it, and assume you know the context - what the variables mean, their units, and how the data is structured. That familiarity is what LDS questions actually test: a student who has handled the data recognises instantly that rainfall is measured in mm, windspeed in knots on the Beaufort scale, and cloud cover in oktas (eighths of sky).

LDS questions are marked on interpretation in context as much as on calculation (AO2/AO3). An answer that says the mean is bigger earns nothing; an answer that says Heathrow's daily mean temperature is higher than Hurn's on this sample, so the sample suggests Heathrow is warmer, earns the mark. The calculation is the easy half - the written interpretation is the differentiator, and it is the reason to spend revision time with the actual data rather than reading about it.

Practical revision: open the data set in a spreadsheet once, compute means, standard deviations and quartiles for a couple of stations, draw a histogram, and try a systematic sample. The exam's LDS questions are formulaic once you have done this once or twice.

The Edexcel weather data set

The Edexcel LDS is weather data from 8 weather stations: 5 in the UK - Heathrow, Hurn, Leeming, Leuchars and Camborne - and 3 overseas - Beijing, Jacksonville and Perth. Perth sits in the southern hemisphere, so its seasons are reversed - a favourite comparison question. The data covers two years: 1987 (May to October only) and 2015 (the full calendar year). The unequal coverage matters: comparing a full 1987 year with a full 2015 year is impossible from the data, and comparing May-October 1987 with the whole of 2015 is misleading because the seasons differ.

The variables: daily mean temperature (degrees C), daily total rainfall (mm), daily total sunshine (hours), daily mean windspeed and wind direction (knots, with Beaufort scale categories), daily mean cloud cover (oktas), daily mean pressure (hPa) and daily mean gust (knots). Two data-cleaning features are examinable: cells marked n/a where a reading was not taken, and rainfall recorded as tr, meaning a trace below 0.05 mm - treated as zero in calculations, or stated as a trace when interpreting. Exam questions ask how you would clean the data before calculating statistics, and the expected answers name these features.

Worked example: sampling interval for a systematic sample

Describe how to take a systematic sample of 31 days from the 365 days of 2015 Heathrow data.

Line 1: Order the days 1 to 365; the sampling interval is k = 365/31 = 11.77, so take every 12th day (M1: interval from N/n).

Line 2: Choose a random start between 1 and 12 using random numbers (M1: random start).

Line 3: Select days start, start + 12, start + 24, ..., giving 31 days (start + 360 <= 365) (A1: sample described completely).

Sampling variation is the key idea behind LDS interpretation: two different samples from the same station give different means, so conclusions from a sample are about the sample unless stated otherwise - a subtle point that separates top answers.

The AQA vehicles data set and other boards

The AQA LDS covers vehicles registered in Great Britain: among the recorded variables are make, engine size (litres), fuel type, engine power and CO2 emissions (g/km). Exam questions build on this context: comparing CO2 emissions between petrol and diesel cars on a sample, calculating mean engine size, drawing histograms of emissions, and discussing how the sample was taken. The same statistical machinery applies as for weather data - the context changes, the technique does not.

OCR A, WJEC/Eduqas and CCEA do not prescribe a single shared national data set in the same way, so their statistics questions supply the data extract or summary statistics you need within the question. The transferable skills are identical: choosing and justifying a sampling method, cleaning data, computing and comparing summary statistics, and interpreting in context. Revising with the AQA or Edexcel data therefore prepares you for all boards - the question formats differ, the skills do not.

What all boards share is the statistical problem-solving cycle: specify the problem and plan, collect (sample) data, process and represent it, and interpret and discuss the results. LDS questions walk through this cycle, and the interpretation stage is where context knowledge from the data set earns its marks.

A comparison question has the same shape whichever data set it uses: given the mean and standard deviation of CO2 emissions for petrol cars and for diesel cars on a sample, comment on which type emits more on average (location, in context) and which is more variable (dispersion, in context), then note that the conclusion describes the sample, not all vehicles. Histogram questions may ask why frequency density is needed when class widths differ, and cleaning questions may ask how impossible values (a negative engine size, a car with no recorded fuel type) should be handled before calculating statistics. Practising each archetype once or twice against real data is the whole preparation this topic needs.

Sampling methods: choosing and justifying

A census measures every member of the population; a sample measures a subset. A census is fully accurate but expensive, slow, and impractical for large or destructive testing; a sample is cheaper and faster but subject to sampling variation and bias. Exam questions ask for one advantage and one disadvantage of each - answer in context, not in the abstract.

MethodHowAdvantageDisadvantage
Simple randomRandom numbers on a numbered sampling frameFree of bias; equal chance for allNeeds a full frame; can miss subgroups
SystematicEvery kth member after a random start, k = N/nSimple and quickNeeds ordered frame; periodicity risk
StratifiedEach stratum sampled proportionally: n_h = (N_h/N) x nRepresents subgroups proportionallyNeeds strata identified in the frame
QuotaInterviewer fills quotas of each typeFast; no frame neededNon-random; interviewer bias
OpportunityTake whoever is availableVery quick and cheapStrongly biased to the reachable

The stratified calculation is the one examinable formula: for a stratum of size N_h in a population of N with total sample size n, the stratum sample is n_h = (N_h/N) x n. Round carefully - half-up or state the rounding, and check the strata sum to n.

Worked example: stratified sample

A school of 800 students has 300 in Year 12 and 500 in Year 13. A researcher takes a stratified sample of 40 students. How many from each year?

Line 1: Year 12: n_h = (300/800) x 40 = 15 (M1: proportional formula).

Line 2: Year 13: (500/800) x 40 = 25 (A1).

Line 3: Check: 15 + 25 = 40 (A1: strata sum to the sample size).

Justification answers follow a pattern: name the method, state why it suits the context (proportional representation, no frame available, speed), and state the bias risk if it is non-random.

How LDS questions appear in exams - and the traps

LDS questions fall into recurring archetypes, and each has a fixed answer shape. (1) Compare two distributions: given two means and standard deviations, comment on location (which is bigger, in context), dispersion (which is more variable, in context) and, if asked, skewness - all in sentences naming the variable and the stations or groups. (2) Describe or criticise a sampling method: name the method, state the bias risk, and suggest the fix. (3) Data cleaning: explain what tr and n/a mean and how they are treated, and test outliers with the 1.5 x IQR rule. (4) Interpret statistics in context: connect a calculated value to the data's meaning.

Worked example: outlier test

A 31-day sample of daily total sunshine from Hurn has Q1 = 1.1 h, median = 3.4 h and Q3 = 6.2 h. Test whether 14.9 h is an outlier.

Line 1: IQR = 6.2 - 1.1 = 5.1 (M1).

Line 2: Upper fence: Q3 + 1.5 x IQR = 6.2 + 7.65 = 13.85; lower fence: 1.1 - 7.65 = -6.55 (M1: 1.5 x IQR rule).

Line 3: 14.9 > 13.85, so 14.9 is an outlier by the 1.5 x IQR rule (A1: conclusion).

Line 4: A genuinely sunny day is plausible weather, so it should be kept unless there is evidence of a recording error (A1: interpretation in context).

The interpretation discipline: an outlier is a statistical fact (outside the fences) but discarding it is a judgement - sunny days are real, so keep them; impossible values (negative rainfall, a gust exceeding the recorded windspeed by an implausible margin) suggest errors, so investigate. Exam questions award the justification mark for exactly this reasoning.

Worked example: misleading comparison

A student compares the mean of all 1987 temperatures with the mean of all 2015 temperatures and concludes the climate cooled. Why is this misleading?

Line 1: 1987 covers only May-October, while 2015 covers the full year - the seasons differ, so the comparison is not like-for-like (B1).

Line 2: One year is weather, not climate - a single year's mean does not establish a trend (B1).

Line 3: A fairer comparison restricts both years to the same months (e.g. May-October) (B1).

Finally, know the tool expectations: summary statistics and quartiles come from your calculator's statistics mode, histograms need frequency density for unequal class widths, and every written conclusion names the context - station, variable, units. These habits are the difference between method marks earned and interpretation marks dropped.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: The Edexcel large data set contains daily mean temperature data for Heathrow in 1987 and 2015. A student compares the mean of all the 1987 data with the mean of all the 2015 data and concludes that the climate at Heathrow cooled between the two years. Give two reasons why this comparison may be misleading, and suggest how a fairer comparison could be made. (Edexcel 9MA0-style, 4 marks)
Mark-scheme logic: The 1987 data covers only May to October, while the 2015 data covers the full calendar year, so the two means are not based on the same months - the seasonal structure differs (B1: unlike-for-like comparison identified). A single year is weather rather than climate, so one year's mean against another's does not establish a trend (B1: interpretation). A fairer comparison restricts both years to the same months - for example comparing May to October 1987 with May to October 2015 - or takes samples of the same size in the same way from both years (B1 B1: fairer comparison described).

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Give one advantage and one disadvantage of a census compared with a sample.
Answer: Advantage: a census measures every member, so it gives a completely accurate picture with no sampling variation (B1). Disadvantage: it is expensive and time-consuming for large populations, and impossible when testing is destructive (B1: in context).
Describe how to take a systematic sample of 31 days from the 365 days of 2015 Heathrow data.
Answer: Order the days 1-365; the interval is k = 365/31 = 11.77, so take every 12th day (M1). Choose a random start between 1 and 12 using random numbers (B1). Select the start day, then every 12th day after it, giving 31 days since start + 360 <= 365 (A1: complete description).
A school of 800 students has 300 in Year 12 and 500 in Year 13. A stratified sample of 40 students is taken. How many from each year group?
Answer: Year 12: (300/800) x 40 = 15 (M1 A1). Year 13: (500/800) x 40 = 25 (A1). Check: 15 + 25 = 40 (B1: strata sum to n).
A 31-day sample of daily total sunshine has Q1 = 1.1 h, median = 3.4 h and Q3 = 6.2 h. Use the 1.5 x IQR rule to test whether 14.9 h is an outlier.
Answer: IQR = 6.2 - 1.1 = 5.1 (M1). Upper fence = Q3 + 1.5 x IQR = 6.2 + 7.65 = 13.85 (M1). 14.9 > 13.85, so it is an outlier by the rule (A1). However, a very sunny day is plausible weather, so it should be kept unless there is evidence of a recording error (B1: interpretation).
In the Edexcel weather data, what does a daily total rainfall recorded as tr mean, and how should it be treated when calculating statistics?
Answer: tr means a trace: rainfall below 0.05 mm (B1). When calculating, it is treated as zero (or noted as a trace), since no measurable rainfall fell (B1: treatment stated).
A sample of 30 days gives Heathrow a mean daily temperature of 11.2 degrees C with standard deviation 5.6, and Hurn a mean of 10.4 degrees C with standard deviation 4.9. Compare the two distributions.
Answer: Location: Heathrow's mean is higher, so on this sample Heathrow was warmer on average (B1: in context). Dispersion: Heathrow's standard deviation is larger, so its temperatures were more variable (B1: in context). Both statements describe the sample - a different sample could give different values (B1: sampling variation acknowledged).
