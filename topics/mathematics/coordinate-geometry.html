Coordinate Geometry

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC/Eduqas, CCEA

Straight lines, gradients, parallel and perpendicular conditions, circle equations, tangents, chord lengths and loci - A-Level pure maths coordinate geometry (Edexcel 9MA0 Topic 3, AQA 7357 Section C) revised with worked examples and mark-scheme logic.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Find the equation of a line from a gradient and point or from two points, in y = mx + c or ax + by + c = 0 form (AO1) - Edexcel 9MA0 3.1, AQA 7357 C1
  • Calculate distances and midpoints, and use the parallel (m1 = m2) and perpendicular (m1 m2 = -1) conditions (AO1/AO2) - Edexcel 9MA0 3.1, AQA 7357 C1
  • Find the equation of a circle from its centre and radius, and recover centre and radius by completing the square from the expanded form (AO1/AO2) - Edexcel 9MA0 3.6, AQA 7357 C2
  • Use circle properties: the radius is perpendicular to the tangent, and the perpendicular bisector of a chord passes through the centre (AO2) - Edexcel 9MA0 3.6, AQA 7357 C2
  • Find intersections of lines with lines and lines with circles, interpreting a zero discriminant as tangency and deriving exact chord lengths (AO2) - Edexcel 9MA0 3.1/3.6
  • Determine loci of points satisfying distance conditions and prove geometric facts using gradients and distances (AO2/AO3)

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Gradient: The steepness of a line: m = (y2 - y1)/(x2 - x1), the change in y per unit change in x.
Midpoint: The point halfway along a segment: M = ((x1 + x2)/2, (y1 + y2)/2).
Distance formula: The distance between (x1, y1) and (x2, y2) is sqrt((x2 - x1)^2 + (y2 - y1)^2), from Pythagoras.
Perpendicular bisector: The line through the midpoint of a segment at right angles to it; every point on it is equidistant from the segment's endpoints, and for a chord it passes through the circle's centre.
Equation of a circle: The circle with centre (a, b) and radius r has equation (x - a)^2 + (y - b)^2 = r^2.
Tangent: A line touching a circle or curve at exactly one point; it is perpendicular to the radius at the point of contact.
Chord: A line segment joining two points on a circle; the perpendicular bisector of any chord passes through the centre.
Locus: The set of points satisfying a geometric condition; equidistance from a point and a line gives a parabola, from two points a perpendicular bisector.

📌 Key Points

Gradient m = (y2 - y1)/(x2 - x1); line through (x1, y1): y - y1 = m(x - x1).
Distance d = sqrt((x2 - x1)^2 + (y2 - y1)^2); midpoint M = ((x1 + x2)/2, (y1 + y2)/2).
Parallel lines have equal gradients; perpendicular lines have gradients multiplying to -1 (m1 x m2 = -1).
Circle: (x - a)^2 + (y - b)^2 = r^2 with centre (a, b), radius r; radius is perpendicular to the tangent; the perpendicular bisector of a chord passes through the centre.
Line-circle intersection: substitute and examine the resulting quadratic - discriminant zero means tangency; chord length follows from the two roots.

Lines: gradient, equations and the perpendicular condition

Straight-line work underpins half the pure paper (Edexcel 9MA0 3.1; AQA 7357 C1; OCR A H640 4.01; WJEC/Eduqas and CCEA AS Pure Mathematics). The gradient between two points is m = (y2 - y1)/(x2 - x1), and the equation of a line comes in two interchangeable forms: point-slope y - y1 = m(x - x1) and slope-intercept y = mx + c. Exam answers usually prefer ax + by + c = 0 form when a question says give your answer in the form ax + by + c = 0 - multiply through and collect every term on one side.

Two conditions do the geometric work: parallel lines have equal gradients (m1 = m2) and perpendicular lines have gradients whose product is -1 (m1 x m2 = -1), so a perpendicular gradient is the negative reciprocal. For a line 2x + 3y = 6, rewrite as y = -2x/3 + 2 to read the gradient -2/3; any perpendicular line has gradient 3/2.

Worked example: perpendicular bisector

Find the equation of the perpendicular bisector of the line joining A(-1, 4) and B(5, -2).

Line 1: Midpoint of AB = ((-1 + 5)/2, (4 - 2)/2) = (2, 1) (M1).

Line 2: Gradient of AB = (-2 - 4)/(5 - (-1)) = -6/6 = -1 (M1).

Line 3: Perpendicular gradient = -1/(-1) = 1 (A1).

Line 4: Through (2, 1): y - 1 = 1(x - 2), so y = x - 1 (A1).

Check the answer: any point on y = x - 1 should be equidistant from A and B - testing (2, 1) gives distance sqrt(9 + 9) = sqrt(18) to both, confirming the bisector.

Circles: equation, centre and radius

The circle with centre (a, b) and radius r has equation (x - a)^2 + (y - b)^2 = r^2 (Edexcel 9MA0 3.6; AQA 7357 C2; OCR A H640 5.01; CCEA AS1/A21). Two directions of travel: (1) given centre and radius, write the equation; (2) given the expanded form x^2 + y^2 + 2gx + 2fy + c = 0, complete the square in x and in y to recover the centre (-g, -f) and radius sqrt(g^2 + f^2 - c). Centre and radius questions award method marks for the completing-the-square lines, so show them.

A circle can be specified without its centre: as the circle on a given diameter, where the centre is the midpoint of the diameter's endpoints and the radius is half the distance between them. This construction appears constantly in exam questions.

Worked example: circle from a diameter

Find the equation of the circle with diameter endpoints A(1, 2) and B(5, 6), and find the tangent at A.

Line 1: Centre = midpoint of AB = ((1 + 5)/2, (2 + 6)/2) = (3, 4) (M1).

Line 2: Radius = half of AB: AB = sqrt((5 - 1)^2 + (6 - 2)^2) = sqrt(32), so r^2 = 32/4 = 8 (M1 A1).

Line 3: Circle: (x - 3)^2 + (y - 4)^2 = 8 (A1).

Line 4: Gradient of the radius from centre (3, 4) to A(1, 2): (2 - 4)/(1 - 3) = 1 (M1).

Line 5: Tangent gradient = -1 (perpendicular condition). Tangent at A: y - 2 = -1(x - 1), so y = -x + 3 (A1).

Note the efficient habit: keep r^2 as a value rather than computing r, since the equation only needs r^2. The angle in a semicircle is a right angle (Thales), which is why a diameter-endpoint question often asks to prove a triangle is right-angled.

Circle theorems and tangency

Three circle properties are examinable (Edexcel 9MA0 3.6; AQA 7357 C2; OCR A H640 5.01): (1) the tangent is perpendicular to the radius at the point of contact - the workhorse for tangent equations; (2) the perpendicular bisector of a chord passes through the centre - the workhorse for finding a centre from a chord; (3) the angle in a semicircle is 90 degrees. Tangency can also be established algebraically: substitute the line into the circle, and a repeated root (discriminant zero) proves the line touches at exactly one point.

Worked example: proving tangency algebraically

Show that the line 3x + 4y = 25 is a tangent to the circle x^2 + y^2 = 25, and find the point of contact.

Line 1: Substitute x = (25 - 4y)/3: ((25 - 4y)/3)^2 + y^2 = 25 (M1: substitution).

Line 2: Multiply by 9: (25 - 4y)^2 + 9y^2 = 225, so 625 - 200y + 16y^2 + 9y^2 = 225 (M1).

Line 3: 25y^2 - 200y + 400 = 0, so y^2 - 8y + 16 = 0, i.e. (y - 4)^2 = 0 (A1: repeated root).

Line 4: The discriminant is zero, so there is exactly one intersection point: the line is a tangent (A1: conclusion).

Line 5: Point of contact: y = 4, x = (25 - 16)/3 = 3, i.e. (3, 4) (A1).

Worked example: tangent at a point using the radius

Find the tangent to the circle (x - 2)^2 + (y + 1)^2 = 25 at the point (5, 3).

Line 1: Radius from centre (2, -1) to (5, 3): gradient (3 - (-1))/(5 - 2) = 4/3 (M1).

Line 2: Tangent gradient = -3/4 (A1: perpendicular).

Line 3: y - 3 = -(3/4)(x - 5), so 4y - 12 = -3x + 15, i.e. 3x + 4y = 27 (A1).

To find a centre from a chord: the centre lies on the chord's perpendicular bisector - combine with a second chord (or a point and the radius) to pin down the centre by intersecting two bisectors.

Intersections and exact chord lengths

Intersections are simultaneous equation problems. Line with line: solve the pair directly. Line with circle: substitute the line's expression for y (or x) into the circle to get a quadratic in one variable; its roots give the intersection points. Two outcomes matter: two distinct roots mean the line cuts the circle (a chord), a repeated root means tangency, and no real roots mean the line misses.

Chord lengths can be found without ever computing both points in full: if the quadratic in x has roots x1 and x2 and the line has gradient m, then the chord length is sqrt(1 + m^2) |x1 - x2| - but in practice, finding both points and using the distance formula is safer under exam pressure.

Worked example: exact chord length

The line y = 2x + 1 intersects the circle (x - 1)^2 + (y - 2)^2 = 10 at points A and B. Find the exact length AB.

Line 1: Substitute: (x - 1)^2 + (2x + 1 - 2)^2 = 10, so (x - 1)^2 + (2x - 1)^2 = 10 (M1).

Line 2: x^2 - 2x + 1 + 4x^2 - 4x + 1 = 10, giving 5x^2 - 6x - 8 = 0 (A1).

Line 3: Factorising: (5x + 4)(x - 2) = 0, so x = 2 or x = -4/5 (A1).

Line 4: Points: A(2, 5) and B(-4/5, -3/5) (M1: y = 2x + 1 for each).

Line 5: AB = sqrt((2 + 4/5)^2 + (5 + 3/5)^2) = sqrt((14/5)^2 + (28/5)^2) = sqrt(980/25) = sqrt(980)/5 = 14sqrt(5)/5 (A1: exact form).

Keep every step exact: fractions, surds and squared terms should stay unevaluated until the final line, since rounding early destroys exact answers and drops accuracy marks. When the quadratic in x does not factorise nicely, use the quadratic formula for the two roots and continue exactly as before - the chord length method does not depend on the roots being rational.

Loci, geometric proof and exam technique

A locus question translates a geometric condition into an equation. Equidistance from two points gives the perpendicular bisector; equidistance from a point and a line gives a parabola. The method: write the distance to each object, equate, square to remove the roots, and simplify.

Worked example: a parabola locus

Find the locus of points equidistant from the point (2, 3) and the line y = -1.

Line 1: Distance to the point: sqrt((x - 2)^2 + (y - 3)^2) (M1).

Line 2: Distance to the line y = -1: |y + 1| (M1).

Line 3: Equate and square: (x - 2)^2 + (y - 3)^2 = (y + 1)^2 (A1).

Line 4: Expand: (x - 2)^2 + y^2 - 6y + 9 = y^2 + 2y + 1, so (x - 2)^2 = 8y - 8 (A1).

Line 5: y = 1 + (x - 2)^2/8: a parabola with vertex (2, 1), focus (2, 3) and directrix y = -1 (A1).

Geometric proof problems use gradients and distances: to prove a triangle is right-angled, either show two gradients multiply to -1 or show the sides satisfy Pythagoras (a^2 + b^2 = c^2 with the longest side as c). For A(1, 1), B(2, 2) and C(4, 0): gradient of AB is 1, gradient of BC is (0 - 2)/(4 - 2) = -1, and 1 x (-1) = -1, so the angle at B is 90 degrees.

Exam technique: (1) write the completing-the-square or gradient lines explicitly - method marks live there; (2) keep r^2 unradiquified; (3) when a question says show that a line is a tangent, the expected proof is a zero discriminant, not a sketch; (4) use exact fractions throughout; (5) finish circle questions by checking the point lies on the circle (substitute into the equation). Board coverage: Edexcel 9MA0 Topic 3, AQA 7357 C1-C2, OCR A H640 4.01 and 5.01, WJEC/Eduqas AS Unit 1 geometry, CCEA AS1/A21 - identical techniques, differing wording.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: Find the equation of the circle with diameter endpoints A(1, 2) and B(5, 6), and find the equation of the tangent at A. (Edexcel 9MA0-style, 7 marks)
Mark-scheme logic: Centre = midpoint of AB = ((1 + 5)/2, (2 + 6)/2) = (3, 4) (M1). AB = sqrt((5 - 1)^2 + (6 - 2)^2) = sqrt(32), so r^2 = 32/4 = 8 (M1 A1). Circle: (x - 3)^2 + (y - 4)^2 = 8 (A1). Gradient of the radius from (3, 4) to A(1, 2) is (2 - 4)/(1 - 3) = 1 (M1), so the tangent gradient is -1 (perpendicular to radius). Tangent at A: y - 2 = -1(x - 1), i.e. y = -x + 3 (A1).

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Find the equation of the line through (-2, 3) perpendicular to 2x + 3y = 6, in the form ax + by + c = 0.
Answer: Rewrite 2x + 3y = 6 as y = -2x/3 + 2, so its gradient is -2/3 (M1). Perpendicular gradient: 3/2 (A1). Through (-2, 3): y - 3 = (3/2)(x + 2), so 2y - 6 = 3x + 6, i.e. 3x - 2y + 12 = 0 (A1).
Find the intersection of y = 2x - 1 and 3x + y = 7.
Answer: Substitute y = 2x - 1 into 3x + y = 7: 3x + 2x - 1 = 7, so 5x = 8 and x = 8/5 (M1 A1). Then y = 2(8/5) - 1 = 11/5 (A1). Intersection point (8/5, 11/5).
Find the tangent to the circle (x - 2)^2 + (y + 1)^2 = 25 at the point (5, 3).
Answer: Centre (2, -1). Radius gradient: (3 - (-1))/(5 - 2) = 4/3 (M1). Tangent gradient: -3/4 (A1). y - 3 = -(3/4)(x - 5), so 4y - 12 = -3x + 15, i.e. 3x + 4y = 27 (A1).
Points A(1, 1), B(2, 2) and C(4, 0). Show that triangle ABC is right-angled, stating the right angle.
Answer: Gradient of AB = (2 - 1)/(2 - 1) = 1 (M1). Gradient of BC = (0 - 2)/(4 - 2) = -1 (M1). Product: 1 x (-1) = -1, so AB is perpendicular to BC (A1). The right angle is at B (A1: stated). Alternative: AB^2 = 2, BC^2 = 8, AC^2 = 10, and 2 + 8 = 10 satisfies Pythagoras.
Show that the line 3x + 4y = 25 is a tangent to the circle x^2 + y^2 = 25 and find the point of contact.
Answer: Substitute x = (25 - 4y)/3 into the circle and multiply by 9: (25 - 4y)^2 + 9y^2 = 225 (M1). Expanding: 625 - 200y + 25y^2 = 225, so 25y^2 - 200y + 400 = 0, i.e. y^2 - 8y + 16 = 0, (y - 4)^2 = 0 (A1: repeated root). Discriminant zero, so the line touches at one point: tangent (A1). Point of contact: y = 4, x = (25 - 16)/3 = 3, i.e. (3, 4) (A1).
The line y = 2x + 1 intersects the circle (x - 1)^2 + (y - 2)^2 = 10 at A and B. Find the exact length AB.
Answer: Substitute: (x - 1)^2 + (2x - 1)^2 = 10, giving 5x^2 - 6x - 8 = 0 (M1 A1). Factorising: (5x + 4)(x - 2) = 0, so x = 2 or -4/5 (A1). Points A(2, 5), B(-4/5, -3/5) (M1). AB = sqrt((14/5)^2 + (28/5)^2) = sqrt(980/25) = 14sqrt(5)/5 (A1: exact).
