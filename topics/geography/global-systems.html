Global Systems and Governance

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC/Eduqas, CCEA

A* revision for global systems and global governance: globalisation dimensions and flows, trade and TNCs, IMF, World Bank and WTO power relations, and the Antarctic Treaty 1959 as the set-text global common, for AQA 7037 and other boards.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain globalisation as the widening, deepening and speeding-up of worldwide interconnectedness, describe its economic, political, social and environmental dimensions, and account for the factors accelerating it - transport, communications, finance, management/information systems, security, trade agreements and global marketing (AQA 3.2.1.1)
  • Describe and explain unequal flows of people, money, ideas and technology, and analyse the asymmetries (capital concentration, brain drain, terms of trade) that make globalisation winners and losers (AQA 3.2.1.1)
  • Describe global trade flows and patterns, explain commodity chains and market access, and assess how unequal power relations are reproduced through the IMF, World Bank, WTO, structural adjustment and trade blocs (AQA 3.2.1.2-3.2.1.3)
  • Explain the spatial organisation of TNCs - global production networks, outsourcing, FDI, transfer pricing - using Apple/Foxconn, and evaluate TNC power relative to states (AQA 3.2.1.2)
  • Explain the concept of the global commons and describe the Antarctic Treaty system (1959 Treaty, Madrid Protocol 1991, CCAMLR, MPAs), threats to Antarctica, and the role of NGOs and international law (AQA 3.2.1.4)
  • Evaluate the effectiveness of global governance of Antarctica and discuss sovereignty versus interdependence in a globalised world (AQA 3.2.1.4)

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Globalisation: The widening, deepening and speeding-up of worldwide interconnectedness: the process by which decisions, events and activities in one region come to have significant consequences for people and places elsewhere, across economic, political, social and environmental dimensions.
Glocalisation: The adaptation of global products, brands or services to local markets and cultures - global reach with local tailoring (McDonald's vegetarian McAloo Tikki in India; Coca-Cola's localised campaigns).
Global commons: Resource domains that lie outside national jurisdiction and are shared by all - the high seas, the atmosphere, Antarctica and outer space; no state can exclude others from them, and overuse by some diminishes them for all.
TNC (transnational corporation): A firm that owns or controls production or service facilities in more than one country, coordinating a global production network - locating design, manufacture, assembly and marketing in different places to maximise profit (Apple, Foxconn, Shell).
FDI (foreign direct investment): Investment by a firm or government in productive assets (factories, offices, land, mergers) in another country, giving a lasting interest and control; the main channel through which TNCs transfer capital and technology (global FDI roughly $1.3 trillion in 2023).
Structural adjustment: IMF/World Bank conditionality attached to loans: privatisation of state assets, trade liberalisation, subsidy removal, currency devaluation and public-spending cuts - criticised for deepening recessions and opening economies to foreign competition (Jamaica from 1977; Greece 2010-15).
Transfer pricing: The pricing of transactions between branches of the same TNC in different countries, used to shift taxable profits to low-tax jurisdictions and minimise the tax paid where profits are actually earned (Apple ordered in 2016 to repay ~13 billion euros to Ireland).
Antarctic Treaty (1959): The agreement signed on 1 December 1959 by the 12 states active in the International Geophysical Year, in force since 1961: Antarctica used for peaceful purposes only, territorial claims frozen (Article IV), military activity and nuclear explosions banned, freedom of scientific investigation guaranteed, decisions taken by consensus of consultative parties.

Globalisation: dimensions, flows, drivers and unequal power (AQA 3.2.1.1)

Definition and dimensions: globalisation is the widening, deepening and speeding-up of worldwide interconnectedness - the process by which decisions and events in one place increasingly shape lives and livelihoods elsewhere. It operates across four interlocking dimensions: economic (the growth of TNCs, FDI and global production networks; 24-hour financial markets), political (the growth of IGOs and trading blocs; the diffusion of liberal-democratic norms), social and cultural (migration and diasporas, global media and consumer culture, English as a lingua franca) and environmental (shared atmosphere and oceans; cross-border problems such as climate change, ozone depletion and plastic waste). Synoptic framing: the dimensions are mutually reinforcing - cheap communications enable offshoring, offshoring generates migration flows, migration spreads cultural traits - so high-bandwidth answers chain the dimensions rather than listing them. Historical depth earns AO1: earlier waves of globalisation existed (the Silk Road; colonial trade empires), but the current phase, beginning around 1950 and accelerating sharply after 1990 (the end of the Cold War, China's open-door policy and its WTO accession in 2001), is unprecedented in speed, scale and reach because of the transport, communications and financial revolutions set out below.

Global flows and their asymmetries: globalisation is made visible by flows of capital, labour, products and services, information and ideas - and the flows are deeply unequal.

The asymmetries: capital and technology are concentrated in a triad of North America, Europe and East Asia; many LIDCs export low-value primary commodities (fuel, ores, coffee, cotton) while importing high-value manufactures, so their terms of trade deteriorate; and the poorest states lose skilled workers - a large share of doctors and nurses trained in sub-Saharan Africa work in ACs - producing brain drain for the origin and brain gain for the destination, only partly offset by remittances and diaspora investment. Flows of services are also unequal but new: outsourcing of back-office and call-centre work to India (Bengalore and Hyderabad; the Indian IT/business-process sector employs roughly 5 million) exploits time-zone arbitrage, while medical tourism and digital work redistribute income in the opposite direction - first-class AO2 evidence that flows are multi-directional and changing.

Accelerating factors: the current phase was unlocked by a cluster of interdependent factors that AQA 3.2.1.1 names explicitly. Transport: containerisation from Malcom McLean's 1956 voyage - standardised 20-foot TEUs collapsed loading costs and port time, slashing the cost of moving goods across oceans; modern vessels of 18,000+ TEU (Maersk's Triple-E class) exploit economies of scale; air freight carries high-value, time-sensitive goods (electronics, flowers, pharmaceuticals). Communications: fibre-optic cables carry around 99% of intercontinental data; mobile platforms enable instant transactions and banking (M-Pesa in Kenya), and social media marketing reaches billions at negligible marginal cost. Finance: deregulation such as London's 1986 'Big Bang', computerised 24-hour trading across time zones and global credit markets. Management and information systems: just-in-time supply chains (the Toyota model) with RFID and satellite tracking allow components to cross borders several times before final assembly. Security: post-9/11 securitisation shapes the flows of people, goods and data (border screening, export controls on strategic technology). Trade agreements: successive WTO rounds plus regional blocs cut tariffs and standardise rules. Global marketing: global brands adapt to local markets - glocalisation: McDonald's sells the vegetarian McAloo Tikki in India, Coca-Cola localises campaigns and even product names, Apple maintains a uniform product identity while tailoring advertising to local cultures.

Case detail: Bangladesh and the global garment trade

Bangladesh shows an EME embedded in global supply chains: ready-made garments are roughly 84% of export earnings (about $47 billion in 2022-23), supplied to brands such as H&M, Primark and Zara on the strength of among the world's lowest wage costs (the minimum wage was raised to about $113 a month in 2023 only after mass strikes). The Rana Plaza factory collapse (24 April 2013) killed 1,134 workers, exposing the human cost of cost-driven outsourcing and prompting the Accord on Fire and Building Safety, signed by more than 200 brands. Assessment points: participation in global flows has delivered growth of around 6% a year, mass female employment and rising human development, yet the country remains dependent on a single low-margin industry vulnerable to buyer power, automation and order-cancellation shocks - the classic interdependence-with-vulnerability argument for 20-mark essays.

Interdependence, complexity and vulnerability: global systems create mutual reliance, so local events now have global consequences: the Ever Given grounded in the Suez Canal in March 2021 closed it for six days, holding up roughly $9.6 billion of trade a day; the 2020-22 semiconductor shortage idled car plants on three continents; Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine spiked global wheat, fertiliser and energy prices and pushed food inflation worldwide. Interdependence therefore delivers efficiency and growth, but also contagion risk - the 2008 financial crisis spread through linked banking systems within weeks - and it concentrates power in the actors that control the 'switches' of the global network: chokepoints (Suez, Malacca, Hormuz), technical standards, financial centres and digital platforms. The gains, too, are unevenly shared: the richest 1% of adults hold close to half of global wealth, while the same globalisation has lifted more than a billion people out of extreme poverty since 1990, mostly in China. This unequal distribution of both gains and control is the conceptual bridge into Sections 2 and 3: who owns the switches decides whom the rules serve.

Measuring globalisation (AO3): composite indices quantify the unevenness: the KOF Globalisation Index combines economic (trade and FDI), social (contact and information flows) and political (embassies, treaties, peacekeeping) sub-indices - Switzerland, the Netherlands and Belgium rank highest; the AT Kearney/Foreign Policy index is comparable. Use indices for three AO3 judgements: globalisation is uneven in degree (some states remain barely integrated); it is uneven in nature (integration is not the same as westernisation - East Asian and Gulf globalisation follow different cultural paths); and its costs and benefits are distributed unequally - the core evaluation in essays asking whether globalisation benefits everyone.

Flows of people in detail: migration theory earns AO1: Lee's push-pull model (push factors: poverty, conflict, drought; pull factors: wages, safety, opportunity; intervening obstacles: borders, distance, cost) explains why movement is 'stepwise' - LIDC to EME to AC - and why flows follow diaspora networks (chain migration lowers the costs and risks for the next migrant). Scale and consequence: refugees and people in refugee-like situations number over 40 million (UNHCR), concentrated in LIDC host states that can least afford them; remittances reach a quarter of GDP in Nepal and around 9% in the Philippines, exceeding aid several-fold. Migration is gendered (construction corridors for men; domestic and care work for women) and increasingly urban-ward. AO2: migration makes places as well as economies - diasporas transplant culture (food, faith, festivals) and transfer ideas as well as money, the direct link from this section into Changing Places (AQA 3.2.2).

Cultural globalisation and resistance: cultural flows now travel at media speed: Netflix operates in over 190 countries, the Premier League is broadcast to 200+ territories, and English functions as the lingua franca of business, science and aviation. The exam debate is homogenisation versus heterogenisation: one view sees a 'McWorld' of converging consumer culture eroding local identity (placelessness); the other stresses that globalisation also distributes culture outwards - K-pop, Bollywood and Nollywood have global audiences - and provokes resistance: glocalisation as adaptation, and political backlash from populism and nationalism (the Brexit vote in 2016; 'China dream' discourse). AO3: globalisation produces convergence in consumption but divergence in politics - the judicious line in essays asking whether globalisation creates a uniform, borderless world.

Global systems: trade flows, TNCs, IGOs and unequal access to markets (AQA 3.2.1.2-3.2.1.3)

Global systems and trade flows: a global system is a set of interconnected flows and nodes - inputs, transactions, outputs - linking production and consumption worldwide; trade is its most visible flow. World merchandise trade is roughly $24 trillion a year, dominated by the US-EU-East Asia triad plus China (exports of about $3.4 trillion). World trade has grown roughly 400-fold since 1950 - far faster than output - because tariffs fell under GATT/WTO rounds and transport and communications costs collapsed. The historic pattern - ACs exporting manufactures, LIDCs exporting raw materials - was reshaped by global shift: manufacturing migrating to low-cost EMEs from the 1970s (Japan, then the Asian Tigers, then China after WTO accession in 2001, now Vietnam, Bangladesh and Ethiopia), leaving deindustrialisation and rust belts in ACs (Detroit, north-east England) - a process whose social legacy is examined in Changing Places (AQA 3.2.2). Commodity chains reveal where value is captured: in the coffee chain, producing countries receive well under 10% of final retail value, with roasting, branding and retail concentrated in ACs; dependence on a narrow commodity base exposes states to price crashes - oil is roughly 80-90% of Nigeria's export earnings, and the 2014-16 oil price collapse cut government revenue by more than a third (the resource curse).

Access to markets and unequal power relations: market access is structurally unequal. Barriers facing LIDC exporters include tariffs and tariff escalation (processed goods face higher duties than raw materials, discouraging industrialisation); rich-world agricultural subsidies - the EU's Common Agricultural Policy and US farm subsidies depress world prices and undercut developing-country farmers, the core complaint behind the stalled Doha Development Round (launched 2001, effectively dead since 2008); technical and sanitary standards whose certification costs small producers cannot meet; and geographical disadvantage - landlocked states (Chad, Uganda) pay high transport costs and depend on neighbours' ports. Protectionism has returned with the US-China trade war (2018-19 tariffs on roughly $450 billion of bilateral trade), 'buy national' procurement rules and export controls on strategic technologies (advanced semiconductors). AO2: these barriers are not accidental - they reflect unequal power, because the states that write trade rules protect their own sectors while requiring others to liberalise.

Structural adjustment and its critics: both Bretton Woods institutions were created in 1944 and remain dominated by AC capital, so LIDCs borrow on terms set by others. Structural adjustment programmes condition IMF lending on privatisation, trade liberalisation, subsidy removal and public-spending cuts: Jamaica's programmes from 1977 (documented in the film Life and Debt, 2001) opened its markets to subsidised US imports and cut public services while local farmers could not compete - the classic 9-mark example of governance producing unequal outcomes. Greece's bailouts (2010-15) attached austerity that deepened the recession - evidence that conditionality bites ACs too, but under very different power relations. Reform pressure now comes from the BRICS' New Development Bank and China's Belt and Road Initiative (about 150 countries signed up; lending of the order of $1 trillion) - an alternative, China-centred financing system, though BRI debt sustainability is contested (Sri Lanka's Hambantota port leased to China Merchants for 99 years in 2017).

Trade blocs: preferential groupings deepen integration - and divert as well as create trade. The EU single market (27 members; free movement of goods, services, capital and people); USMCA (US, Mexico, Canada; replaced NAFTA in 2020 with tightened automotive rules of origin); ASEAN (10 members; a global manufacturing hub); and the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA, trading from 2021; 54 signatories) which aims to raise intra-African trade from roughly 15% of the continent's trade and lift tens of millions out of poverty. AO2: blocs raise members' collective bargaining power against outsiders (the EU negotiates as one bloc) but discriminate against excluded exporters; and within blocs, poorer members absorb asymmetric shocks (Greece within the eurozone; Lesotho within the Southern African Customs Union) - unequal power operates inside blocs as well as between them.

TNCs: the operating units of global systems: TNCs coordinate production across borders and are the principal system-builders of globalisation. Their global production networks locate each function where it is most profitable: R&D and design in AC agglomerations; component manufacture in specialised clusters; final assembly in low-wage zones; marketing and after-sales in consumer markets. Apple is the archetype: design in Cupertino, California; chips fabricated by TSMC in Taiwan, memory from Samsung (Korea) and Sony (Japan); final assembly by Foxconn in Zhengzhou - 'iPhone City', employing 200,000-350,000 workers at peak, whose dormitories, 2010-12 suicides and strikes drew global scrutiny; and global logistics delivering millions of units within days of launch. Apple's revenues (about $383 billion in 2023) exceed the GDP of most UN member states - TNC power is comparable to state power. TNC behaviour creates flows and inequalities simultaneously: FDI transfers capital, technology and training (Foxconn-linked investment helped raise inland Chinese wages), but transfer pricing and profit-shifting move taxable profits to low-tax jurisdictions (Apple was ordered in 2016 to repay about 13 billion euros to Ireland in the EU state-aid case, upheld in 2024), and buyer power squeezes suppliers (Rana Plaza, Section 1). Offshoring is now partially reversing - reshoring and 'friend-shoring' of semiconductors (the US CHIPS Act, 2022, ~$52 billion of subsidies) show geopolitics re-sorting the map of production: a first-class AO3 point that globalisation is not a one-way process.

Case detail: Shell in the Niger Delta - TNC power and the environment

Royal Dutch Shell has pumped oil in the Niger Delta since 1958. The UN Environment Programme assessment of Ogoniland (2011) found oil contamination of soil and groundwater up to 900 times guideline levels and estimated clean-up could take 25-30 years; Shell has paid settlements (15.5 million dollars over the 1995 execution of Ken Saro-Wiwa's campaign was settled out of court in 2009; a UK Supreme Court ruling in 2021 allowed Nigerian claims against Shell's parent company to proceed). Use for AO2/AO3: a TNC operating with weak host-state regulation externalises environmental costs onto communities - unequal power in a global system extends beyond trade and finance to the environment, and links forward to global-commons governance (Section 3).

Liberalisation in historical perspective: GATT (1947) held eight negotiating rounds, cutting average industrial tariffs from about 22% in 1947 to under 3% today; the Uruguay Round (1986-94) created the WTO (1995) and extended the rules to services (GATS) and intellectual property (TRIPS). TRIPS is itself an unequal-power case: patent rules protect AC pharmaceutical firms while restricting access to generic medicines - the 2001 Doha Declaration on TRIPS and public health allowed compulsory licensing for health emergencies only after the South African HIV-drug controversy. AO2: liberalisation benefits efficient exporters, but the extension of the rules reflects AC interests - unequal power operates inside the institutions that claim to be neutral.

The new trade geography: the map of trade is being re-sorted by geopolitics: RCEP (signed 2020; 15 Asia-Pacific members; roughly 30% of world GDP) is the largest bloc by population and excludes the USA; BRICS expanded in 2024 (Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran, Saudi Arabia, the UAE joining) with its own New Development Bank; Mexico overtook China as the USA's largest goods trading partner in 2023 as firms nearshore under USMCA. AO3: these shifts are first-class counterpoint evidence in essays arguing that globalisation is not irreversible or one-directional - integration patterns respond to politics, security and power, not just to efficiency.

Global governance: Antarctica as a global common, international law, NGOs, sovereignty vs interdependence (AQA 3.2.1.4)

The global commons: parts of the planet outside national jurisdiction - the high seas, the atmosphere, Antarctica, outer space - are global commons: shared resource domains that no state owns, from which none can be excluded, and whose overuse by some diminishes them for all. Hardin's 'tragedy of the commons' (1968) predicts over-exploitation where users act individually and rationally; Elinor Ostrom's work showed commons can be governed successfully where users build rules, monitoring and sanctions together. Governing them requires international law, norms and institutions rather than sovereignty - and Antarctica (AQA 3.2.1.4) is the set-text example of governing a global common.

Antarctica: the resource and the claims: 14 million km2 (roughly 1.4 times the USA), holding about 90% of Earth's ice and roughly 70% of its fresh water - enough to raise global sea level by tens of metres if fully melted. It has no indigenous population and no permanent residents: about 1,000 people overwinter and roughly 4,000-5,000 scientists and support staff occupy seasonal and year-round stations. Seven states claim sectors (Argentina, Australia, Chile, France, New Zealand, Norway and the UK - the Argentine, Chilean and British claims overlap), most states recognise none, and the USA and Russia reserve the right to make claims. Resources at stake: minerals and hydrocarbons (suspected offshore oil); the krill fishery (krill is the base of the Southern Ocean food web; the precautionary quota is about 5.6 million tonnes a year, actual catches around 400,000 tonnes); finfish including Patagonian toothfish (high-value and IUU-poached); tourism (~74,000 visitors in 2019-20, IAATO-supervised); fresh water (icebergs); and bioprospecting - genetic resources from cold-adapted organisms for pharmaceuticals and industrial enzymes, which no treaty yet regulates.

The Antarctic Treaty (1959) and its system: signed on 1 December 1959 by the 12 states active in the 1957-58 International Geophysical Year (Argentina, Australia, Belgium, Chile, France, Japan, New Zealand, Norway, South Africa, the USSR, the UK and the USA), in force since 1961, now with 57 parties of which 29 are consultative (decision-making) parties. Key provisions: Article IV freezes territorial claims - no new claims, no enlargement of existing ones and no recognition of others' claims (a diplomatic fudge that made agreement possible and holds to this day); Antarctica to be used for peaceful purposes only - military activity, nuclear tests and radioactive waste disposal are banned; freedom of scientific investigation with exchange of personnel, data and plans; and decisions by consensus of consultative parties. Case detail: Ross Sea Marine Protected Area (2017)

After five years of negotiation, CCAMLR members - including Russia and China, whose fleets fish krill and toothfish - agreed a 2 million km2 marine protected area in the Ross Sea, the world's largest at designation, with 1.12 million km2 fully closed to fishing and a review scheduled after 35 years (2052). Significance: the largest act of cooperative ocean protection in history, agreed after progress stalled repeatedly (a Chinese vessel fire in 2015 pushed the parties to cooperate) - evidence that governance works when science (penguin and krill population data) and diplomacy combine. But the 35-year sunset clause and reliance on voluntary compliance show its fragility. NGOs - the Antarctic and Southern Ocean Coalition (ASOC), WWF and Greenpeace - campaigned throughout: the standard AO2 evidence for NGO influence in global governance.

NGOs, international law and non-state governance: NGOs watchdog the commons: ASOC holds standing at Antarctic Treaty Consultative Meetings; Greenpeace built the first non-governmental Antarctic base (World Park Base, 1987-92) to lobby for a 'World Park' and against mining; WWF tracks krill fisheries and pushes for MPAs. Their leverage is agenda-setting, science and publicity rather than legal power - contrast with states' enforcement power: treaty inspection teams may inspect any station or vessel without notice. International law supplies the framework, but enforcement depends on consent: there is no Antarctic police force; compliance rests on transparency, peer inspection and reputational cost. This is the general condition of global-commons governance - law without sovereign enforcement.

Threats and the effectiveness of governance (AO2/AO3): pressures on Antarctica include: climate change (the Antarctic Peninsula has warmed by roughly 3 degrees C since 1950; West Antarctic ice-sheet thinning - the Thwaites 'doomsday glacier' alone could eventually add tens of centimetres to sea level); recovering whale and fish stocks inviting commercial expansion; tourism growth (quadrupled since the 1990s; ever-larger vessels raise oil-spill risk - the cruise ship Explorer sank in 2007, fortunately with no casualties); bioprospecting outside any regulatory regime; and, beyond the 2048 review window, potential pressure to revisit the mining ban as global mineral demand rises. Assessment: governance of Antarctica is widely judged the most successful global-commons regime - territorial claims frozen for over 65 years, mining banned, science open to all 57 parties, disputes settled by consensus and prevented from escalating (no war has been fought there), the ozone hole healing under the Montreal Protocol. But its weaknesses are structural: consensus means any consultative party can veto (Russia and China have blocked successive MPA proposals); climate impacts on Antarctica are governed by a separate regime (the UNFCCC) that Antarctica's ecosystems cannot participate in; and enforcement is consensual rather than coercive. Strong 20-mark answers weigh institutional success (longevity, environmental protection, conflict prevention) against fragility (compliance, external pressure, climate change) and conclude conditionally.

Sovereignty vs interdependence (synoptic close): Antarctica crystallises the tension at the heart of globalisation: claimant states assert sovereignty; the treaty system pools it - sovereignty is frozen in exchange for shared scientific access, peace and environmental protection. More broadly, global governance trades absolute sovereignty for the benefits of interdependence: states accept treaty constraints (WTO rules, UNCLOS, the Madrid Protocol) because the gains from cooperation exceed the gains from autonomy - yet unequal power means the rules are written largely by the powerful (Section 2), and IGO membership itself redistributes sovereignty asymmetrically (the US quota-veto at the IMF; consultative vs non-consultative status in Antarctica). The AO3 judgement: sovereignty is not lost but shared and reconfigured - and where governance fails or is absent (climate, bioprospecting), even the most isolated continent shows that interdependence is unavoidable.

Governance machinery in practice: the system runs through annual Antarctic Treaty Consultative Meetings (ATCMs) and the Committee for Environmental Protection (CEP, since 1998), which reviews environmental impact assessments, waste plans and site guidelines; mutual inspections - any party may visit any station or vessel without notice - have operated since 1961; and disputes can go to the International Court of Justice where parties accept its jurisdiction. Tourism is regulated largely through self-regulation: IAATO operators follow site guidelines, landing-size limits and biosecurity protocols - a distinctive AO2 example of non-state governance working, though critics note it is voluntary and unaccountable. Claimant states enforce their law only over their own nationals in their own sectors; no state enforces law over another's nationals - sovereignty in Antarctica is territorial claim without territorial jurisdiction, the sharpest illustration of the sovereignty-interdependence trade-off.

Why Antarctica matters for the synoptic essay: the topic links across the specification: to water and carbon cycles (ice sheets and albedo; the Southern Ocean absorbs a large share of anthropogenic CO2, and its weakening would accelerate warming), to hazards (sea-level rise from ice-sheet loss), and to global systems (krill quotas as resource governance; tourism as a global flow). 20-mark essays on governance reach Level 4 by weighing named instruments against named failures - Treaty and Madrid Protocol against consensus vetoes and IWC withdrawal - and by concluding conditionally: success in conflict prevention and mining prohibition, fragility in climate response and voluntary compliance.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: 'Global governance of Antarctica is effective because the Antarctic Treaty has frozen territorial claims and banned mining.' To what extent do you agree? (20 marks) - AQA-style synoptic question (AQA 3.2.1.4)
Mark-scheme logic: <p><strong>Task:</strong> 'To what extent' demands a supported judgement weighing governance successes (claims frozen, mining ban) against weaknesses and external pressures. <strong>AO1 (~6 marks):</strong> define the global commons (shared, non-excludable, no sovereignty) and the Antarctic Treaty system: the 1959 Treaty (Article IV freezing claims, peaceful purposes, military and nuclear activity banned, freedom of science, consensus decisions), the Madrid Protocol 1991 (Article 7 mining ban, natural-reserve designation, 2048 review), CCAMLR and marine protected areas, plus supporting international law (Montreal Protocol 1987, UNCLOS, Polar Code). <strong>AO2 (~8 marks):</strong> evidence of effectiveness - claims frozen for 65+ years among seven claimants with overlapping sectors (Argentina, Chile, UK) and no conflict; all mining banned indefinitely under the Madrid Protocol despite known mineral potential; science open across 57 parties with mutual inspections; the Ross Sea MPA (2017, 2 million km2) as the world's largest; the ozone hole recovering under the Montreal Protocol (~2066). Evidence of limits: consensus allows veto - Russia and China have blocked successive MPA proposals; compliance is voluntary with no enforcement police (Japan left the IWC in 2019 and resumed whaling in its own EEZ); IUU fishing of Patagonian toothfish persists; tourism has quadrupled since the 1990s (~74,000 visitors 2019-20) and bioprospecting is unregulated; climate change - Peninsula warming ~3 degrees C since 1950, Thwaites thinning - is governed by a separate, weaker regime; the 2048 mining-ban review is a real pressure point. <strong>AO3 (~6 marks):</strong> judgement - the Treaty system is effective at what it was designed for (conflict prevention, demilitarisation, science, and now mining prohibition), but effective governance of a global common requires controlling <em>all</em> threats, and climate change plus unregulated activities fall largely outside the system. Conclude: agree to a large extent - the Antarctic Treaty is the most successful global-commons regime and the claims/mining provisions are its strongest elements - but effectiveness is conditional on consensus holding, and climate impacts and the 2048 review mean governance must extend beyond the Treaty itself to remain effective.</p>

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Explain the concept of the global commons. (4 marks)
Answer: The global commons are resource domains outside national jurisdiction that belong to no state and are shared by all: the high seas, the atmosphere, Antarctica and outer space. Two marks for identifying the areas and two for the properties - no state can exclude others from use (non-excludability), and overuse by some users diminishes the resource for all ( rivalry), which Hardin called the tragedy of the commons and which requires international rather than national governance.
Assess the role of TNCs in accelerating globalisation. (9 marks)
Answer: TNCs are the operating units of globalisation: their global production networks move capital, goods, technology and management practice across borders. They generate FDI (global FDI ~$1.3 trillion, 2023), transfer technology and training to host economies (Foxconn-linked investment raised inland Chinese wages), and spread global marketing and glocalisation (McDonald's localised menus). They also knit together supply chains through outsourcing and just-in-time systems, embedding countries like Bangladesh in world markets. Assessment: TNC power is comparable to state power (Apple revenues ~$383 billion exceed most members' GDP), and their behaviour is profit-led rather than development-led - transfer pricing shifts profits offshore (Apple's ~13 billion euro Irish state-aid case), buyer power squeezes suppliers (Rana Plaza 2013, 1,134 deaths), and production can relocate abruptly. Judgement: TNCs have been the decisive accelerators of the current phase of globalisation, but their contribution is double-edged - they connect places while concentrating power, so their role is best assessed as system-building with unequal outcomes.
Explain how the IMF and World Bank can create unequal power relations. (6 marks)
Answer: Levels-based: both were created at Bretton Woods (1944) and are dominated by developed-economy capital - IMF voting is weighted by quota, giving the USA (~16%) an effective veto, and the World Bank president is traditionally nominated by the USA. LIDCs therefore borrow on terms set by others: structural adjustment programmes attach conditions - privatisation, trade liberalisation, subsidy removal, spending cuts (Jamaica from 1977; documented in Life and Debt) - which open economies to foreign competition and can deepen recessions (Greece 2010-15). Chained explanation of institutional design plus unequal outcomes = 5-6.
Assess the effectiveness of global governance of Antarctica. (9 marks)
Answer: Effective: the Antarctic Treaty (1959, 57 parties, 29 consultative) has frozen seven overlapping territorial claims for 65+ years, banned military activity and nuclear tests, and guaranteed open science with mutual inspections; the Madrid Protocol (1991) bans all mining indefinitely and designates Antarctica a natural reserve; CCAMLR sets precautionary krill quotas and created the Ross Sea MPA (2017, 2 million km2); the Montreal Protocol is healing the ozone hole (recovery ~2066). Limits: consensus lets any consultative party veto (Russia and China have blocked MPA proposals); compliance is voluntary with no enforcement police; IUU toothfish fishing, unregulated bioprospecting and quadrupling tourism (~74,000 visitors 2019-20) persist; climate change - Peninsula warming ~3 degrees C since 1950 - lies largely outside the treaty system; the 2048 mining-ban review is a pressure point. Judgement: highly effective at conflict prevention, demilitarisation and mining prohibition, but weaker against external threats (climate, fishing, tourism), so effectiveness depends on consensus holding and on regimes beyond the Treaty.
'Globalisation increases interdependence but reduces national sovereignty.' To what extent do you agree? (20 marks)
Answer: AO1: define globalisation (widening, deepening, speeding-up of interconnectedness), interdependence (mutual reliance through flows of capital, goods, people, ideas) and sovereignty (supreme authority within a territory). AO2: agree - interdependence is empirically clear (trade ~$24 trillion; supply chains where the Ever Given's 2021 Suez grounding held up ~$9.6 billion a day of trade; contagion in the 2008 crisis), and states accept treaty constraints that pool sovereignty: WTO rules bind trade policy, IMF conditionality constrains economic policy (Greece), UNCLOS and the Madrid Protocol limit territorial autonomy, and membership itself redistributes power (US quota-veto at the IMF). Disagree - sovereignty is reconfigured, not removed: powerful states write the rules (US-China trade war tariffs, CHIPS Act reshoring, US blocking WTO Appellate Body appointments show states overriding the system); interdependence is chosen because its benefits exceed autonomy's; and sovereignty persists at the edges - North Korea, and Antarctica's claimant states whose sovereignty was frozen rather than abolished. AO3: judgement - interdependence and sovereignty trade off, but asymmetrically: small states lose more autonomy (conditionality, bloc rules) while large states retain exit options; global governance converts absolute sovereignty into shared, rule-bound sovereignty, and where governance fails the strong reassert theirs. Conclude: largely agree for most states, but agree only partially for the powerful - sovereignty is shared and reconfigured rather than simply reduced.
