Changing Places

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC/Eduqas, CCEA

A* revision for Changing Places: concepts of place and placelessness (Relph), endogenous and exogenous factors, rebranding and regeneration with the Detroit case study, and methods for a local place study, for AQA 7037 3.2.2, Edexcel and OCR.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define place through location, locale and sense of place, explain placelessness (Relph 1976) and non-places (Auge 1992), and distinguish insider and outsider perspectives on place (AQA 3.2.2.1)
  • Categorise endogenous factors (location, topography, physical geography, land use, built environment, infrastructure, demographic and economic characteristics) and exogenous factors (flows of people, capital, resources and ideas) and analyse how they interact to shape place character (AQA 3.2.2.1)
  • Explain how shifting flows of people, resources, money and investment reshape the demographic, cultural and economic character of places, using deindustrialisation and the Detroit case study (AQA 3.2.2.2)
  • Describe and evaluate rebranding and regeneration strategies - flagship, market-led and heritage/legacy approaches - and assess their effectiveness in Detroit (AQA 3.2.2.2; Edexcel Regenerating Places)
  • Plan and evaluate a local place study using quantitative sources (census, Index of Multiple Deprivation), qualitative sources (interviews, media, photographs) and GIS, and discuss how places are represented (AQA 3.2.2.3; OCR Changing Spaces; Making Places)

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Place: A location invested with meaning and identity, combining Agnew's (1987) three fundamental aspects: location (where it is), locale (the material setting for social relations) and sense of place (subjective emotional attachment).
Sense of place: The subjective meanings, emotions and attachments that people and groups give to a place; strongest at home (Relph's existential insider) and constructed differently by outsiders such as tourists, planners and census analysts.
Placelessness: Relph's (1976) term for the erosion of distinctive, authentic places by standardised, place-independent landscapes - clone high streets, retail parks, airports, motorway services - produced by the 'inauthentic' attitudes of planners, developers and corporations; Augé's (1992) 'non-places' (transit spaces with no history or community) are the extreme case.
Endogenous factors: Internal characteristics that shape a place's character from within: location, topography, physical geography, land use, built environment, infrastructure, and its demographic and economic features.
Exogenous factors: External influences on place character arising from relationships and connections with other places: shifting flows of people (migration), capital and investment, resources, and ideas and technology, mediated by globalisation, TNC decisions and government policy.
Rebranding: How a place is re-presented to overcome a negative image: new logotypes, media campaigns, flagship developments and events designed to change external perceptions and attract people and investment.
Regeneration: The physical, economic and social renewal of a declining area - property-led and flagship schemes (Detroit's RiverWalk, Little Caesars Arena), heritage-led reuse (Michigan Central Station) and community-level programmes.
Gentrification: The influx of middle-class residents and investment into historically working-class neighbourhoods, refurbishing housing but displacing original residents - visible in Detroit's Midtown and Corktown.

Concepts of place: location, locale, sense of place, placelessness, endogenous and exogenous factors (AQA 3.2.2.1)

What is place? Open with Agnew's (1987) three fundamental aspects: location - the objective 'where of place', fixable by grid coordinates; locale - the material setting for social relations, the physical and built environment staging everyday life; and sense of place - the subjective, emotional attachment to a place, its meanings and identity. Tuan (1974) called positive attachment topophilia ('love of place'); the examiner-recognised step is that space becomes place only when invested with meaning. High-band answers add that places are multi-layered: residents, commuters, tourists and planners read the same locale differently, so one place can hold multiple, even conflicting, senses of place.

Placelessness and authenticity: Relph (1976, Place and Placelessness) argued that modernity produces placelessness - standardised, serialised landscapes lacking genuine place identity: chain stores and clone high streets, motorway services, airport terminals, retail parks, international hotels, anonymous suburbs. It stems from the 'inauthentic' attitudes of planners and corporations imposing place-independent design; Augé (1992) extended this with non-places - transit and consumption spaces where people coexist without community, history or identity, negotiated through signs and tickets rather than relationships. Relph's perspectives earn AO2: existential insider (belongs, place as home), empathetic outsider (strives to understand), objective outsider (detached study, as census analysts do), ideological outsider (imports media preconceptions - risks stereotyping). Massey (1991, 'A Global Sense of Place') counters: places are not sealed containers but bundles of social relations and flows (Kilburn in London: multi-ethnic, globally connected), so identity should be progressive - outward-looking - not reactionary localism; Harvey (1996) adds that places are socially constructed and can exclude (gated communities).

Endogenous and exogenous factors: endogenous factors act from within; exogenous factors reach the place through relationships and connections with other places. Both must be chained to a case study - lists score level 1.

The two interact: exogenous flows are filtered through endogenous qualities. Detroit's river-and-lakes location drew the automobile industry; globalisation - the same force - later stripped it away. This double edge of connectivity is the synoptic heart of the topic and the pivot for most 20-mark judgements.

Examiner tip: framing endogenous/exogenous in essays

Trace one chain, not a list: endogenous location draws exogenous investment; exogenous change rewrites the endogenous base (jobs, demographics, land use); the new base attracts new flows. Chains reach level 3.

Relationships and connections: demographic, cultural and economic change - rebranding and regeneration in Detroit (AQA 3.2.2.2)

Shifting flows remake place character: a place's demographic, cultural and economic character is continuously rewritten by relationships and connections - shifting flows of people, resources, money and investment. Demographic change: migration rewrites age structure and ethnic mix (2021 census: England and Wales 81.7% White, one in six residents born abroad; rapid EU migration reshaped towns such as Boston, Lincolnshire). Cultural change: language, food, religion and festivals follow migrants and global media. Economic change: employment shifts from primary to secondary to tertiary and quaternary, and deindustrialisation - the collapse of manufacturing employment in ACs - is the topic's dominant theme. Detroit is the set-text example of place both made and unmade by exogenous flows.

Detroit: made by the car, unmade by deindustrialisation. Made: Detroit's location on the Detroit River - the link between Lakes Erie and Huron, a border crossing to Canada (Ambassador Bridge 1929, Windsor Tunnel 1930) - plus cheap land and Great Lakes access to iron ore and coal drew manufacturing. Ford's moving assembly line (1913, Highland Park) and the River Rouge complex (from 1918, about 100,000 workers at peak) made it the 'Motor City': the Big Three dominated, wartime output earned 'Arsenal of Democracy', population peaked at 1.85 million (1950) - then among the richest US cities per head - and Motown Records (1959) gave it a global cultural brand. Unmade: automation and offshoring cut manufacturing from about 296,000 jobs (1950, roughly half of city employment) to under 20,000; Japanese competition after the 1973 oil crisis closed Big Three plants; the 1967 uprising (43 deaths, about 2,000 buildings burned) accelerated white flight to the suburbs; and the 2008 financial crisis pushed GM and Chrysler into Chapter 11 (2009) and caused about 65,000 foreclosures (2005-15). Population fell about 65% to 639,111 (2020); a third of the city's 139 square miles became vacant; unemployment peaked near 25% (2009); and in 2013 Detroit filed the largest municipal bankruptcy in US history (about 18 billion dollars of debt), exiting in 2014 through the 'grand bargain' that shed about 7 billion dollars.

Rebranding and regeneration: the comeback city? The two work together: regeneration (physical-economic renewal) and rebranding (re-presentation to change perceptions). The Detroit Riverfront Conservancy (founded 2003) converted derelict docks into the RiverWalk - over three miles including the Renaissance Center (GM headquarters, 1996), Atwater Beach and Cullen Plaza (2016) - drawing three million-plus visits a year. Rocket Mortgage moved its headquarters downtown (2010); Dan Gilbert refurbished over 100 buildings (about 5.6 billion dollars); the QLine streetcar (2017, 137 million dollars) relinked Woodward Avenue; Little Caesars Arena (2017, 863 million dollars) anchored the District Detroit quarter; Shinola (2011) rebuilt a heritage brand ('Built in Detroit'); and Ford spent about 950 million dollars restoring Michigan Central Station (derelict from 1988, reopened 2024) as a mobility-innovation campus - beside the 'America's Great Comeback City' rebrand. AO3 evaluation: the projects are flagship-concentrated downtown and in Midtown (a 'comeback island') while outer neighbourhoods lose population, housing and services; the economy stays narrower than before; and rebranding attracts visitors and graduates faster than it restores manufacturing jobs - so regeneration changed the place image but only partly reversed the deindustrialisation that caused decline.

Local place study: quantitative and qualitative sources, GIS and representations of place (AQA 3.2.2.3; OCR Changing Spaces; Making Places)

Quantitative sources: the local place study (AQA 3.2.2.3; OCR 'Changing Spaces; Making Places'; Edexcel 'Diverse Places') is built from citable data. The ONS Census (2021, England and Wales) gives decennial counts of population, age, ethnicity, country of birth, tenure, qualifications, occupation and car availability down to output-area level, enabling change-over-time comparison with the 2001 and 2011 tables. The Index of Multiple Deprivation (England: IMD 2019) ranks every LSOA on seven weighted domains - income (22.5%), employment (22.5%), education, health, crime, barriers to housing and services, living environment - and is designed for choropleth mapping of deprivation hot and cold spots. Add claimant-count unemployment, Land Registry house-price series and environmental quality surveys (bipolar scoring of litter, noise, greenery) collected along transects.

Qualitative sources and representations: quantitative data describe who and how many; qualitative data recover meaning and sense of place. Interviews (structured through unstructured) and open-question surveys capture insider testimony - why residents value or fear change - but samples are small and answers can be performative. Participant observation (walking the place as an 'empathetic outsider'), field sketches and photography (your own images versus archive photographs) document lived experience. Media representations - newspapers, TV, film, tourism brochures, property marketing, art, literature - are curated constructions that rebrand places selectively: compare a council's investment prospectus with residents' interviews or national press coverage, and the gap between representation and lived reality is itself AO3 evidence. Every source is a representation: the census's categories, the media's angles and your own photographs all involve choices.

GIS: QGIS or ArcGIS layers sources spatially: choropleth maps of IMD deciles or census ethnicity by output area; buffers around regeneration sites; heat maps of geotagged photographs or social-media posts to compare an outsider's digital image of a place with residents' priorities. Strengths for AO3: rapid multi-layer comparison, pattern detection, change-over-time mapping. Limitations: the census is a decade old the day it is published; data are aggregated (ecological fallacy - LSOA averages hide individual experience); digital sources under-represent elderly and low-income groups.

Synthesis: a strong study triangulates - a quantitative skeleton (census, IMD), qualitative flesh (interviews, observation, media analysis) and GIS to integrate both - and reflects explicitly on insider versus outsider perspectives (Relph's four-fold framework) so the place profile distinguishes lived meaning from external representation.

Examiner tip: the 9-mark source-evaluation answer

Judge each source on purpose (why was it produced?), date, scale (national dataset or one street's sample?), curation (whose view is absent?) and fitness for the question. Conclude with a judgement: census data are authoritative but static; interviews are current but small-sample; media sources are vivid but curated - so triangulate.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: 'Rebranding is the most effective response to the problems created by deindustrialisation.' To what extent do you agree with this view? (20 marks) - AQA-style 3.2.2 synoptic question
Mark-scheme logic: <p><strong>Task:</strong> 'To what extent' demands a judgement comparing rebranding's effectiveness against other responses to deindustrialisation - diversification, housing and infrastructure renewal, retraining, and population strategies. <strong>AO1 (~6 marks):</strong> define rebranding (re-presenting a place to change perceptions: logotypes, media campaigns, flagship projects) and regeneration (physical-economic renewal), set against deindustrialisation's problems: mass manufacturing job loss (Detroit: about 296,000 manufacturing jobs in 1950 to under 20,000 today), depopulation (1.85 million in 1950 to 639,111 in 2020), dereliction (about 40 square miles of vacant land; the Packard Plant), poverty and fiscal collapse (largest-ever US municipal bankruptcy, 2013, about 18 billion dollars). <strong>AO2 (~8 marks):</strong> evidence for rebranding's effectiveness - the RiverWalk (three-plus miles, three million-plus visits a year) recast the riverfront image; Rocket Mortgage's 2010 downtown relocation and Dan Gilbert's roughly 5.6 billion dollars of property investment restarted the commercial core; Michigan Central Station's 950-million-dollar reopening (2024) rebranded the city around mobility technology; Shinola and Motown heritage sell a credible story; population loss slowed to near-stability (about 633,000 by 2023) and downtown employment grew. Against: benefits are spatially concentrated downtown and in Midtown, outer neighbourhoods continue to lose services and housing; rebranding attracts tourism, graduates and tech jobs but not mass manufacturing employment; gentrification and 'comeback island' inequality follow; and the strategy depends on exogenous investment that can leave as fast as it arrived, as the auto industry proved. Weigh alternatives: diversification (health, education, tech campuses) addresses employment directly; blight-removal and housing programmes address dereliction where flagships do not; but without a new image neither attracts the people and capital required - image and substance are mutually dependent. <strong>AO3 (~6 marks):</strong> judgement - rebranding is effective as a necessary gateway (it reverses the perception problem that deters investment) but insufficient alone; agree with the statement in the short term, since Detroit's image recovery preceded measurable economic recovery, but disagree in the long term, because only diversified employment, housing and neighbourhood-level regeneration - not just flagship re-presentation - can reverse the underlying deindustrialisation. Best answers conclude: rebranding is the most visible response and the most effective first move, not the most complete one.</p>

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Explain the difference between endogenous and exogenous factors in shaping the character of a place. (4 marks)
Answer: Endogenous factors are internal characteristics that give a place its identity from within: location, topography, physical geography, land use, built environment and infrastructure, and its demographic and economic characteristics. Exogenous factors are external influences reaching the place through its relationships and connections with other places - shifting flows of people (migration, white flight), money and investment (TNC location decisions, government spending), resources and ideas (technology, globalisation). Two marks for each side of the distinction, with credit for chaining to a named place (e.g. Detroit's river location drew auto investment - endogenous enabling exogenous).
Explain what Relph (1976) meant by placelessness. (6 marks)
Answer: Relph argued that modern planning and global capitalism produce placelessness: landscapes of standardised, place-independent design that lack authentic local identity - chain stores and clone high streets, retail parks, motorway service areas, airports and international hotel chains. Levels logic: Level 1 names the concept only; Level 2 explains the mechanism - 'inauthentic' attitudes of developers and planners impose uniform design, so places lose their distinctive genius loci; Level 3 chains mechanism, example and critique - Auge's (1992) 'non-places' (transit spaces with no history or community) plus Massey's (1991) counter-argument that places are open bundles of global relations, so connectivity is not the same as placelessness. Six marks require concept, mechanism, example and an alternative view.
Assess the usefulness of census data and GIS in investigating the character of your local place. (9 marks)
Answer: Census (ONS 2021) usefulness: authoritative, complete-count data at output-area level on age, ethnicity, tenure, qualifications and occupation, enabling change-over-time analysis against the 2001 and 2011 censuses and deprivation mapping via the IMD 2019 (seven domains, LSOA level). GIS usefulness: layering (choropleths of IMD deciles, ethnicity, tenure), buffers around regeneration sites and heat maps of geotagged photographs reveal spatial patterns invisible in tables and integrate quantitative with qualitative data. Assessment: the census is authoritative but decennial and aggregated to areas (ecological fallacy), its categories are contested and it records who lives somewhere, not meanings - so it under-captures sense of place; GIS visualises patterns but depends on input-data quality, and digital sources skew young and affluent. Judgement: highly useful for demographic and economic character and change, weak on perception and meaning - strongest when triangulated with interviews and observation.
Assess the effectiveness of the strategies used to regenerate Detroit. (9 marks)
Answer: Strategies and evidence: flagship/property-led - the Riverfront Conservancy's RiverWalk (from 2003; three-plus miles; three million-plus visits a year), Little Caesars Arena (2017, 863 million dollars) and the QLine (2017, 137 million dollars); market-led - Rocket Mortgage's 2010 headquarters relocation and Dan Gilbert's roughly 5.6 billion dollars of downtown property refurbishment; heritage/legacy - Shinola's 'Built in Detroit' brand and Ford's 950-million-dollar Michigan Central Station reopening (2024); plus fiscal repair through the 2014 'grand bargain'. Effectiveness: the city's image has transformed, population loss has slowed to near-stability (about 633,000), and downtown and Midtown have revived with growing service and tech employment. Limits: revival is concentrated in the core while outer neighbourhoods continue to lose housing and services; manufacturing employment remains under 20,000; gentrification tensions persist. Judgement: effective at rebranding and concentrating investment, only partly effective at reversing deindustrialisation's social damage - effectiveness depends on spreading benefits beyond the flagship core.
'The character of places is determined more by relationships and connections than by physical endowment.' To what extent do you agree? (20 marks)
Answer: AO1 (~6): define character (demographic, cultural, economic, built), endowment (endogenous factors: location, topography, land use, infrastructure) and relationships and connections (exogenous flows of people, capital, resources and ideas). AO2 (~8): agree - Detroit: an ordinary river crossing became the world 'Motor City' because of exogenous flows (Ford's moving assembly line, auto investment, worker migration), and was remade again by globalisation (Japanese competition, offshoring, the 2008 crisis) despite an unchanged endowment; Massey (1991) argues places are bundles of social relations, so character is relational by definition; UK towns' ethnic and linguistic character has been rewritten by migration flows (2021 census). Disagree - endowment conditions which flows a place can capture: Detroit's river, rail and lakes geography is precisely why the auto industry located there; coasts, coalfields and passes still anchor cities; topography can block flows outright; and the endogenous built environment persists as character long after flows change (the derelict Packard Plant defines Detroit's image as much as any new investment). AO3 (~6): judgement - the dichotomy is false but useful: flows determine a place's trajectory and current character, while endowment determines which flows it can attract and filter; over time past flows become endogenous (Motown's cultural legacy now anchors Detroit's rebranding). Conclude: largely agree for the pace and direction of change; partially agree for the slower, underlying character set by endowment.
