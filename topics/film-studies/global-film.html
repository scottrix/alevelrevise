Global Film

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level WJEC/Eduqas

A-Level Film Studies revision for Component 2A (Global film two-film study): non-English language European and non-European set films, their contexts, auteurism, and how production, distribution and exhibition shape global cinema. WJEC/Eduqas A670QS.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Describe the two-film study requirements: one non-English language European film (Group 1) and one non-English language film produced outside Europe (Group 2), studied through the core study areas only (AO1)
  • Analyse how each set film represents a distinct geographical, social and cultural world through expressive use of film form
  • Apply the social, political, cultural and institutional contexts of the films' production — censorship, national cinema policy, festival economics
  • Identify transnational auteur signatures (del Toro, Cuarón, Bong Joon-ho, Sciamma) and evaluate auteurism in global film
  • Explain how production, distribution and exhibition (festivals, subtitles, streaming, awards) shape global film's meanings and reach
  • Construct the 40-mark two-film response: core-area analysis of both films, AO1/AO2 in equal measures, no specialist study area required

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Global film: The spec's term for films studied in Component 2A — a two-film study of one European and one non-European non-English language film, each representing a distinct geographical, social and cultural world.
National cinema: The idea that film industries express national identity and culture; each Group 1 film reflects its director and its country of origin, but the category is complicated by co-production and streaming.
Transnational cinema: Film production, funding and meaning-making that crosses national borders — the more accurate frame for global films financed, cast and distributed internationally.
Festival film: A film premiered and validated through the international festival circuit (Cannes, Venice, Berlin), whose awards economy shapes which global films reach Western audiences.
Wuxia: The Chinese martial-arts genre of chivalric heroes, gravity-defying choreography and spectacular wire work; House of Flying Daggers is both a spectacular wuxia film and an intense love story.
Female gaze: A critical perspective on visual pleasure constructed from women's experience; Portrait of a Lady on Fire is described in the spec as a poetic manifesto about the female gaze.
Non-English language film: A film in a language other than English; the spec's Group 1 (European) and Group 2 (outside Europe) lists are non-English language, though La Haine (Kassovitz, 1995) — a former set film — remains useful context.
Distribution: The industry stage that carries a film from producer to exhibitor — for global films typically via festival sales, specialist distributors, and increasingly streaming platforms.
Exhibition: The screening context — cinema, festival, television or streaming — which shapes spectator response, particularly for subtitled films designed for cross-cultural audiences.
Auteur signature: Recurring formal and thematic features identifying a director's authorship — e.g. Cuarón's long takes and autobiographical memory, or Bong's tonal whiplash between genres.

📌 Key Points

Component 2A: 40 marks, choice of two questions, two films (one European, one outside Europe), core study areas only — no specialist study area attached.
Comparison is not assessed anywhere in Component 2 — but both films must be referenced in the answer.
Group 1: Life is Beautiful, Pan's Labyrinth, Mustang, Portrait of a Lady on Fire, Another Round, The Worst Person in the World.
Group 2: City of God, House of Flying Daggers, Taxi Tehran, A Fantastic Woman, Roma, Parasite.
Streaming (Netflix) funded and exhibited Roma and bought Parasite — a key distribution/exhibition context.

The Two-Film Study: Distinct Worlds, Expressive Form

Component 2A requires the study of two non-English language films: one European (Group 1) and one produced outside Europe (Group 2), in relation to the core study areas — no specialist study area is attached. The section's purpose is to extend the range and diversity of narrative film: each film represents a distinct geographical, social, cultural world and a particular expressive use of film form.

Group 1 (European): Life is Beautiful (Benigni, Italy, 1997) treats the Holocaust through comedy and fantasy in problematic ways; Pan's Labyrinth (Del Toro, Spain, 2006) mixes surrealism and fantasy in its narrative of civil-war Spain; Mustang (Ergüven, France/Turkey, 2015) explores five orphaned sisters in a remote Turkish village facing arranged marriages; Portrait of a Lady on Fire (Sciamma, France, 2019) is a poetic manifesto about the female gaze and a love affair in eighteenth-century France; Another Round (Vinterberg, Denmark, 2020) explores Danish drinking culture and midlife crisis; The Worst Person in the World (Trier, Norway, 2021) fragments a romance into chapters.

Group 2 (outside Europe): City of God (Meirelles, Brazil, 2002) contrasts young lives in the favelas' violent environment; House of Flying Daggers (Zhang, China, 2004) fuses wuxia spectacle with love story; Taxi Tehran (Panahi, Iran, 2015) is shot entirely from dashboard cameras by a banned filmmaker — repression made form; A Fantastic Woman (Lelío, Chile, 2017) explores gender and identity and provoked real legal change to transgender rights in Chile; Roma (Cuarón, Mexico, 2018) follows a Mixtec domestic worker in 1970s Mexico City, exposing a class system based on gender and ethnicity; Parasite (Bong Joon-ho, South Korea, 2019) is a biting class satire in which the poor Kim family infiltrate the wealthy Parks.

La Haine (Kassovitz, France, 1995) — a former set film — remains useful context for the French banlieue tradition that Mustang's social confinement echoes, but centre your preparation on the current list.

Pairing strategy. Any Group 1 film may be chosen with any Group 2 film — the spec allows any combination. The most productive pairings maximise contrast in world and form: Pan's Labyrinth with Parasite (fantasy allegory vs realist satire, both about fascism's and class's violence); Portrait of a Lady on Fire with Roma (the gaze and mutual looking; women's interiority under class constraint); Mustang with City of God (patriarchal enclosure vs urban anarchy); Another Round with Parasite (European midlife melancholy vs Korean class violence). Because no specialist study area is attached, your preparation should cover, for each film: two sequences mapped to core areas (film language, narrative, representation, contexts) and one auteur signature. That coverage answers either of the two offered questions, which usually focus on film language, narrative construction or the representation of an issue.

Contexts and Auteurism in Global Film

Political and social contexts are inseparable from these films. Censorship and repression: Pan's Labyrinth is set in 1944 Francoist Spain and reads as allegory of fascism; Taxi Tehran exists because Panahi is banned from filmmaking in Iran — the dashboard camera is both an evasion of surveillance and a confession of confinement. Social hierarchy: Parasite's semi-basement/staircase geography literalises class; Roma's fixed compositions (the carport, the service corridor) position Cleo within the household's class and ethnic order. Gender regimes: Mustang's domestic enclosure (bars on windows, the 'house of sluts' taunt) constructs patriarchy as architecture; A Fantastic Woman's framing of Marina against hostile institutions carries a country's legal context — the film fed directly into Chile's 2018 gender-identity law.

Institutional context matters equally: European films are typically financed through national film funds, co-production treaties and pre-sales, then validated by the festival circuit — Pan's Labyrinth, Mustang, Portrait of a Lady on Fire and Another Round all premiered or won major prizes at Cannes or Venice, which is how global films acquire Western distribution. Roma and Parasite belong to the newer context: streaming and global awards — Netflix funded and exhibited Roma (with a limited theatrical release), and Parasite became the first non-English language Best Picture winner (2020), changing assumptions about subtitled film's commercial ceiling.

Auteurism in global film: each Group 1 film 'reflects its director and its country of origin' — the spec's own auteurist phrasing. Identify transnational signatures: Del Toro's fairy-tale monsters and fascist antagonists; Cuarón's long takes, autobiographical memory and black-and-white 65mm digital cinematography; Bong's tonal whiplash (comedy into horror) and staircase geography; Sciamma's controlled, painterly framing and mutual gaze; Vinterberg's Dogme-rooted naturalism. Evaluate how far these signatures survive across national contexts — Cuarón's and Bong's Hollywood work suggests auteurism now travels, but institutional context (national funding, festival validation) still shapes what gets made.

Historical range as context. The two groups span 1997-2021, and the historical shift matters: Life is Beautiful (1997) belongs to the pre-streaming festival-and-distributor era; City of God (2002) marked the global breakthrough of Brazilian cinema via Cannes and Miramax; Parasite (2019) and Roma (2018) belong to the streaming-and-global-awards era. Also know the national cinema policy context: European co-production treaties, national film funds and screen quotas exist to protect and promote non-English language film, and tax-incentive regimes attract transnational productions. Auteurism refined: the spec's phrasing — each film 'reflects its director and its country of origin' — invites you to weigh director against nation as author. The strongest answers test both: is Roma Cuarón's film or Mexico's? Is Parasite Bong's or Korean cinema's? The answer is that the signatures (long takes, tonal whiplash) and the national contexts (class structures, censorship) are inseparable — and saying so is the evaluation the top band rewards.

Production, Distribution and Exhibition of Global Film

Production: global films are typically low-to-mid budget productions financed through national film funds (Spain's ICAA, Mexico's FONCA-era support for Roma via Cuarón's relationship with Netflix), co-production treaties and private finance; City of God was made with Brazilian and French co-production, mixing professional with non-professional actors cast from the favelas. Production context explains film language: limited budgets and location access explain City of God's kinetic handheld immediacy; full creative control and streaming finance explain Roma's deliberate slow pacing and monochrome 65mm digital texture.

Distribution: the traditional route ran through festival sales (Cannes, Venice, Toronto) to specialist distributors handling subtitled releases to art-house audiences — a niche that shaped what Western spectators saw of world cinema. La Haine (1995) and City of God (2002) both broke through that niche commercially. The newer route is streaming: Netflix's purchase of Parasite's international rights and its funding/exhibition of Roma globalised subtitled film's audience overnight — Roma screened in limited cinemas (partly to qualify for awards) before streaming in 190 countries. The tension between theatrical exclusivity and streaming reach is a live industry debate worth citing in any industry question.

Exhibition: subtitled films historically exhibited in art-house cinemas and festivals; streaming platforms now exhibit them globally, and the 2020 Parasite Best Picture Oscar marked mainstream acceptance. Exhibition shapes response: subtitles themselves are a formal element — reading versus watching changes spectator engagement (the spec's spectator-awareness applies), and a festival premiere creates a different reception context to a streaming drop. Weave these industry contexts into language analysis: Parasite's accessibility to global audiences (its genre hybridity) is inseparable from its distribution strategy, and Roma's intimacy is inseparable from Netflix's production control.

Global film's audience completes the industry picture. Subtitled film historically fought the perception of a niche art-house audience; the set films' commercial histories show the shift: City of God and La Haine crossed over commercially in their eras; Parasite grossed over $250m worldwide — unprecedented for a subtitled film — and Another Round and The Worst Person in the World reached mainstream audiences through awards buzz and streaming. Exhibition shapes response: a festival premiere (Cannes competition, where Portrait of a Lady on Fire won Best Screenplay and Mustang premiered) creates a critical reception context that a streaming drop does not; subtitles change engagement (reading versus watching — the spectator's attention splits); and cinephile audiences meet these films differently from casual streamers. For any industry-focused question, bind one production, one distribution and one exhibition fact to a specific film and a specific technique — quantified industry context plus film language is the combination examiners reward.

The Component 2A Essay

Section A is worth 40 marks (choice of two questions) and requires reference to two global films — one European, one outside Europe. Three rules distinguish it: questions are based on the core study areas only (no specialist study area is attached, so no auteur/ideology/spectatorship obligation — though you may still use them); comparison is not assessed anywhere in Component 2 — but both films must be referenced; and AO1/AO2 are assessed in equal measures. Questions typically focus on core areas: film language (mise-en-scène, cinematography, editing, sound), narrative construction, representation of an issue, or contexts reflected in the films.

Structure for A*:

  1. Introduction naming both films, directors, countries and dates, with a thesis responsive to the question.
  2. Two/three analytical paragraphs per film, each anchored in precise moments and precise terminology, each completing the chain technique → connotation → response.
  3. Context woven: political/social context (fascism, patriarchy, class) explained through technique — the dashboard camera, the staircase geography, the barred windows; institutional context (festival, streaming) where relevant.
  4. Cross-reference both films even though comparison is not assessed — connectives showing shared strategies or contrasts read stronger than two sealed halves.
  5. Conclusion judging how the distinct worlds are constructed and how spectators are positioned within them.

Preparation: for each of your two films, map two sequences to the core areas (film language, narrative, representation, contexts) and one to auteur signature. Group 1 films reward attention to fantasy/reality transitions (Pan's Labyrinth), the gaze (Portrait of a Lady on Fire) and social enclosure (Mustang); Group 2 films reward attention to class geography (Parasite, Roma), kinetic urban form (City of God) and repression-as-form (Taxi Tehran). That coverage answers either of the two offered questions.

Band awareness for 2A. Middle-band answers describe the two films separately and list context; top bands complete the technique → connotation → response chain for each precise moment, weave political/social context into language (the dashboard camera is repression; the staircase is class), cross-reference the films lightly, and judge how each distinct world positions its spectator. Timing: 40 marks in roughly 55-60 minutes, one of four sections in the 2½-hour paper — budget accordingly and leave time for the three 20-mark sections. Remember the marks distribution: 2A 40 marks, 2B (documentary), 2C (silent cinema) and 2D (experimental film) 20 marks each — 2A is the component's centre of gravity, and your strongest prepared material should sit there.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: Explore how mise-en-scène and cinematography construct the distinct worlds of your two global films. [40]
Mark-scheme logic: 40 marks = AO1 20 + AO2 20, equal measures; Component 2A assesses the core study areas only, with reference to two global films (A670QS Component 2A).<br><br><strong>AO1 - knowledge and understanding (20):</strong> accurate terminology for mise-en-scène and cinematography (composition, depth of field, framing, colour palette, camera movement); accurate film-specific detail — directors, countries, dates, precise sequence placement; knowledge of how the elements operate in each film's distinct world (e.g. Parasite's staircase/basement geography; Pan's Labyrinth's fantasy-realist colour coding); awareness of institutional context where relevant (festival validation, streaming finance).<br><br><strong>AO2 - application and analysis (20):</strong> every technical observation anchored to the construction of the films' distinct worlds — connotations explained, not asserted; discussion of how mise-en-scène and cinematography overlap (framing and depth of field) in both films; explicit discussion of how spectators are positioned within each world (alignment, enclosure, participation); context woven to explain choices (Francoist history, class hierarchy, patriarchy) rather than listed; cross-references between the two films integrated throughout.<br><br><strong>Top-band shape:</strong> a thesis in the introduction (e.g. how each film's form makes its social world physical), two to three precise moments per film, integrated cross-references, and a conclusion that judges how the distinct worlds generate different spectator positions — not a summary of techniques.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Explore how narrative construction reflects the social contexts of your two global films. [40]
Answer: AO1: accurate narrative terminology (linear arc, ellipsis, chapter structure, fantasy interludes, open ending) plus accurate detail from both films. AO2: bind structure to context: Parasite's linear escalation into the underground sequences reflects class rigidity — the narrative's descent literalises hierarchy; Pan's Labyrinth alternates fantasy and reality strands so that civil-war Spain and Ofelia's resistance structure the meaning of the fantasy; Mustang's linear tightening (seasons, arranged marriages) mirrors patriarchal enclosure; Roma's episodic domestic structure reflects the household's rhythms of class and gender. Judgement: narrative shape is where social context becomes form — the strongest answers judge how far structure, not subject alone, carries each film's politics.
How do your two global films represent class or social hierarchy? [40]
Answer: AO1: accurate detail — Parasite's Kim/Park contrast and semi-basement geography; Roma's Cleo as Mixtec domestic worker serving an affluent family in 1970s Roma neighbourhood; City of God's favela hierarchies. AO2: bind representation to film language: Parasite's vertical compositions (stairs, basement, garden) make class physical; Roma's fixed, symmetrical framings position Cleo at the edges of the frame and the household, with the carpark washing sequence making her labour visible; City of God's handheld immediacy and pop-driven kinetic form construct favela life as energetic rather than merely deprived. Judgement: both films construct hierarchy through space and framing — and the strongest answers judge how far each film's form invites identification or observation.
Compare how far your two global films reflect their directors' authorship. [40 — auteur focus, allowed but not required]
Answer: AO1: accurate detail — Cuarón's long takes, autobiographical memory and monochrome 65mm digital cinematography in Roma; Del Toro's fairy-tale monsters and fascist antagonists in Pan's Labyrinth. AO2: identify signatures and test them: Del Toro's monster-fascist pairing recurs across his work (the Pale Man visual-rhymes with Vidal), suggesting strong authorship; Cuarón's signature survived a shift from Hollywood (Gravity) to streaming-funded personal filmmaking, suggesting auteurism now travels across institutional contexts. Note the collaborative and institutional counterweight: national film funds, festival validation and streaming finance shape what gets made. Judgement: auteurism is persuasive where signatures recur across contexts — but global film's institutional conditions constrain it, a reasoned verdict.
Evaluate how digital technology and streaming have changed global film, with reference to your two films. [40]
Answer: AO1: accurate industry context — Netflix funding/exhibition of Roma, limited theatrical release for awards qualification, Parasite's global streaming rights and 2020 Best Picture win; digital cinematography (Roma's 65mm digital monochrome). AO2: bind to film language: streaming finance gave Cuarón full creative control, visible in Roma's deliberate pacing and intimate framing; digital cameras enabled the low-light naturalism of its domestic interiors. Argue the counter-case: Parasite's theatrical genre hybridity and Parasite's commercial breakthrough suggest the cinema-first model still dominates global film's validation (festivals, awards). Judgement: digital and streaming transformed distribution and exhibition faster than production — a reasoned verdict weighing both sides.
Explore how sound and performance generate response in your two global films. [40]
Answer: AO1: accurate terminology (diegetic/non-diegetic, sound bridge, silence, mixing; naturalistic vs non-professional performance) plus accurate detail. AO2: bind to response: Pan's Labyrinth's lullaby leitmotif and diegetic wartime sounds construct wonder and dread in alternation; Roma's immersive ambient mixing (street sounds, the forest fire) positions spectators inside Cleo's acoustic world, making her isolation felt; non-professional performance in City of God (actors cast from the favelas) generates authenticity that professional performance could not; Mustang's ensemble of sisters constructs collective rather than individual identity through performance. Judgement: sound and performance are where these films position spectators most powerfully — the strongest answers judge how far responses are engineered vs negotiated.
