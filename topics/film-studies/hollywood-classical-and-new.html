Hollywood: Classical and New

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level WJEC/Eduqas

A-Level Film Studies revision for Component 1A (Hollywood 1930-1990): classical Hollywood style and the studio system, New Hollywood, auteurism as a critical approach, and the comparative essay technique for the 40-mark question. WJEC/Eduqas A670QS.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Describe the institutional and stylistic features of classical Hollywood (1930-1960) — studio system, continuity editing, genre and star systems — and apply them to the set films (AO1)
  • Explain the collapse of the studio system and the emergence of New Hollywood (1961-1990), including European influence on the new generation of directors
  • Apply the idea of the auteur (spec Area 7) to one Classical and one New Hollywood film, and evaluate it against the collaborative alternative
  • Compare how the contexts of production of the two periods are reflected in film language, narrative and representation (contexts foregrounded)
  • Construct the 40-mark comparative response: integrated comparison, AO1/AO2 in equal measures, precise terminology assessed integrally
  • Deploy knowledge of reception history (Vertigo, Casablanca) to explain how meanings of the films have shifted

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Classical Hollywood style: The coherent, largely invisible style consolidated in the studio era: continuity editing, goal-oriented protagonists, causal linear narrative, balanced mise-en-scène and genre conventions working to absorb the spectator.
Studio system: The 1930s-40s Hollywood arrangement in which the major studios (the Big Five) controlled production, distribution and exhibition, running films through in-house units on factory schedules.
Vertical integration: A studio's ownership of every stage of the industry — production, distribution and cinema exhibition — ended by the 1948 US v. Paramount Pictures antitrust decision.
Paramount decision: The 1948 Supreme Court antitrust ruling that forced studios to divest their cinema chains, beginning the collapse of the studio system and the era's immediate aftermath.
Production Code: The Hays Code of self-censorship governing Hollywood content from 1934 to 1968, constraining representations of sex, violence and morality — and prompting smuggled meanings in films like Some Like It Hot.
New Hollywood: The period 1961-1990 (practically, late 1960s onward) when a new generation of directors, influenced especially by European cinema, produced films with new styles, subjects and freedoms as the studio system waned.
Auteur: A principal creative individual (most commonly the director) whose signature features make a distinctive impact on a film's look, style, messages and values — the critical approach assessed in Component 1A.
Politique des auteurs: The policy of the auteurs developed by critics at Cahiers du Cinéma (Truffaut, 1954), treating the director as the author of a film; imported into Anglophone criticism by Andrew Sarris as auteur theory (1962).
Star system: The studio-era industrial practice of manufacturing and contracting stars as products and guarantees of value — Bette Davis in All About Eve opens up debates about star-as-auteur within Classical Hollywood.
Melodrama: A genre of heightened emotion, domestic conflict and expressive (often ironic) style; Sirk's Hollywood melodramas, culminating in Imitation of Life, pair lush aesthetic with critique of race, class and gender.

📌 Key Points

Component 1A: 40 marks, choice of two questions, must reference two films — one Classical (1930-1960), one New Hollywood (1961-1990).
Specialist study area: auteur; core areas are studied foregrounding contexts; terminology is assessed integrally here.
Comparison is assessed ONLY in this section — Section B/C two-film studies are not comparative.
1948 Paramount decision + television = collapse of vertical integration = conditions for New Hollywood.
Evaluate the auteur: Sarris's three criteria vs the collaborative alternative (cinematographers, editors, producers, stars).

Classical Hollywood Style and the Studio System (1930-1960)

The films of Group 1 were produced during the Hollywood studio era and its immediate aftermath and effectively established the Classical Hollywood style. Its ingredients: continuity editing (eyeline match, 180-degree rule, shot/reverse shot) that makes the cut invisible; a goal-oriented protagonist driving a causal, linear narrative; balanced mise-en-scène and lighting; and the genre and star systems that guaranteed value and legibility. The studio system itself — the Big Five's vertical integration of production, distribution and exhibition — ran films through in-house units on factory schedules, which is precisely the industrial context that makes the auteur question interesting for this component.

Apply the style to the set films. Casablanca (Curtiz, 1942) began life as an unremarkable studio genre picture but has become synonymous with the height of the Classical style: its love-vs-duty narrative resolves into ideological clarity (Rick's neutrality giving way to anti-fascist commitment), and its craft is studio craft — assignment directing, contract players, classical three-act structure with a framing narrative of flashback. Vertigo (Hitchcock, 1958) bends the style around an unstable protagonist: genre cues (noir, romance, psychological thriller) are present, but the narrative withholds a straightforward cause-effect logic and the dolly-zoom, spiralling Herrmann score and obsession-driven structure make Hitchcock's authorship the film's most distinctive feature. All About Eve (Mankiewicz, 1950) is notable for the striking influence of its star, Bette Davis, opening up debates about auteurism within Classical Hollywood: star persona as authorship. Imitation of Life (Sirk, 1959) is the pinnacle of Sirk's Hollywood melodrama — lush, ironic aesthetic (mirrors, staircases, saturated colour) whose expressiveness carries prescient comments on race, class and gender. Some Like It Hot (Wilder, 1959) is a quintessential Hollywood comedy that directly challenged Hollywood's representations of gender and sexuality, testing what the Production Code could contain.

Reception history is examinable context. Casablanca's critical and popular appeal has grown significantly since its first release — an unremarkable assignment-directed genre film that has become the emblem of the Classical style — and Vertigo underwent the more dramatic reversal: on release many saw it as a disappointing film from a major Hollywood studio and famous director, but it is now often seen as the greatest ever example of auteur filmmaking. Reception shifts with time, and the spec expects you to know this. It also expects precision on the industrial conditions: films in the 1940s and 1950s were produced during the studio era and its immediate aftermath, so even a late-Classical film like Imitation of Life (Universal-International, 1959) works within, while stylistically straining against, studio-era norms — Sirk's expressive style is only legible as irony because the Classical style tells us what conformity looks like.

New Hollywood (1961-1990)

The conditions for New Hollywood were institutional: the 1948 Paramount decision forced studios to divest their exhibition chains; television cut audiences and pushed the industry toward spectacle, wider formats and, by the mid-1960s, crisis; the Production Code collapsed into the ratings system (1968). Into this vacuum came a new generation of directors — film-school educated, often called the movie brats — showing new influences, especially from European cinema: the French New Wave's location shooting, jump cuts and reflexive play; European art cinema's ambiguity and open endings.

Group 2 films show the range. Bonnie and Clyde (Penn, 1967) imports a European sensibility into a Hollywood genre: its tonal shifts (comedy into carnage), location shooting, sympathetic outlaw protagonists and the balletic slow-motion violence of the finale mark a break with the Production Code era — its violence and sexuality would have been impossible a decade earlier. Night of the Living Dead (Romero, 1968) is effectively an independent production whose horror conventions carry overt ideology — a Black protagonist cast at the height of the civil rights struggle, and an ending whose newsreel-style resolution indicts society rather than the monsters. One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest (Forman, 1975) is an institutional film with an auteur at its centre: naturalistic performance, hand-held immediacy and an anti-institutional narrative that reads as New Hollywood's challenge to authority (mental institution as metaphor for society). Alien (Scott, 1979) fuses spectacular genre filmmaking (haunted-house-in-space horror, H.R. Giger's design) with directorial signature — its slow, painterly pacing and interior-world mise-en-scène are Scott's. Do the Right Thing (Lee, 1989) is the clearest independent-ideology case: a single-block structure, heightened stylisation (direct address, canted framings, the Love/Hate knuckle-rings), diegetic radio cross-cutting and a climactic riot that refuses the spectator a comfortable ideological resolution.

The comparison the examiner expects: contexts reflected in film language. Classical films encode the studio era's industrial gloss, censorship constraints and stable genres; New Hollywood films encode industrial collapse, new freedoms and European influence — visibly in editing rhythm, location work, moral ambiguity and style-forward direction.

Edit rhythm as the visible marker of the break. Classical films cut to preserve continuity and absorb the spectator; New Hollywood films cut to draw attention — the tonal jump cuts and location immediacy of Bonnie and Clyde, the documentary-style newsreel insert in Night of the Living Dead, the kinetic montage of block life in Do the Right Thing. Compare this directly in the essay: same genre skeletons (the gangster film, the horror film, the institutional drama), different editing rhythm, different freedom of subject. Also note performance: the Classical star system manufactures polished, type-consistent performance; New Hollywood's film-school generation favour naturalism and improvisation — Forman's ensemble in One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest and the street-cast energy of Do the Right Thing mark the shift as clearly as any camera move.

The Idea of the Auteur (Area 7)

The auteur as critical approach derives from critics at Cahiers du Cinéma — Truffaut's politique des auteurs (1954) — who argued films are authored by individuals (most obviously the director) rather than being generic products. Andrew Sarris imported it into Anglophone criticism (1962) with three criteria: technical competence, personal style, interior meaning. The spec asks you to consider: to what extent it is possible to identify the distinctive contribution of creative individuals within a large industrial production process; how far it is appropriate to talk about these individuals as auteurs; and how far it is more appropriate to consider filmmaking as a collaborative process.

Auteur case studies from the set films: Hitchcock (control of camera and suspense structure as psychological instrument — Vertigo is now often seen as the greatest ever example of auteur filmmaking); Sirk (melodrama style as authorship — expressive, ironic mise-en-scène that critiques while appearing to conform); Wilder (genre mastery with subversive content); Penn (European-inflected reinvention of the gangster film); Scott (world-building and painterly style); Lee (stylisation and ideological directness). Also consider Bette Davis as a star-as-auteur argument, and producers/editors as hidden authors.

Evaluate the approach — this is where the 40 marks are won. For: it explains the coherence of style across a director's work (the signature), gives the film a unity of message and value, and rewards close analysis of film language. Against: filmmaking is demonstrably collaborative — cinematographers (Robert Burks shot for Hitchcock), editors, composers (Herrmann), producers and stars all leave imprints; the approach romanticises the director and neglects industry and context (precisely the contexts this section foregrounds); genre products constrain individual signature; it can read intention where none exists. The strongest answers conclude with a reasoned verdict — e.g. auteurism is most persuasive where signature survives institutional constraint (Hitchcock, Sirk) and least persuasive for studio product like Casablanca, whose authorship is collective.

Two refinements for the evaluation. First, signature can be institutional as well as individual: the spec notes the imprint of signature features may include cinematographers, performers or institutions — so MGM's gloss, a studio's genre machinery, or a recurring collaborative team (Hitchcock and Burks; Hitchcock and Herrmann) are all authorial imprints. Second, avoid the intention fallacy: auteurism tempts you to read intention where accident or collaboration explains the effect. Test every signature claim against the evidence: does the feature recur across the director's work? Does it survive different genres, budgets and crews? If yes, the auteur claim is strong; if the feature appears only in one genre product, the collaborative or genre explanation is stronger — and saying so earns the evaluation marks rather than losing them.

The Component 1A Comparative Essay

Section A is worth 40 marks (choice of two questions), requires reference to two Hollywood films — one from Group 1, one from Group 2 — and assesses AO1 and AO2 in equal measures. Three features distinguish it from the other sections: comparison is assessed (nowhere else); subject-specific terminology is assessed integrally; and questions are based on the core study areas foregrounding contexts plus the auteur. Questions typically name the specialist focus (e.g. contexts of production, auteur, narrative, representation of an issue) and sometimes quote a critic or a statement to evaluate.

Structure for A*:

  1. Thesis-driven introduction that answers the question directly and names both films, directors and periods.
  2. Integrated comparison throughout — comparative connectives (whereas, conversely, similarly, by contrast) in every paragraph; never two half-essays. Anchor each point in precise moments from both films.
  3. Context woven, not listed: the studio system and censorship for Group 1; industrial collapse, ratings, European influence for Group 2 — each attached to a specific technique (VistaVision gloss vs location grain; balanced continuity vs jump-cut rhythm).
  4. Auteur applied and evaluated: signature features for each director, then judge the approach against the collaborative alternative.
  5. Conclusion with a reasoned verdict that answers the question's command words (how far, evaluate, discuss).

Typical question shapes: Compare the contexts of production of your two films and how far these are reflected in film language. How far is it more appropriate to see your two films as the work of auteurs than as collaborative products? Compare how narrative or representation in your two films reflects their social and historical contexts. Prepare both films in terms of two sequences each mapped to: context, auteur, narrative, representation — that quadruple coverage lets you answer any of the two offered questions.

Band awareness. Answers that describe the two periods separately sit in the middle bands; top bands require the comparison to be embedded (both films in the introduction and in most paragraphs), the auteur approach evaluated rather than merely described, and terminology used precisely and integrally — the spec explicitly states that terminology is assessed in an integral way through this comparative study. Timing: 40 marks in roughly 55-60 minutes; budget four to five analytical paragraphs plus introduction and conclusion, and prepare both films so that whichever question focus is offered (contexts, auteur, narrative, representation), each film has ready-made, precisely-located material.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: It is the director who is the true author of a film. With reference to your two Hollywood films, how far do you agree? [40]
Mark-scheme logic: 40 marks = AO1 20 + AO2 20, equal measures; Section A also assesses comparison and the validity of the auteur approach, with terminology assessed integrally (A670QS Component 1A).<br><br><strong>AO1 - knowledge and understanding (20):</strong> accurate account of the auteur approach and its origins (Cahiers du Cinéma, Truffaut's politique des auteurs, Sarris's criteria); accurate institutional contexts — the studio system and vertical integration for the Classical film, the Paramount decision, television and European influence for the New Hollywood film; accurate detail from both films (directors, dates, sequences, signature features); knowledge of the collaborative alternative (cinematographers, editors, composers, producers, stars).<br><br><strong>AO2 - application and evaluation (20):</strong> integrated comparison throughout (comparative connectives, points anchored in both films); signature features identified and explained for each director (e.g. Hitchcock's camera-as-psychological-instrument vs Penn's European-inflected genre revisionism); context woven to explain how far each director's signature survived institutional constraint; explicit evaluation of the auteur approach — its explanatory power vs its neglect of collaboration, genre and industry — and a reasoned verdict answering the how far command word.<br><br><strong>Top-band shape:</strong> a direct thesis in the introduction; every paragraph compares; the verdict is not an afterthought but the culmination of the evidence (e.g. auteurism persuasive for Sirk/Hitchcock where style survived the studio; less persuasive for studio product like Casablanca).

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Compare how far the contexts of production of your two Hollywood films are reflected in their film language. [40]
Answer: AO1: accurate contexts — for the Classical film, the studio system (Big Five, vertical integration), assignment directing, the Production Code, classical genre/star systems; for the New Hollywood film, the post-Paramount industrial collapse, television, the ratings system, European influence and the movie-brat generation. AO2: bind each context to technique: studio gloss and balanced continuity (Casablanca's classical coverage and stable genre resolution) vs location work, tonal instability and slow-motion violence (Bonnie and Clyde's finale as impossible a decade earlier); censorship explains smuggled meanings in Some Like It Hot's costume and performance, while the ratings era explains Alien's explicit body horror. Integrated comparison throughout; conclude by judging how far institutional change is visible on screen — strongly so in editing, location and moral ambiguity, less so in the persistence of genre.
Classical Hollywood style is an invisible style. With reference to your chosen Classical Hollywood film, discuss. [40]
Answer: AO1: define the style precisely — continuity editing (eyeline match, 180-degree rule, shot/reverse shot), causal linear narrative, goal-oriented protagonist, balanced mise-en-scène, classical score. AO2: argue both sides. For: Vertigo's early sequences observe the system (cutting preserves space, narrative sets a goal) — the style works invisibly to absorb the spectator. Against: Vertigo's celebrated moments break the ideal — the dolly-zoom makes the camera visible as a psychological instrument; the spiralling Herrmann score is expressive, not subordinate; narrative withholds clear cause and effect. Judgement: the Classical style is the baseline against which the film's deviations become meaningful; invisible style is best understood as the default the auteur visibly bends.
Compare the narrative construction of your Classical Hollywood film and your New Hollywood film. [40]
Answer: AO1: narrative terminology — causal linear structure, framing narrative, flashback, open ending, ellipsis, goal-oriented protagonist; accurate detail from both films. AO2: build the comparison on a thesis — the Classical film closes its narrative into ideological clarity (Casablanca resolves love into duty; the framing flashback returns Rick to commitment), while the New Hollywood film destabilises cause and effect, ends ambiguously or indicts society (Night of the Living Dead's newsreel-style ending; Bonnie and Clyde's abrupt, unheroic close); European art cinema's open endings and ambiguity inform the later film's structure. Note how mise-en-scène, editing and sound carry narrative function in both. Judgement: conclude how far the two films share the three-act skeleton while diverging in closure, ambiguity and spectator alignment.
Evaluate the usefulness of the auteur approach for understanding one of your Hollywood films. [40]
Answer: AO1: the approach's origins and claims (Truffaut, Sarris's three criteria), plus accurate film detail. AO2: argue for — signature coherence across the director's work (Hitchcock's suspense structure, Sirk's ironic melodrama style), unity of message and value, the approach's attention to film language. Argue against — collaboration (Robert Burks's cinematography, Herrmann's score, the studio's genre machinery), the approach's neglect of industry and context, and its romantic myth of the director. Verdict: most useful where signature survives institutional constraint (Vertigo), least useful for studio product (Casablanca, whose authorship is collective); the approach is a lens, not a complete explanation.
Compare the representation of gender in your two Hollywood films. [40]
Answer: AO1: accurate detail — Some Like It Hot's cross-dressing and Production Code constraints; All About Eve's older-woman/star dynamics; Bonnie and Clyde's reworking of the outlaw couple; Imitation of Life's gendered melodrama. AO2: bind representation to film language — costume and performance construct gender in Some Like It Hot (and the comedy smuggles sexuality past censorship), while framing, mirrors and staircases in Sirk's melodrama construct femininity as performance under class and racial pressure; the New Hollywood film redistributes gender power (Bonnie's agency within a violent partnership) against the Classical film's containment of it. Judgement: the two periods' censorship and industrial contexts explain the difference — representation changed because the Code fell, and the strongest answers tie each representational claim to a technique.
