Filmmaking and Screenwriting (NEA)

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level WJEC/Eduqas

A-Level Film Studies revision for Component 3 (Production, NEA, 30%): short film or screenplay requirements, the digitally photographed storyboard, the production log, and how to write the 1600-1800 word evaluative analysis against the WJEC marking criteria.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • State the Component 3 requirements precisely: short film (4-5 minutes) OR screenplay (1600-1800 words) plus digitally photographed storyboard of a key sequence, and an evaluative analysis (1600-1800 words) (AO1/AO3)
  • Apply narrative conventions of mainstream screenwriting — the narrative arc, three-act structure, dialogue, character and the use of images and sound to convey narrative (Area 5)
  • Keep an unassessed production portfolio (blog or Pinterest-style) covering short-film study, character/narrative ideas and film-language research
  • Write the evaluative analysis against its three required strands: narrative structure of the short film, cinematic influences, and creating meaning and effect
  • Apply the storyboard conventions — shot number, indicative image, framing, duration, mise-en-scène detail, editing techniques and sound details
  • Explain the conditions of the NEA: individual unaided work, 13-15 weeks recommended, the production brief's four options, and the AO3 weighting (30%)

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Non-exam assessment (NEA): Component 3: Production — internally assessed, externally moderated coursework worth 30% of the qualification (40 marks for the production, 20 for the evaluative analysis).
Production brief: The annually-set brief offering four options, of which one must be chosen — two based on narrative elements and two on character-led elements of the short film.
Master scene script: The standard screenplay format: single column, wide margins, sequential page numbering, centred dialogue with character names in upper case, sluglines in upper case, Courier 12-point, action in present tense.
Slugline: The scene heading: interior/exterior indicator (INT., EXT., INT/EXT), location descriptor and lighting descriptor (DAY, NIGHT or TIME), followed by the scene/action descriptor.
Digitally photographed storyboard: The screenplay option's companion: a photographed storyboard of a key section of the screenplay (up to 2 minutes of screen time, approximately two pages, approximately 20 storyboard shots).
Storyboard conventions: Shot number plus indicative image incorporating intended framing; duration of shot; mise-en-scène detail and camera movement; specific editing techniques (e.g. DISSOLVE TO); sound details (diegetic or non-diegetic, but not dialogue); landscape mode; up to five credited found shots.
Evaluative analysis: The 1600-1800 word written analysis of the production in relation to professionally produced films, including at least three short films totalling a minimum of 80 minutes from the WJEC compilation.
Narrative arc: The overall shape of a story from setup through conflict to resolution — the practical screenwriting concept behind the commonly used three-act structure (Area 5).
Character-led option: A production brief option grounded in character-driven narrative elements, where dramatic qualities, motivation and psychological insight drive the story rather than plot events.
Film poetics: What elements of film filmmakers select and how they combine them to create meaning (Area 5) — the concept underpinning both the production and its evaluative analysis.

📌 Key Points

Component 3: 30% of qualification, 80 marks total — production 40, evaluative analysis 20, plus intermediate criteria.
Option 1: short film (4-5 minutes). Option 2: screenplay (1600-1800 words) + digitally photographed storyboard of a key sequence (~2 minutes, ~20 shots).
Evaluative analysis: 1600-1800 words; must reference at least three short films totalling a minimum of 80 minutes from the WJEC compilation (Appendix A).
All production work must be individual and demonstrably the candidate's own, unaided work; misuse of AI is malpractice.
Learners are responsible for camerawork and editing of their short film — it is recommended that learners do not act in their own film.

Component 3: What You Must Produce

Component 3 is a synoptic NEA worth 30% of the qualification: one production and its evaluative analysis. You produce either a short film (4-5 minutes) or a screenplay for a short film (1600-1800 words) plus a digitally photographed storyboard of a key section from the screenplay — and an evaluative analysis (1600-1800 words). The production is marked out of 40, the evaluative analysis out of 20, using the marking criteria in Appendix A of the specification.

The production brief (set for a defined period, e.g. first assessment Summer 2023, last Summer 2026; the Summer 2027 brief publishes on the WJEC Portal) offers four options, of which one must be chosen — two based on narrative elements and two on character-led elements of the short film. Read the brief precisely: your production must demonstrably answer the option chosen.

  • Conditions: complete the production within approximately 13-15 weeks to balance NEA against the exam components; all production work must be individual and demonstrably your own, unaided work; teacher support is limited to general advice on one draft of the evaluative analysis — no specific guidance, no creative decisions from teachers. Misuse of AI is malpractice — follow the Eduqas 'Misuse of AI in Assessment' guidance.
  • Filmmaking option: you must be responsible for the camerawork and editing; it is recommended that you do not act in your own film (performance is not assessed); unassessed participants may act; unassessed assistants may operate lighting/sound under your direction; live action or animation is permitted, but animation must not use video-game software or pre-populated characters/backgrounds.
  • Screenplay option: the screenplay must be for a live action film (to fulfil the storyboard), in standard master scene script format.

Marks and weighting in detail. The production is marked out of 40 and the evaluative analysis out of 20, using the criteria in Appendix A; AO3 (apply knowledge and understanding of elements of film to the production of a film or screenplay) is the NEA's own objective, but AO2 applies through the evaluative analysis — analysing and evaluating your own work in relation to professionally produced work. Internally assessed by your centre (standardised if more than one teacher), externally moderated by WJEC; sample assessed work is made available on the Eduqas website. Timing discipline: the recommended 13-15 weeks splits roughly into research/scripting (4-5 weeks), production (4-5 weeks) and the evaluative analysis (3-4 weeks) — and the evaluative analysis should be written while the production is fresh, not as an afterthought. Check the coversheet requirements early: the short films analysed must be listed on it, and both teachers and learners authenticate the conditions on it.

Short Film Narrative and Screenwriting Craft

The spec sets a compilation of contrasting short films for study (Appendix A: e.g. Meshes of the Afternoon, La Jetée, The Grandmother, Elephant (Clarke, 1989), The Wrong Trousers, About a Girl, Wasp, Two Cars, One Night, Night Fishing, Pitch Black Heist, Curfew, Swimmer, the Wild Tales compilation, Stutterer, The Silent Child, Hair Love, The Human Voice) to develop knowledge of the short film form, particularly its distinctive narrative. Study the set shorts for what they teach: economy of setup, single-location or single-relationship focus, the dramatic weight of endings, how dialogue is rationed, and how images and sound carry narrative without exposition.

Screenwriting craft (Area 5): the narrative arc and the commonly used three-act structure (setup — confrontation — resolution); how narrative construction provides psychological insight into character; how the dramatic qualities of a sequence are constructed, including through dialogue; and the narrative conventions of mainstream screenwriting — construction of dialogue, character, and the use of images and sound to convey narrative. For a 4-5 minute short film, structure compresses: establish a character and a want within 30-60 seconds, escalate through one central conflict, and land an ending that re-frames what came before (the Curfew/Stutterer model). In screenplay format: each scene numbered with a slugline (INT./EXT. + location + DAY/NIGHT/TIME), action in present tense, dialogue centred with character names in upper case, Courier 12-point — and screenplays do not include camera directions except essential instructions in exceptional circumstances (e.g. SLO-MO).

Dialogue craft deserves its own attention because the spec singles it out — how the dramatic qualities of a sequence are constructed, including through dialogue. In short film, dialogue is rationed: every line either reveals character, escalates conflict, or withholds — the Curfew model (a phone call reframed by an ending) or Stutterer (a voice heard but not spoken) demonstrate dialogue as dramatic structure rather than exposition. The Silent Child makes silence itself the dramatic device — BSL signing as performance and the hearing world's failure as theme. For the screenplay option, dialogue centred with upper-case speaker names is also where format is most visible to markers. And for both options, images and sound carry narrative: a sound bridge across a cut, a recurring diegetic noise (the kettle, the clock), a visual motif (a doorway, a reflection) — the spec's film poetics concept: what you select and how you combine it is the meaning.

The Production Log and Evaluative Analysis

Keep an unassessed portfolio throughout the course (electronic — a blog or Pinterest page) consisting of: notes on or visuals from the short films studied that could inform your production; character(s) and narrative ideas; examples of cinematography, mise-en-scène, editing and sound that could inform the production; effective screenplay techniques (for the screenplay option); and examples of storyboards (if taking the screenplay option). The log is not marked, but it is the raw material for the evaluative analysis — and evidence of authentic, individual development.

The evaluative analysis (1600-1800 words) must analyse and evaluate the production in relation to other professionally produced films or screenplays, including at least three short films totalling a minimum of 80 minutes from the compilation set by WJEC — listed on the coversheet. Its three required strands:

  1. Narrative structure of the short film — analyse how the narrative features and dramatic qualities of all short films studied are constructed, including through dialogue, highlighting key ideas which informed your own production.
  2. Cinematic influences — analyse how key visual/audio elements of other professionally produced films or screenplays, including the short films studied, influenced your short film.
  3. Creating meaning and effect — an evaluative analysis of how your production creates meanings and generates responses for the spectator in relation to other professionally produced films or screenplays.

What examiners reward: specific, named influences applied precisely (not genre mentions); film-language precision in describing your own work (shot types, editing techniques, sound design choices — by their proper names); the three strands each substantively addressed; honest evaluation — what worked, what did not, and why, in relation to the professional films; extended word-processed writing (sub-headings permitted, screen shots may illustrate). One teacher-annotated draft is permitted; the evaluative analysis is completed under supervised conditions (not exam conditions), and must be listed on the coversheet with the short films analysed.

Worked example of strand logic — a production about an isolated teenager in a coastal town, choosing a character-led brief option: Strand 1 would analyse About a Girl's monologue-driven structure, Wasp's observational family realism and Curfew's reveal-ending, drawing from each a structural principle (monologue as spine; environment as character; ending as reframe) and stating how each informed the production. Strand 2 would name precise influences: Wasp's available-light handheld staging informing the town's restricted follow shots; The Silent Child's sound design (silence against diegetic detail) informing the film's soundscape; Saint Maud-style God's-eye framing (a feature film may also be referenced) informing the final aerial insert. Strand 3 would evaluate: how the restricted framing generates the spectator's enclosure, where the reveal-ending re-frames the film, and honestly — which techniques worked and which did not, judged against the professional films. Specific, named, precise — that is the difference between the middle and top bands.

The Storyboard and Filming Rules

The screenplay option's digitally photographed storyboard must cover a key section of the screenplay — up to 2 minutes of screen time, corresponding to approximately two pages and approximately 20 storyboard shots — and demonstrate how the screenplay will be realised. Images may be indicative and need not employ a realistic mise-en-scène; Photoshop of backgrounds is permitted but not assessed; up to five found shots may be used where photography would not be possible (source must be credited); images are presented in landscape mode; a template is available on the Eduqas website.

Storyboard conventions — the storyboard must include:

  • shot number plus indicative image of the main shot (e.g. close-up of face);
  • the image must incorporate the intended framing (e.g. close-up of face ranged right);
  • duration of shot (or a timeline);
  • visual details including mise-en-scène detail and any camera movement within the shot;
  • specific editing techniques beyond simple cuts (e.g. DISSOLVE TO);
  • sound details referencing diegetic or non-diegetic sound — but not dialogue.

Filming rules for the short-film option: you are responsible for camerawork and editing; it is recommended you do not act in your own film (unassessed participants may act; performance skills are not assessed); lighting and sound may be operated by unassisted assistants under your direction; live action or animation is permitted, but animation must not use video-game software or software with pre-populated characters and backgrounds. The production encourages an appropriate range of camera shots and editing techniques, applying your knowledge and understanding of cinematography, mise-en-scène, editing and sound — the AO3 criteria reward application of film language, not production values alone. Plan coverage of the micro-elements deliberately: a film that shows controlled framing, motivated camera movement, purposeful editing (beyond cuts) and designed sound scores across the production criteria.

Common mistakes to avoid — worth knowing as a checklist before submission: acting in your own film (recommended against; keep performance to unassessed participants); including camera directions liberally in the screenplay (permitted only exceptionally); dialogue in storyboard sound details (excluded — storyboard sound is diegetic/non-diegetic detail only); portrait-mode storyboard images (must be landscape); uncredited found shots (must be credited, maximum five); evaluative analysis that describes the production without relation to the professional films (the relation is the assessment); evaluative analysis under or over the 1600-1800 word band (stay inside it); and missing coversheet details (short films analysed must be listed). Finally, keep the unassessed portfolio current as you go — it is the raw material of the evaluative analysis, and examiners' sample work on the Eduqas website shows that top-band productions are planned around the micro-elements (cinematography, mise-en-scène, editing, sound) rather than assembled around a story alone.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: Evaluate your production in relation to at least three of the short films studied, analysing how your narrative structure, cinematic influences and use of film form create meaning and generate response for the spectator. [20 — Component 3 evaluative analysis]
Mark-scheme logic: 20 marks = the evaluative analysis marking criteria (Appendix A of the A670QS spec): analyse and evaluate own work in relation to other professionally produced work (AO2), applied through the production (AO3).<br><br><strong>Strand 1 - narrative structure of the short film:</strong> analysis of how the narrative features and dramatic qualities of all short films studied are constructed (including through dialogue) — e.g. the economy of setup in Curfew, the single-relationship focus of Stutterer, the reveal-ending logic of About a Girl — with key ideas explicitly linked to how they informed your own production's structure (arc, acts, ending).<br><br><strong>Strand 2 - cinematic influences:</strong> specific, named influences applied precisely — not 'I was influenced by horror films' but 'the available-light handheld staging of Wasp informed my kitchen sequence's restricted perspective' — covering visual and audio elements (cinematography, mise-en-scène, editing, sound) with the micro-features named by their proper terms.<br><br><strong>Strand 3 - creating meaning and effect:</strong> an evaluative analysis of how your production creates meanings and generates spectator response in relation to the professional films — the technique → connotation → response chain applied to your own work, with honest evaluation of what worked and what did not, judged against the short films studied.<br><br><strong>Craft requirements:</strong> 1600-1800 words; at least three short films totalling a minimum of 80 minutes from the WJEC compilation, listed on the coversheet; extended writing with optional sub-headings and screen shots; the three strands each substantively addressed rather than one dominating.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Your production brief offers two narrative-led and two character-led options. How should the choice shape your short film's structure?
Answer: Narrative-led options are plot-driven: structure around escalation of events — establish the situation fast, compress the arc (setup within 30-60 seconds, one central conflict, a re-framing ending), and let images and sound carry narrative without exposition. Character-led options are driven by dramatic qualities and psychological insight: structure around a want and its obstacle, ration dialogue so performance and framing carry meaning, and let the ending turn on a character decision rather than a plot reveal. Either way the spec's Area 5 applies — the narrative arc, the three-act skeleton, and the dramatic qualities of sequences including through dialogue. Answer the brief option chosen demonstrably: examiners mark against the brief.
What are the formatting requirements of the screenplay, and why do they matter?
Answer: Master scene script layout: single column with wide margins; sequential page numbering (top right); 'mf' (more follows) bottom right; dialogue centred with speaker's name in upper case; slugline and sound in upper case; character names in upper case on first appearance only; Courier 12-point; each scene numbered with a slugline (INT./EXT. + location descriptor + DAY/NIGHT/TIME); scene/action descriptor with succinct character description on first appearance; action in present tense; screenplays do not include camera directions (only essential instructions in exceptional circumstances, e.g. SLO-MO). They matter because they are assessed: format conventions demonstrate professional screenwriting competence and make your realisation intentions legible — and the storyboard depends on them (up to 2 minutes of screen time corresponds to approximately two pages).
How do you select the three short films for the evaluative analysis, and what must you do with them?
Answer: Select at least three short films totalling a minimum of 80 minutes from the WJEC compilation (Appendix A) — e.g. Curfew (19'), Pitch Black Heist (13') and Night Fishing (33') exceed 80 minutes with three films; or a four-film mix of shorter titles. They must be listed on the coversheet. Then use them across all three strands: analyse the narrative features and dramatic qualities of all films studied (including through dialogue), analyse how their key visual/audio elements influenced your production, and evaluate how your production creates meaning and response in relation to them. Name specific techniques and specific scenes — general influence claims ('I liked its style') earn nothing.
What does the digitally photographed storyboard have to include to score well?
Answer: Approximately 20 shots covering a key section of the screenplay (up to 2 minutes of screen time, about two pages): shot number plus an indicative image of the main shot; the image incorporating the intended framing (e.g. close-up of face ranged right); duration of shot or a timeline; visual details of the mise-en-scène plus any camera movement within the shot; specific editing techniques beyond simple cuts (e.g. DISSOLVE TO); and sound details referencing diegetic or non-diegetic sound — but not dialogue. Images may be indicative and need not employ a realistic mise-en-scène; up to five credited found shots are permitted; present in landscape mode. Score comes from completeness against these conventions and the precision of the framing/sound/editing detail.
What are the conditions of the NEA — what can your teacher do, and what is malpractice?
Answer: The production is individual and must be demonstrably your own, unaided work, completed within approximately 13-15 weeks. Teachers may provide general advice on areas of possible improvement for one draft version of the evaluative analysis — but no specific or detailed guidance on improvements, and no creative decisions. Learners must complete the evaluative analysis under supervised conditions (not exam conditions). For the filmmaking option you are responsible for camerawork and editing; unassessed participants may act; unassessed assistants may operate lighting/sound under your direction. Misuse of AI is malpractice — formal investigation following JCQ procedures can withdraw marks for the component or the whole qualification. Work is retained in centre until December of the following year for moderation checks.
