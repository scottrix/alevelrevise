Documentary Film

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level WJEC/Eduqas

A-Level Film Studies revision for Component 2B (Documentary film): Nichols' documentary modes, the filmmakers' theories (Longinotto, Broomfield, Moore, Watkins), the set documentaries, and the critical debate on digital technology. WJEC/Eduqas A670QS 20-mark question.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define documentary through Grierson's 'creative treatment of actuality' and apply Bill Nichols' six modes (poetic, expository, observational, participatory, performative, reflexive) to the set films (AO1)
  • Apply two of the spec's filmmakers' theories (Longinotto, Broomfield, Moore, Watkins) to the documentary studied — where it embodies or challenges them
  • Analyse how documentary conventions (voiceover, interviews, archive footage, observational camera, reconstruction) construct meaning and generate response
  • Evaluate the critical debate on the impact of digital technology on film, applying it to the documentary studied (Area 8, Debate 2)
  • Analyse how the fact/fiction divide is problematised in contemporary documentary practice
  • Construct the 20-mark response: core areas plus critical debates and filmmakers' theories, AO1/AO2 in equal measures

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Documentary: A film genre claiming a factual relationship to its subject; the spec frames the contemporary form as 'much freer', using cinematic techniques creatively while problematising the divide between fact and fiction.
Creative treatment of actuality: John Grierson's classic definition of documentary — a crafted (not neutral) treatment of real material — which already contains the genre's central problem: construction enters from the start.
Expository mode: Nichols' term for documentaries arguing a point through voiceover, titles and interviews that address the spectator directly and organise footage to a thesis (e.g. classic broadcast documentary).
Observational mode: Nichols' term for documentaries committed to capturing reality played out before the camera — fly-on-the-wall, minimal intervention, no voiceover or captions; Sisters in Law is the set-film case.
Participatory mode: Nichols' term for documentaries in which the filmmaker interacts with subjects — interviews, encounters, on-screen presence; Broomfield and Moore are the spec's participatory filmmakers.
Performative mode: Nichols' term for documentaries foregrounding the filmmaker's subjective, expressive engagement with the subject — knowledge as personal experience; For Sama combines performative and observational modes.
Reflexive mode: Nichols' term for documentaries that make their own construction the subject — drawing attention to filmmaking itself; Stories We Tell's self-reflexive approach and Exit Through the Gift Shop's provocation are the cases.
Monoform: Peter Watkins' term for mainstream media's relentless, undifferentiated visual form, which he opposes with alternative structures — his critique sits behind his docu-dramas Culloden and The War Game.
Agit-prop: Agitation-propaganda filmmaking taking up an overt point of view — the spec's description of Michael Moore's committed, polemical documentaries.
Archive footage: Pre-existing recorded material incorporated into a documentary — home video in Amy, newsreel and home video in For Sama — raising questions of authorship, ethics and (with digital) access.

Set films: Sisters in Law, Exit Through the Gift Shop, Stories We Tell, Amy, For Sama (assessment from Summer 2025).

Documentary Modes and Conventions

John Grierson defined documentary as the creative treatment of actuality — a definition worth memorising because it already contains the genre's central problem: documentary is constructed from the start, so the question is never whether it constructs but how openly. Bill Nichols systematised the genre into six modes, and the spec's set films map onto them precisely.

  • Poetic — expressive, associative organisation over argument (fragments of tone and image).
  • Expository — voiceover, titles and interviews organise footage to a thesis; the classic broadcast mode; 'the voice-of-God' narration that answers questions before the spectator asks them.
  • Observational — fly-on-the-wall capture of reality with minimal intervention; Sisters in Law is the most conventional of the set films, an observational documentary committed to capturing the reality played out in front of the camera.
  • Participatory — the filmmaker interacts with subjects through interviews and encounters; Broomfield and Moore are the spec's exemplars.
  • Performative — subjective, expressive engagement; knowledge as personal experience; For Sama combines performative and observational modes.
  • Reflexive — the film's own construction becomes the subject; Stories We Tell's self-reflexive investigation into her family, and Exit Through the Gift Shop's provocation about the nature of documentary film, are the cases.

Conventions to analyse as film language: voiceover (presence, authority, reliability), formal interviews (framing, eyeline, staging), archive footage (authorship, ethics, context), reconstruction (Watkins' docu-drama method), observational camera (handheld, available light), captions and graphics, and the near-absent score of observational work. Each convention is a choice about the film's relationship to truth — bind every one to meaning and response.

Modes are not boxes — films combine them, and the spec's set films do: For Sama is performative-observational; Stories We Tell is reflexive-participatory; Amy is expository in structure but archival in method; Exit Through the Gift Shop is reflexive with a participatory core. For the exam, name the dominant mode, note the combination, and analyse what the combination does — e.g. For Sama's performative voiceover inside observational footage means subjective testimony and witnessed reality reinforce each other, so the film's claim to truth is doubled. Also know the genre's history as context in one sentence: actuality films of the Lumière brothers vs the fantasy films of Méliès — the earliest documentary debate, later systematised by Grierson's British documentary movement and Bazin's realism.

Filmmakers' Theories (Area 9)

The documentary is explored in relation to key filmmakers from the genre; two of the following theories must be chosen for study — the film studied may directly embody aspects of them or strongly challenge them. In either case the theories provide the means of exploring different approaches to documentary.

Kim Longinotto (Sisters in Law): 'I don't think of films as documents or records of things. I try to make them as like the experience of watching a fiction film as possible, though, of course, nothing is ever set up.' Her work finds characters the audience will identify with — 'you can make this jump into someone else's experience'. Unlike Moore and Broomfield, Longinotto is invisible, with very little use of voice-over, formal interviews, captions or incidental music. As the 'eyes' of her audience, she doesn't like to zoom or pan; she says she doesn't want her films to have conclusions but to raise questions. Sisters in Law embodies this theory: observational, immersive, judgement-free, built on identification with its subjects (the judges Vera Ngassa and Beatrice Ntuba, and the women and girls whose cases they resolve).

Michael Moore: a very visible presence; participatory and performative; highly committed — overtly polemical, taking up a clear point of view, agit-prop documentary. He justifies his practice as providing 'balance' for mainstream media which, in his view, provides false information; his humour lampoons subjects and recognises that documentaries must entertain. Exit Through the Gift Shop challenges Moore's model from a different direction: Banksy's film is itself a provocation — its narrator-author relationship (Thierry Guetta's transformation into 'Mr Brainwash') may be a stunt, forcing the spectator to question the nature of documentary film.

Nick Broomfield: participatory, performative investigative documentarist with a distinctive interview technique used to expose people's real views; keeps the filmmaking presence minimal (crew of no more than three); his films are 'like a rollercoaster ride... a diary into the future'. Peter Watkins: established his reputation with the docu-dramas Culloden and The War Game (both documenting events using actors and reconstruction), asking questions of conventional documentary out of deep concern with mainstream media, which he calls the 'monoform'.

How to use the theories in the exam. The question will ask about your documentary in relation to the debates and the theories. The method: state the theory (with its key claims, and the spec's quotations where memorable); test the film against it — embodies, extends or challenges; then analyse film language as evidence. Sisters in Law is the cleanest embodiment of Longinotto: no zooms or pans (she is the 'eyes' of her audience), no incidental music or captions, conclusions replaced by questions — so spectators make the judgements the judges make. Exit Through the Gift Shop is the cleanest challenge to all four theories: its authorship is the question, so no theory explains it fully — and arguing that is a strong evaluative move. If you study Moore or Broomfield, be ready to contrast the visible filmmaker's polemic with Longinotto's invisibility as alternative ethics of documentary address.

The Set Documentaries: Fact, Fiction and Form

Sisters in Law (Ayisi and Longinotto, Cameroon/UK, 2005) is the most conventional of the choices — an observational documentary set in Cameroon, following women working in the legal system as judges and lawyers, focusing on resolving family cases including wife abuse and child cruelty. Its film language is Longinotto's theory in practice: available-light handheld camera, no zooms or pans, near-absent score, no captions or voiceover — so spectators judge as the judges do, watching reality unfold. Stories We Tell (Polley, Canada, 2012) engages in an investigation into Polley's own family — specifically the identity of her genetic father — innovative in its commitment to a self-reflexive approach: reconstruction, home-movie pastiche, interviews with family members who sometimes contradict the film itself, so the film's own construction becomes its subject and the fact/fiction divide dissolves by design.

Amy (Kapadia, UK, 2015) takes a different approach to a singer's life: composed almost entirely of home video footage, brought together to offer a compelling portrait of its subject — an archival film whose authorship lies in selection and sequencing, whose intimacy is genuine, and whose ethics (Winehouse's exploitation by media, management and family) are carried by the assembly itself. For Sama (Al-Kateab, UK, 2019) combines performative and observational modes to document a young mother's experience during the Battle of Aleppo — five years of footage shot inside the siege, structured as a letter to her daughter, so the subjective voice is the film's form. Exit Through the Gift Shop (Banksy, UK, 2010) is the street artist's provocative statement about the value of art that forces the spectator to question the nature of documentary film — is Guetta's story real, staged, or both?

All five 'frequently make the division between fact and fiction ambiguous and exploit digital technology' — the spec's phrasing. Map each film to its mode(s), its filmmaker-theory relationship, and two sequences where film language carries its claim to truth.

Ethics per film — one sentence each, exam-ready: Sisters in Law represents suffering (wife abuse, child cruelty cases) through observation — consent and dignity are its ethical questions, and its subjects' agency (the judges' authority) is part of its answer. For Sama films a war from inside a family's life — the ethics of filming a child in a siege, resolved by the film's own framing as a mother's letter. Amy constructs a portrait from archive the subject never consented to as a film — its ethics lie in indicting the exploitation it also participates in. Stories We Tell films family members without full control of the outcome — consent to the process, not the result. Exit Through the Gift Shop makes its subject's consent itself the puzzle. Weave one of these into any response-analysis paragraph — ethics is where documentary's meaning and response meet.

Critical Debate: Digital Technology, Realism and Ethics

Critical Debate 2 (Area 8): the significance of digital technology in film, studied in relation to the documentary film. The debate: some commentators argue that although digital technology could potentially transform cinema, narrative films designed for cinema release have changed little from pre-digital times; others consider the impact of digital filmmaking is only beginning to emerge, in high-concept Hollywood and in low-budget experimental work. Documentary is the genre where the digital's impact is clearest — apply it to your film:

  • Access: lightweight digital cameras enabled For Sama's five years of footage inside a siege — physically impossible on film stock with a crew.
  • Archive: digital storage and home video enabled Amy's composition from found footage; digital restoration makes archive usable at feature length.
  • Distribution/exhibition: streaming and online release changed documentary's audience and economics — For Sama premiered at Cannes/SXSW and reached global audiences via Channel 4 and PBS; Stories We Tell and Amy built their reputations partly online.
  • Manipulation: digital editing and (increasingly) synthetic imagery make the fact/fiction divide harder to police — Stories We Tell's fabricated home movies exploit exactly this; Exit Through the Gift Shop stages the question.

Realism and ethics complete the analysis. Documentary claims a relationship to the real — observational camera, natural light and absence of reconstruction are realism's grammar (Sisters in Law), while voiceover, staging and reconstruction signal construction (Stories We Tell, Watkins' docu-dramas). Ethics: consent, representation of suffering (the abuse cases in Sisters in Law; the siege in For Sama), the subject's exploitation (Amy), and the filmmaker's presence (Moore's polemic vs Longinotto's invisibility). The 20-mark question assesses AO1 and AO2 in equal measures: define the terms, apply the theories, analyse film language, and reach a reasoned judgement on the debate or statement offered.

Realism's grammar, expanded. The realist vs expressive debate (Bazin's lineage: the Lumière actuality vs Méliès fantasy; German Expressionism and Soviet Montage against cinema's 'realist calling') underwrites documentary's choices: observational camera, natural light and long takes are realist; stylisation, reconstruction and expressive score are expressive. For Sama and Sisters in Law sit at the realist pole; Stories We Tell and Exit Through the Gift Shop weaponise the expressive pole against the genre's own claims. Band awareness for 2B: 20 marks in roughly 25-30 minutes — AO1 (define, locate, know the theory) and AO2 (apply, analyse, judge) in equal measures. Middle-band answers assert the debate; top-band answers apply it to specific film-language evidence and weigh both sides in the verdict. Prepare your documentary in terms of: dominant mode, filmmaker-theory relationship, two sequences, and one digital-technology application — that quadruple coverage answers either of the two offered questions.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: Evaluate the impact of digital technology on documentary film, with reference to your documentary film. [20]
Mark-scheme logic: 20 marks = AO1 10 + AO2 10, equal measures; Component 2B assesses core areas, critical debates (digital technology) and filmmakers' theories (A670QS Component 2B).<br><br><strong>AO1 - knowledge and understanding (10):</strong> accurate account of the digital debate (both sides: little changed vs impact only beginning); accurate knowledge of the documentary studied (director, date, mode(s), production context); knowledge of the chosen filmmakers' theories where relevant (e.g. Longinotto's invisibility, Moore's agit-prop) and of documentary's relationship to the real.<br><br><strong>AO2 - application and evaluation (10):</strong> the debate applied to the film — access (lightweight digital enabling For Sama's siege footage), archive (Amy's home-video composition), manipulation (Stories We Tell's fabricated home movies), distribution (streaming/online reach); film language analysed to support claims (observational camera, available light, absence of score); a reasoned judgement weighing both sides of the debate rather than asserting one — e.g. digital transformed documentary's access and archive decisively while narrative film changed least, so the documentary is where the digital's impact is most visible.<br><br><strong>Top-band shape:</strong> precise film language detail, both sides of the debate engaged, the filmmakers' theory used as an interpretive lens, and a verdict that answers the evaluate command word.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Documentary film can never show us the truth. With reference to your documentary film, discuss. [20]
Answer: AO1: Grierson's 'creative treatment of actuality' — construction enters from the start; the film studied's mode(s) and methods. AO2: apply: Sisters in Law's observational grammar (available light, no zooms/pans, absent score) claims truth through non-intervention — yet selection, framing and Longinotto's identification with her subjects are choices, so the film constructs as it records; Stories We Tell makes this the subject itself, fabricating home movies so the spectator questions every image. Judgement: documentary shows truths selectively and constructedly — the truthful claim depends on how openly the construction is declared; the strongest answers weigh both sides and reach a reasoned verdict.
With reference to your documentary film, explore how film language constructs meaning and generates response. [20]
Answer: AO1: accurate conventions (voiceover, interviews, archive footage, observational camera, reconstruction) and the film's specifics. AO2: bind language to response: For Sama's handheld, available-light camera and letter-to-daughter voiceover construct intimacy and immediacy, positioning spectators as the addressee of a mother's testimony; Amy's home-video archive generates both intimacy and complicity — spectators consume the exploitation the film indicts; Sisters in Law's invisible camera aligns spectators with the judges' gaze, so response is judgement learned by watching. Judgement: documentary's film language engineers identification, judgement or complicity — name which, and how.
Compare the approaches of two of the filmmakers' theories you have studied, with reference to your documentary film. [20]
Answer: AO1: accurate account of both theories — Longinotto (invisible, observational, no voiceover/captions/incidental music, films that raise questions) and Moore (visible, participatory, performative, agit-prop, committed polemic using humour); the film studied's relationship to each. AO2: apply: Sisters in Law embodies Longinotto's theory (immersive observation, judgement-free, identification with subjects), while Exit Through the Gift Shop challenges both models — a film whose authorship is itself the question. Evaluate: Longinotto's approach claims more direct access to reality but hides its selections; Moore's declares its selections but risks polemic. Judgement: neither approach is more truthful — they trade visibility of construction against intimacy of access, a reasoned verdict.
How far does your documentary film problematise the divide between fact and fiction? [20]
Answer: AO1: the spec's framing — contemporary documentary as a freer form using cinematic techniques creatively while problematising the fact/fiction divide; the film's mode(s). AO2: apply: Stories We Tell problematises it by design — reconstructions, contradictory interviews and fabricated home movies make the film's construction its subject; Amy problematises it through archive — selection and sequencing construct a portrait whose intimacy is real but whose narrative is authored; Exit Through the Gift Shop stages the question as a possible hoax. Judgement: the divide is problematised most where the film draws attention to its own construction — the strongest answers judge how far each film's form, not its subject, performs the problematising.
With reference to your documentary film, evaluate the usefulness of an observational approach to documentary filmmaking. [20]
Answer: AO1: the observational mode (fly-on-the-wall capture, minimal intervention, no voiceover or captions) and Longinotto's theory as its spec exemplar; accurate film detail. AO2: apply: Sisters in Law's observational grammar generates authenticity and judgement-free access to its subjects' lives — its power is what it withholds (no captions, no score, no conclusions). Evaluate: observation hides its selections (framing, duration, sequencing are still choices), and its refusal of context can obscure power relations. Judgement: the observational approach is most useful where subjects can speak for themselves, least where context must be supplied — a reasoned verdict that answers the command word.
