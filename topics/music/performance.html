Performance and Repertoire

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level Edexcel, OCR, WJEC/Eduqas

A* revision of performance for Edexcel 9MU0 (with OCR H543 and Eduqas): the 8-minute recital, difficulty levels and repertoire choice, the three assessment grids plus difficulty marks, and how to programme and record for full marks.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • State the 9MU0/01 requirements: 30%, 60 marks, a recorded recital of at least eight minutes of music, solo, ensemble, improvisation or music-technology realisation (AO1).
  • Apply the difficulty levels booklet: level 7 is 'standard', 8+ 'more difficult', levels 1-6 'less difficult' and potentially self-penalising (AO1).
  • Explain the marking: three grids (technical control; accuracy and fluency; expressive control, style and context) out of 48 plus 12 marks for difficulty (AO1).
  • Plan a recital programme balancing difficulty, duration and contrast, and manage the recording rules (recorded after 1 March, submitted by 15 May, whole re-records only) (AO1).
  • Compare Edexcel's approach with OCR H543 and Eduqas, including their programme-note requirements (AO1).

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Recital: The 9MU0/01 assessment format: one or more pieces performed live and uninterrupted as a programme, totalling at least eight minutes of music (tuning and gaps excluded).
Difficulty level: Edexcel rates performed pieces 1-8+ in its Difficulty Levels Booklet; level 7 is 'standard' for A level, 8+ 'more difficult', 1-6 'less difficult' - the raw mark maps through the difficulty column to the recital total.
Technical control (technique): Assessment grid 1 (AO1): command of the instrument or voice - sound production, security, handling of challenges.
Accuracy and fluency: Assessment grid 2 (AO1): pitch/rhythm accuracy and continuity - errors in notes, timing and recovery are penalised here.
Expressive control, style and context: Assessment grid 3 (AO1): interpretation - phrasing, dynamics, articulation and awareness of the music's style and context.
Ensemble performance: Performing with others; assessed on your live part (even while not playing), with difficulty decided by comparing your part with solo examples.
Music-technology realisation: A permitted performance option: realising music using music technology (e.g. sequencing/electric instruments), assessed on the final live performance.
Programme note: A written note identifying pieces (title, composer, duration) accompanying a recital - required by OCR and Eduqas; Edexcel 9MU0/01 requires no programme note, only the recording and administration.

Recital requirements

Edexcel 9MU0/01 (Performing) is 30% of the qualification, 60 marks, non-examined assessment externally assessed by recording. The requirements:

  • A public performance of one or more pieces, performed as a recital - live, unedited, uninterrupted.
  • Options: solo, ensemble, improvisation, realising music using music technology, or any combination.
  • Total performance time across all pieces: minimum eight minutes of music - tuning and gaps between pieces do not count.
  • Recorded after 1 March in the year of certification; all materials to the examiner by 15 May.
  • Re-records: the whole performance must be re-recorded, not part; a teacher must be present.

What is assessed (AO1): interpreting musical ideas with technical and expressive control and an understanding of style and context; performing with control and continuity (appropriate tempi); performing fluently with critical understanding of the overall shape, direction and style. The specification asks you to consider the effect of purpose, intention, audience, time and place - a recital staged as an event, with pauses, ordering and presentation managed, reads as a stronger interpretation than a string of unlinked pieces.

Practical planning: programme to comfortably exceed eight minutes (9-10 minutes of music covers nervous gaps and slower live tempi without penalty); order pieces for contrast (fast/slow, styles, keys); rehearse transitions so gaps stay short; record full runs, never stitched takes (editing is prohibited). If improvising, the assessment criteria still apply - control, continuity, fluency and style awareness are judged identically across all performance options.

Assessment nuance: the criteria grids contain additional guidance for ensemble and improvised performances - ensemble players are marked on their role in the whole performance, and improvisers on control of material and stylistic idiom rather than fidelity to a score. Backing tracks may be self-recorded, but only the live performance is assessed, so a sequenced accompaniment must be rehearsed with, not relied on to cover, weak spots. Any instrument is permitted, including technology-based instruments (electric guitar, sequenced realisations) - choose the medium that plays to your strongest technical control, since grid 1 measures command of the chosen instrument or voice, not its prestige. Treat the recital as one programme: manage stage order, pauses and audience address as part of 'purpose, intention, audience, time and place'.

Repertoire choice and difficulty

Repertoire choice is worth real marks: of the 60 available, 48 come from the three assessment grids and 12 from difficulty. Edexcel's Difficulty Levels Booklet grades pieces (and instruments without graded traditions) on a scale where, for A level:

  • Level 7 = 'standard' - the benchmark expectation by the end of the course.
  • Level 8 and above = 'more difficult' - access to the highest difficulty marks.
  • Levels 1-6 = 'less difficult' - flagged as potentially self-penalising: they may restrict access to the higher bands of the assessment grids.

Mechanics: the raw mark from grids 1-3 (out of 48) is applied to the corresponding difficulty level column to give the recital total out of 60 - the same technical quality earns more at higher difficulty. In practice: a secure Grade 7-8 standard programme out-scores a flawless Grade 6 programme.

Edge cases the specification defines: instruments without graded traditions are assessed at comparable demand - beatboxing reaches 'standard' only with fast, highly syncopated material, consistent effects (echo/reverb) and imaginative, mimicking sounds; rapping needs consistent fast pace, controlled articulation, syncopation and a sung hook. Ensemble performances: difficulty is decided by comparing your part with the solo examples, and you are assessed on your live role throughout - including passages where you are tacet.

Programme strategy: pick the hardest music you can play securely; one over-ambitious piece risks grid 2 (accuracy and fluency) more than it gains in difficulty. Balance: two or three contrasting pieces usually programme better than one long work, but a single substantial piece (a sonata movement plus) is acceptable if it fills eight minutes.

Mark penalty (under-length): eight minutes is a floor, not a target - submissions under it are marked but a published mark penalty table applies, and lost minutes cannot be recovered by brilliance elsewhere. Programme for 9-10 minutes of music. Difficulty booklet scope: the booklet covers GCSE, AS and A level repertoire, so many 'Grade 6-8' standbys are already rated - check the level before committing, and note that unfamiliar instruments are rated by comparable demand rather than refused. Standard by the end: level 7 is the A-level benchmark - plan repertoire in Year 12 so your final programme sits at or above it by spring of Year 13; pieces you outgrow (or never reach) both cost difficulty marks. Contrasting pieces from different traditions (a Classical movement, a Romantic character piece, a jazz standard or own-choice popular arrangement) show 'style and context' breadth in grid 3 that a single-style programme cannot.

Marking and programme notes

Marks: 60 total. Three grids, each out of 16, totalling 48 - plus 12 for difficulty:

GridFocus (AO1)What it rewards
1: Technical control (technique)Command of instrument/voiceAssured, secure sound production; challenges handled
2: Accuracy and fluencyPitch/rhythm accuracy, continuityFew minor errors; recovery from slips; steady tempi
3: Expressive control, style and contextInterpretationExciting, communicative performance; style-aware phrasing, dynamics, articulation

Assessed as a whole regardless of how many pieces make up the recital; ensemble performers are judged on their role in the whole performance. Top-band language: grid 1 'assured technical' control; grid 3 an 'exciting and communicative interpretation'.

Recording rules recap: live, unedited, uninterrupted; recorded after 1 March, submitted by 15 May; whole re-records only with a teacher present; any instruments/technology permitted; you may record your own backing track but only the live performance is assessed.

Board contrast: Edexcel requires no programme note - the recording and administration carry the assessment. OCR H543 and Eduqas require written programme notes with the recital (identifying pieces and giving brief context) and weight performance more heavily (OCR at 35%); their recitals are longer (typically 10-12 minutes at A level), so check the current specifications for exact durations and mark allocations. The performance skills themselves - technical control, accuracy, interpretative style - are the common currency of all three boards.

Revision regime: monthly full-run recordings from spring of Year 12; listen back against the three grids (accuracy first); programme notes drafted early where boards require them; performance day rehearsal of pauses, bowing/bowing-free stagecraft and audience engagement - grid 3 rewards communication, and communication is rehearsed, not improvised.

Top-band behaviour: grid 1 Level 5 rewards 'assured technical' control - security in sound production and command of the instrument's challenges; grid 2 rewards performances with only 'a few minor errors in pitch' and rhythm, with recovery from slips; grid 3 rewards an 'exciting and communicative interpretation' achieved through understanding of style. Examiners mark what they hear on one unedited recording - so nail the first take: warm up before recording, record at your best time of day, and treat every full run as the take. Order the programme so your most secure piece sits early (confidence) and your showpiece late (impact); end cleanly, since a rushed last page reads as poor control of the overall shape.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: 'Playing easier music perfectly gains more marks than playing harder music with minor errors.' Evaluate this claim with reference to the Edexcel performance assessment model. (10 marks)
Mark-scheme logic: Mark-scheme logic (AO1 awareness applied analytically): (1) The model: 60 marks = three grids out of 48 + 12 for difficulty; the raw grid mark maps through the difficulty level column, so identical technical quality earns more at higher difficulty. (2) Difficulty levels: level 7 is 'standard'; 8+ 'more difficult'; levels 1-6 'less difficult' and flagged as potentially self-penalising - they may restrict access to higher grid bands. (3) However, accuracy and fluency (grid 2) penalises errors: harder music invites more slips, and lost grid marks can outweigh difficulty gains - 'more than one or two' errors drops a band. (4) Judgement: the claim is false as stated - optimum is the hardest music playable securely; the specification's own warning is that easier pieces self-penalise, but so does insecure over-reaching. A balanced, secure Grade 7-8 programme is the mark-maximising choice.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

State the full requirements of the Edexcel 9MU0/01 performing assessment.
Answer: 30% of the qualification, 60 marks, non-examined assessment externally assessed by recording. A public recital of one or more pieces, performed live and uninterrupted, totalling at least eight minutes of music (tuning and gaps excluded). Options: solo, ensemble, improvisation, realising music using technology, or a combination. Recorded after 1 March in the year of certification; all materials to the examiner by 15 May. Re-records must cover the whole performance with a teacher present; recordings may not be edited afterwards.
Explain how the 60 marks are allocated and how difficulty affects the total.
Answer: Three assessment grids give a raw mark out of 48: grid 1 technical control (technique), grid 2 accuracy and fluency, grid 3 expressive control, style and context - each out of 16. A further 12 marks come from the difficulty of the pieces: the raw mark is applied to the corresponding difficulty level column in the Difficulty Levels Booklet to give the recital total out of 60. Level 7 is 'standard' for A level, 8+ 'more difficult', levels 1-6 'less difficult' and potentially self-penalising. The recital is assessed as a whole, regardless of the number of pieces.
How does Edexcel assess instruments with no graded exam tradition?
Answer: At a comparable level of demand using the Difficulty Levels Booklet guidance: e.g. beatboxing reaches 'standard' only with fast, much syncopated material, consistent effects (echo/reverb) and a wide range of imaginative, mimicking sounds; rapping needs consistently fast pace, controlled articulation, much syncopation and a sung hook. Submissions not matching the descriptions are assessed as 'less difficult'; 'more difficult' adds contrapuntal/melody-and-accompaniment effects, variety of styles and textures, or vocal-percussion demands. Ensemble difficulty is decided by comparing the student's part with solo examples.
Compare the Edexcel and OCR/Eduqas performance assessments.
Answer: Edexcel 9MU0/01: 30%, 60 marks, 8+ minute recital, no programme note, three grids (48) + 12 difficulty marks. OCR H543 and Eduqas weight performance more heavily (OCR at 35%), require written programme notes identifying pieces with brief context, and set longer recitals (typically 10-12 minutes at A level) - check current specifications for exact durations and mark allocations. Common ground: all three assess technical control, accuracy/fluency and expressive, style-aware interpretation (AO1), recorded as an unedited recital. Judgement: Edexcel's shorter recital and difficulty weighting reward strategic programme choice; OCR/Eduqas reward sustained stamina plus written communication skills.
How would you plan a recital programme to maximise marks?
Answer: 1) Choose the hardest music you can play securely - level 7 'standard' minimum; levels 1-6 are self-penalising. 2) Programme 9-10 minutes of music to exceed the eight-minute minimum, covering live gaps and slower tempi. 3) Order for contrast (fast/slow, style, key) with short rehearsed transitions - purpose, audience, time and place are assessed. 4) Balance risk: one over-ambitious piece costs grid 2 (accuracy and fluency) more than it gains in difficulty. 5) Rehearse full-run, unedited recordings from spring; listen back against the three grids, fixing accuracy first. Mark-scheme logic: grids reward assured technique, few minor errors, and an 'exciting and communicative' style-aware interpretation.
