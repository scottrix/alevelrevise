Aural and Dictation

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level Edexcel, OCR, WJEC/Eduqas

A* revision of aural skills for Edexcel 9MU0 (with OCR H543 and Eduqas): melodic and rhythmic dictation method, chord and cadence identification, skeleton-score listening questions and exam technique for the Appraising paper.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Complete short melodic dictation exercises (pitch and rhythm) on staff notation from unfamiliar music related to the set works (Edexcel 9MU0/03 Section A) (AO4).
  • Identify aurally chords, keys, cadences and modulations, including inversions and dominant 7ths (AO3/AO4).
  • Answer skeleton-score listening questions on set works: locate devices, name keys and cadences, justify opinions (AO4).
  • Manage the individual audio player and extract timings to maximise marks per listen (AO4).
  • Apply set-work knowledge to unfamiliar extracts in the Section B essay one (20 marks) (AO3/AO4).

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Melodic dictation: Notating a heard melody - in 9MU0/03 a short melody/rhythm completion exercise, filling in notes on staff notation (or guitar tablature in some questions) from unfamiliar music related to the set works.
Rhythmic dictation: Notating heard rhythms: metre, note values, rests and syncopation, in simple or compound time.
Skeleton score: A reduced or incomplete printed score provided with audio in Section A set-work questions; you locate features, fill gaps and annotate against what you hear.
Compound time: Metres dividing beats into threes (6/8, 9/8, 12/8); dictation questions expect fluent reading of compound-time rhythmic notation.
Simple time: Metres dividing beats into twos (2/4, 3/4, 4/4); identify first from the pulse before notating any rhythm.
Interval: The distance between two pitches; secure interval recognition (3rds, 4ths, 5ths, 6ths, octaves, semitone steps) underpins melodic dictation accuracy.
Cadence: The chordal close of a phrase: perfect (V-I), imperfect (?-V), plagal (IV-I), interrupted (V-vi); aural identification is a recurring Appraising skill.
Dominant 7th: The V chord with an added 7th (G-B-D-F in C); its aural pull (7th falls, leading note rises) makes it the most examined chord in dictation and analysis questions.

Melodic dictation method

In Edexcel 9MU0/03 Section A, one question is a short melody/rhythm completion exercise: pitch and rhythm dictation from an unfamiliar work whose style, language and context relate to the set works. You write onto staff notation (or guitar tablature in some questions) - blanks, not the whole line. Marks come from method, not genius:

  1. First listening: identify key (tonic from the final chord or opening harmony) and metre (simple or compound). Write the key signature and time signature immediately.
  2. Mark the frame: note the first and last pitches, and where the phrase ends (cadence). This anchors everything between.
  3. Second listening: track the contour - up, down, repeated - and mark scale degrees (do-re-mi logic) rather than absolute pitch.
  4. Third listening: fix the rhythm: bar lines, strong beats, rests, syncopation. Rhythm first where marks are cheapest.
  5. Final listening: check intervals by singing back; correct the gap between scale degrees and staff positions.

Training drills that convert directly into marks: sing scale degrees over a drone; transcribe two-bar melodies from the suggested wider listening daily; drill intervals (major/minor 3rds, 4ths, 5ths, 6ths, octave leaps) by associating each with a set-work or popular tune opening; practise compound-time reading (6/8, 12/8 - dotted rhythms, hemiolas) because dictation questions assume compound-time notation fluency (a stated requirement of 9MU0's musical language). If tablature is offered and you are a guitarist, use it - the marks are identical.

Marks discipline: never leave blanks - a plausible scale-degree guess scores; silence does not. Check the number of blanks before each play: target the longest gap first, since its notes constrain the shorter ones.

Common errors that cost cheap marks: missing accidentals (check the key signature and any raised leading notes before writing pitch); octave slips (sing back the line to check register); rhythm written to fit rather than heard (count the beats per bar first). Set-work anchors for intervals: associate each interval with a tune you know from the anthology - a rising 4th with a fanfare opening, a falling semitone with Herrmann's Psycho dissonances, a soaring leap with a Bush chorus; anchors make interval recognition instant under pressure. Compound-time drill: notate two-bar 6/8 and 12/8 rhythms daily from the suggested wider listening - dotted rhythms, hemiolas and off-beat entries are the exam's standard compound-time traps, and 9MU0's musical language explicitly requires fluent compound-time notation.

Rhythmic dictation and chord identification

Rhythmic dictation rewards a strict sequence: establish metre; tap the pulse while marking beats; write bar lines on the second listen; fill note values (minims to semiquavers, dotted values, rests) on the third. Watch the traps: syncopation across bar lines, dotted quaver-semiquaver pairs (habanera in La soirée dans Grenade), hemiolas in 6/8, rests on strong beats. If the extract is from a related popular or jazz style, expect off-beat accents and swung semiquavers - notate the accents before the subdivisions.

Chord and cadence identification is tested throughout Section A on skeleton scores and aurally. The diagnostic questions, in order:

  • How many distinct chords in the bar? (One = static/pedal harmony, common in Stravinsky's Augurs of Spring and Herrmann's ostinati.)
  • Is the bass note the root, 3rd or 5th? (Root = 5/3; 3rd = first inversion (6); 5th = second inversion (6/4).)
  • Is there a dissonant 4th or 7th above the bass? (Suspension or 7th chord - name which resolves where.)
  • Where does the phrase close? (V-I perfect; ?-V imperfect; IV-I plagal; V-vi interrupted.)

Modulation spotting: identify the opening key, then listen for an accidental that changes the pull (F# appearing in C major signals the dominant) and a cadence confirming the new key. On skeleton scores, scan the accidentals first - they map the modulation. Keys and scales: be ready to name major, minor (harmonic), pentatonic (Pagodes) and modal inflections (Dorian in Eleanor Rigby) from listening alone.

Mark-scheme logic: chord questions award partial credit - naming the chord function (e.g. 'a dominant 7th of G') without the inversion still scores; cadence questions require progression and type. Always answer with both the label and the evidence you heard.

Skeleton-score chord method: read the bass first (its note and any figures), scan upper parts for the 4ths and 7ths that mark suspensions, then check accidentals - a sharpened leading note signals a dominant or a modulation. Combine the aural and the visual: what you hear confirms what the score implies, and discrepancies are usually suspensions or passing notes rather than misprints. Chord vocabulary to drill: triads and inversions, dominant 7ths with their three inversions, secondary dominants (V/V, V/vi) and diminished 7ths; name each with its function ('the dominant 7th of G', 'a first-inversion tonic') because functional naming earns partial credit where bare labels do not. Keys and scales: major, harmonic minor, pentatonic (Pagodes) and Dorian inflections (Eleanor Rigby) - be ready to name the scale and cite the note that proves it.

Set-work listening questions and exam technique

Section A (50 marks) contains three set-work questions, each with audio and a skeleton score, mixing short open responses and multiple choice. Each student controls an individual audio player - use it: allot your plays, listen to the whole extract first, then interrogate specific bars. The examinable skills:

  • Identify musical features aurally and locate them on the skeleton score (entries, cadences, modulations, texture changes).
  • Name keys, chords and intervals in context.
  • Complete gaps: missing notes, rhythms or performance directions (dynamics, articulation, tempo terms).
  • Express and justify opinions - 'because' sentences earn marks, adjectives alone do not.

Board-specific tips: Edexcel 9MU0/03 is 2 hours 10 minutes, 100 marks (Section A 50, Section B 50) - budget roughly 30-35 minutes for Section A's four questions and bank the rest for essays. OCR H543 and Eduqas test the same aural skills in their own listening papers; the drill method (key, metre, cadence, devices) transfers directly.

Essay one (Q5, 20 marks) plays an unfamiliar work related to a set work - not from the suggested wider listening list - and asks you to draw links from your set-work study to what you hear. Method: identify the closest set work (by instrumentation, idiom, devices), name two or three shared features precisely (e.g. ostinato-driven texture like Psycho's Prelude; string-plus-voice scoring like Eleanor Rigby; pentatonic layering like Pagodes), then compare treatment and context. No skeleton score is given, so anchor points to moments in time ('at the opening', 'when the voice enters') rather than bar numbers.

Revision regime: transcribe daily (10 minutes); drill cadences and inversions against the set works weekly; replay each set work following its skeleton score until every device is audible at first hearing - Section A rewards ears trained on exactly these pieces.

Command words: 'name' and 'identify' need one-word precision; 'explain' and 'describe how' need mechanism plus effect; 'justify' needs evidence from the audio ('because the strings enter with...'). Match answer length to command word - a paragraph for a 'name' question wastes minutes and gains nothing. Multiple-choice traps: Edexcel options usually include one near-miss (a V7 labelled V, a first inversion labelled root) - eliminate by bass note first, then by the dissonance you hear. Short-answer precision: give device + moment + effect in one sentence each; leave three-part analysis for Section B where the marks are. Final week regime: replay every set work with its skeleton score daily; do one full timed Section A per past paper; re-drill the dictation method play-by-play until it is automatic.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: You hear an unfamiliar extract in a popular style. Complete the melody on the staff provided, then identify the key and the cadence at the end of the phrase. (6 marks)
Mark-scheme logic: Mark-scheme logic (AO4): (1) Correct key signature and time signature written immediately (1 mark); (2) contour and phrase-ending pitch marked after first listen (1); (3) rhythm accurate - metre, bar lines, rests, syncopation (2); (4) pitches accurate - scale degrees onto the staff, intervals correct (1); (5) key named with evidence (tonic from final chord/opening harmony) and cadence identified by progression and type (e.g. imperfect, ?-V) (1). Partial credit applies: rhythm or pitch alone scores; a named key without evidence does not. Never leave blanks - plausible scale-degree guesses earn marks, silence does not.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Describe your complete method for a melody/rhythm dictation exercise with three plays.
Answer: Play 1: identify key (tonic from final chord) and metre; write key signature and time signature; mark first and last pitches and the cadence point. Play 2: mark contour (up/down/repeated) and scale degrees; establish bar lines and strong beats. Play 3: fix remaining pitches and rhythms - longest gap first - then sing back to check intervals. Discipline: never leave blanks; plausible scale-degree guesses score, silence does not; check the number of blanks before each play.
How would you identify aurally whether a chord is in root position, first or second inversion?
Answer: Ask what note the bass sounds relative to the chord: root = root position (5/3); the 3rd above the chord's root = first inversion (6); the 5th = second inversion (6/4). Aural cues: first inversions feel less settled than root positions; second inversions (especially on the tonic) sound unstable and expect resolution - listen for the upper parts resolving 6-5 and 4-3 over a held bass in a cadential 6/4. On a skeleton score, cross-check with the printed bass note and figures.
Name the four cadences and how each sounds; how are they tested in 9MU0/03?
Answer: Perfect V-I: decisive, both root position strongest - 'full stop'. Imperfect ?-V: unfinished, usually from I, ii, IV or vi - 'comma'. Plagal IV-I: softer, 'Amen' colour. Interrupted V-vi: the expected tonic is replaced by the submediant - a surprise that extends the phrase. In Section A they are tested aurally and on skeleton scores: identify progression and type (partial credit for function alone), and in modulation questions the cadence confirms the new key - a V-I in G after C major means the modulation is complete.
How do you spot a modulation in a listening question?
Answer: Identify the opening key first (tonic from the opening chord or final cadence). Listen for an accidental that changes the harmonic pull - F# appearing in C major signals the dominant (G); the raising of the leading note is the mechanism. On a skeleton score, scan accidentals first - they map the modulation. Confirm with a cadence in the new key. Common targets: dominant, subdominant, relative major/minor. In set works: Bach modulates to closely related keys; Wieck-Schumann uses chromatic third relations; Berlioz third-related keys for programme effect.
What does essay one (Q5, 20 marks) in Section B require, and what is the best method?
Answer: It plays an unfamiliar work related to one of the set works (not from the suggested wider listening) and asks you to draw links from your set-work study to the extract. Audio is provided; no skeleton score. Method: 1) identify the closest set work by instrumentation, idiom and devices; 2) name two or three shared features precisely (e.g. ostinato-driven tension like Psycho's Prelude, voice-plus-strings like Eleanor Rigby, pentatonic layering like Pagodes); 3) compare treatment and context, anchoring points to moments in time ('at the opening', 'when the drums enter') since bar numbers are unavailable; 4) justify every point - 'because' sentences earn marks.
