Composition Briefs

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level Edexcel, OCR, WJEC/Eduqas

A* revision of composition for Edexcel 9MU0 (with OCR H543 and Eduqas): free composition vs Pearson-set briefs, the 40/20 mark split, development, texture and harmony strategies, and how the mark grids reward coherence and control.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • State the 9MU0/02 portfolio requirements: two compositions, 60 marks, 40-mark free choice/AoS brief (min 4 minutes) plus 20-mark technique brief (min 1 minute), combined minimum 6 minutes (AO1).
  • Distinguish the six briefs relating to areas of study (released 1 September) from the four technique briefs - Bach chorale, two-part counterpoint, arrangement, remix (released 1 April) (AO1).
  • Apply the assessment criteria: coherence (unity and variety), expressive control (response to brief, audience and occasion) and technical control (idiomatic writing, devices and conventions) (AO2).
  • Use development devices - repetition, sequence, fragmentation, variation - and manage texture and harmonic vocabulary to reach Level 5 bands (AO2).
  • Avoid self-penalising choices: excess length, unidiomatic writing, ignoring the brief's stated audience and occasion (AO2).

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Free choice composition: The 40-mark submission (9MU0/02): either a free composition of your own design or one of six Pearson briefs relating to the areas of study; minimum 4 minutes; briefs released 1 September in the year of certification.
Brief assessing technique: The 20-mark submission: one of four Pearson briefs - Bach chorale, two-part counterpoint, arrangement, remix; minimum 1 minute; released 1 April in the year of certification.
Bach chorale: Technique brief 1: add alto, tenor and bass to a given soprano of 12-18 bars in the manner of J.S. Bach; no modal chorales, no 3/4 time.
Two-part counterpoint: Technique brief 2: complete a baroque texture for bass and violin or flute from a 24-42 bar stimulus, in both treble and bass; no figured bass required.
Remix: Technique brief 4: develop a given .wav sample into a remix for a nightclub setting; assessed on clarity, mixing and stereo imaging; submitted as 44.1kHz, 16-bit wav.
Audience and occasion: The purpose and setting stated in every Pearson brief; marks are awarded for the effectiveness with which the composition meets them.
Stereo imaging: The placement of sounds across the stereo field; part of the remix brief's assessment alongside mixing clarity.
Idiomatic writing: Music that lies comfortably under the chosen instrument(s)/voice(s) - idioms, ranges and techniques the player would naturally use; unplayable or unsingable passages cap the technical-control grid.

Portfolio requirements

Edexcel 9MU0/02 (Composing) is 30% of the qualification, 60 marks, externally assessed, submitted at the end of the course. The portfolio is two compositions:

SubmissionMarksMinimum lengthOptions
Free choice composition404 minutesFree composition OR one of six briefs relating to the areas of study (Vocal, Instrumental, Music for Film, Popular Music and Jazz, Fusion, New Directions)
Brief assessing technique201 minuteOne of four: Bach chorale, two-part counterpoint, arrangement, remix

Combined minimum is six minutes (gaps and tuning do not count); the guided maximum is eight - longer submissions are still marked but may be self-penalising. Timing of releases: free choice briefs on 1 September at the beginning of the year of certification; technique briefs on 1 April; both via the Pearson website, each stating an audience and/or occasion. Group compositions are not allowed.

Technique brief parameters (from the specification): the chorale gives a soprano of 12-18 bars (no modal chorales, no 3/4) and demands Bach's harmonic vocabulary - root position and first inversion triads, sanctioned tonic 6/4s, typical 7th chords, modulations to closely related keys, passing notes and suspensions. The two-part counterpoint stimulus is 24-42 bars, completed in both treble and bass, following 18th-century conventions of texture, voice leading and structure - and explicitly no figured bass. The arrangement brief gives a 12-24 bar melody for up to four specified parts, to harmonise and extend. The remix gives a .wav sample to develop into a nightclub-ready track; the final recording (44.1kHz, 16-bit wav) is assessed for clarity, mixing and stereo imaging.

Choosing: the free composition must complement your technique brief and secure the six-minute total. Play to strengths: a guitarist who writes pop should avoid the chorale; a fluent Bach stylist banks the 20 marks cheaply and spends the time on the 40-mark piece.

Submission format: both compositions are submitted at the end of the course as score plus audio recording (the remix as 44.1kHz, 16-bit wav); free compositions should be worked on and recorded in the intended year of certification, and centres must ensure submissions are valid for the series entered. Notation quality is part of technical control: staff notation with correct key signatures and compound-time rhythmic notation is named in the 9MU0 musical language - an unplayable reading or missing clef costs marks before a note sounds. Produce clean, playable parts with dynamics, articulation and tempo markings, since grid 3 penalises mishandled textures and unidiomatic writing that often trace back to sloppy engraving.

Marking criteria

The 40-mark composition is assessed against three 'Creating and Developing Musical Ideas' grids (AO2); the 20-mark technique brief against a single 'Response to the brief' grid:

  • Grid 1 - Coherence (out of 13): design and management of structures, ideas and formal processes; the balance between unity and variety. Level 5 wording: ideas 'created, developed and extended with assurance and imagination throughout', a 'mature balance of unity and variety'.
  • Grid 2 - Expressive control (out of 13): pacing and management of moods, atmosphere, effects and dramatic flow appropriate to the chosen style/genre; plus response to the brief - or, in a free composition, the realisation of intentions - and effectiveness in meeting the intended purpose and/or audience.
  • Grid 3 - Technical control (out of 14): control of musical elements and of devices and conventions appropriate to the style; unplayable/unsingable or unidiomatic passages, mishandled textures cap this grid.
  • Technique grid - Response to the brief (out of 20): marks for the overall response in terms of creative response and development of the given stimulus. Level 5 chorale wording: 'the stimulus is harmonised with fluency and creativity; lines are elegant and well shaped'.

What separates bands: Level 1-2 submissions are compromised by technical weaknesses - angular lines, dull unvaried rhythm, obtrusive misjudgements; Level 3 is formulaic or mechanical handling - the classic 'safe but dull' chorale or verse-chorus that never develops; Level 4-5 require development and fluency: ideas extended effectively for most of the piece, misjudgements not detracting from overall effect.

Mark-earning priorities in order of cheapness: (1) satisfy the brief's stated audience and occasion - free marks in Grid 2; (2) playable, idiomatic writing - protects Grid 3 entirely; (3) a balanced structure with real development of at least two ideas - the Coherence grid; (4) stylistic accuracy: conventions of the chosen genre (EDM structure for remix, 18th-century part writing for the technique studies, film-score leitmotif logic for a film brief).

Grid 2 nuance: expressive control doubles as the 'did you follow the brief?' grid - in a brief composition, marks reward the response to the brief; in a free composition, the realisation of the piece's intentions. State your intentions in the score's title and tempo markings, and make the audience/occasion audible (a nightclub remix wants build-drop-groove and clear mixing; a piece for a school assembly wants singable lines and manageable registers). Level 3 trap - 'safe but dull': a technically clean verse-chorus or chorale that never develops sits exactly at Level 3 ('formulaic or mechanical approach'); the escape route is development of ideas, not more polish.

Development, texture and harmony strategies

Development is the difference between Level 3 and Level 5. Stock devices, all quotable in your planning:

  • Repetition and return: restate ideas in new keys, registers or instruments - unity with novelty.
  • Sequence and fragmentation: extend a motive by stepwise repetition or break it into cells and recombine.
  • Variation: same idea, new guise - change accompaniment, metre, mode (major to minor) or texture.
  • Contrast: introduce a genuinely different second idea, then reconcile it - the sonata-form instinct.

Texture is the cheapest route to contrast: move between melody-and-accompaniment, counterpoint, homophony, unison/octave moments; layer and reduce dynamically. A film brief wants texture matched to picture and leitmotif transformation; a vocal brief wants text and voice leading; a pop/EDM brief wants build, drop and groove management with clear mixing.

Harmonic vocabulary (stated in the 9MU0 musical language): chord symbols and inversions, standard progressions, dominant 7ths and extended chords (secondary 7ths and 9ths), modulation to closely related keys, plus devices - suspensions, passing notes, pedals, sequences. Deploy a handful deliberately: a Ic-V-I close, one secondary dominant, a suspension at a cadence, a pedal before a return. For the chorale, harmony is the craft: root positions at cadences, stepwise bass, elegant inner parts, dissonances resolved. For two-part counterpoint, rhythmically satisfying, stylish melodic lines in both treble and bass, 18th-century texture and structure conventions.

Avoiding self-penalisation: submit within the guided 8 minutes; end cleanly (abrupt or absent codas read as misjudgement); re-check idiom for every instrument; follow the brief's audience/occasion wording in your structure and dynamics. Across OCR H543 and Eduqas the same skills are rewarded under their own criteria - secure development, idiom and stylistic response are the common currency of all three boards.

Structuring to the brief: match form to audience and occasion - a dance-inspired piece suits rondo or binary repetition; a film brief suits leitmotif transformation across contrasting textures; a vocal brief wants strophic or verse-chorus with text-aware melody. Harmony as pacing: save the secondary dominant and the 7th chords for structural moments (approach to a return, pre-cadence); a pedal before a reprise creates the same retransition pull as a sonata-form dominant pedal. Texture as contrast: alternate melody-and-accompaniment with contrapuntal and homophonic passages, and mark dynamic layering into the score - examiners read the texture plan before hearing it.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: 'To score well, a composition must be long and harmonically complex.' Evaluate this claim with reference to the Edexcel composing assessment criteria. (10 marks)
Mark-scheme logic: Mark-scheme logic (AO2 awareness applied analytically): (1) Length: minimum 4 minutes (free choice) + 1 minute (technique), combined 6 minutes; guided maximum 8 - submissions over the maximum are still marked but 'may be self-penalising', so length beyond the guide adds no reward. (2) Harmony: the musical language names inversions, dominant 7ths and extended chords, but the grids reward <em>control</em> and <em>coherence</em>, not complexity for its own sake - grid 3 caps unidiomatic or mishandled writing, and grid 1 rewards a 'mature balance of unity and variety'. (3) What actually earns Level 5: assured, imaginative development of ideas (grid 1), effective response to the brief's audience and occasion (grid 2), idiomatic command of style and convention (grid 3); the technique grid rewards 'fluency and creativity' with 'elegant, well shaped lines'. (4) Judgement: the claim is false - controlled development of modest material beats sprawling complexity; a fluent 6-minute portfolio with clear structure and idiomatic writing out-scores an overloaded 10-minute one.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

State the full requirements of the Edexcel 9MU0/02 composing portfolio.
Answer: Two compositions, 30% of the qualification, 60 marks, externally assessed. Submission 1: free choice composition - either a free composition or one of six briefs relating to the areas of study (Vocal, Instrumental, Music for Film, Popular Music and Jazz, Fusion, New Directions) - 40 marks, minimum 4 minutes, briefs released 1 September in the year of certification. Submission 2: one of four technique briefs (Bach chorale, two-part counterpoint, arrangement, remix) - 20 marks, minimum 1 minute (longer if the brief specifies), released 1 April. Combined minimum 6 minutes, guided maximum 8; every brief states an audience and/or occasion; group compositions not allowed; remix submitted as 44.1kHz, 16-bit wav.
Outline the parameters of the four technique briefs and what each assesses.
Answer: Bach chorale: add alto/tenor/bass to a given soprano of 12-18 bars (no modal chorales, no 3/4); Bach's vocabulary - root position and first inversion triads, sanctioned tonic 6/4s, typical 7th chords, modulation to closely related keys, passing notes, suspensions. Two-part counterpoint: complete a baroque texture for bass and violin or flute from a 24-42 bar stimulus, in both treble and bass; stylish, rhythmically satisfying lines; 18th-century conventions of texture, voice leading and structure; no figured bass required. Arrangement: harmonise a given 12-24 bar melody and extend it into a coherent piece for up to four specified parts, for a stated audience and occasion. Remix: develop a given .wav sample into a nightclub-ready remix; assessed on editing/manipulation of samples, timbres and textures plus clarity, mixing and stereo imaging of the final 44.1kHz 16-bit wav. All four are marked against a single 'Response to the brief' grid (out of 20) for creative response and development of the stimulus.
Explain what the three assessment grids reward and what caps each one.
Answer: Grid 1 Coherence (out of 13): design and management of structures, ideas and formal processes; balance of unity and variety - capped by repetitive, predictable structures or too great a diversity of ideas. Grid 2 Expressive control (out of 13): pacing of moods, atmosphere, effects and dramatic flow for the chosen style, plus response to the brief (or realisation of intentions in a free composition) and effectiveness for the intended audience - capped by ignoring the brief's audience/occasion. Grid 3 Technical control (out of 14): control of musical elements and of devices and conventions appropriate to the style - capped by unplayable/unsingable passages, unidiomatic handling and mishandled textures. Level 5 language: 'assurance and imagination throughout', 'mature balance of unity and variety'.
How would you develop an 8-bar idea into a Level 5 four-minute composition?
Answer: Restate it in new keys, registers and instruments (unity with novelty); extend it by sequence and fragmentation; vary the accompaniment - metre, mode (major to minor), texture - so the idea returns transformed; introduce one genuinely contrasting second idea and reconcile it (the sonata-form instinct); layer and reduce texture dynamically for pacing; close with a substantial return and a clean coda. Underneath: a deliberate harmonic plan (cadential Ic-V-I closes, one secondary dominant, suspensions, a pedal before a return). Mark-scheme logic: grid 1 wants 'created, developed and extended with assurance and imagination throughout' and a mature unity-variety balance - development, not addition, is what scores.
Why can a composition be 'self-penalising', and how do you avoid it?
Answer: Three ways: 1) Length - over the guided maximum of eight minutes, extra material adds no reward and may dilute quality or cause performance/production faults. 2) Idiom - unplayable, unsingable or unidiomatic passages cap the technical-control grid directly. 3) Ignoring the brief - failing the stated audience and occasion loses expressive-control marks that were otherwise free. Avoid by: submitting 6-8 minutes total, checking idiom instrument by instrument (or voice by voice), planning structure and dynamics explicitly around the brief's wording, and choosing a technique brief that complements your strengths (a fluent Bach stylist banks the 20 marks cheaply).
