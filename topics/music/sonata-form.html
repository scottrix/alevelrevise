Sonata Form and Instrumental Genres

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level Edexcel, OCR, WJEC/Eduqas

A* revision of sonata form for Edexcel 9MU0 (with OCR H543 and Eduqas): exposition, development and recapitulation, first/second subjects, transitions and codas, Classical genres and the set instrumental works of Berlioz and Clara Wieck-Schumann.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Describe the tonal plan of a sonata-form exposition in a major key: first subject in tonic, transition modulating, second subject in dominant, codetta (AO1/AO3).
  • Explain how the development manipulates expository material through fragmentation, sequence and modulation, and how the recapitulation differs from the exposition (AO3/AO4).
  • Analyse first and second subject contrast (melody, rhythm, texture, key) in the set works: Berlioz, Symphonie Fantastique movement I and Wieck-Schumann, Piano Trio Op. 17 movement 1 (AO4).
  • Compare sonata form with related Classical structures: ternary, rondo, sonata-rondo, concerto double exposition and the four-movement symphonic cycle (AO3).
  • Use structural terminology with precision in Edexcel Section B essays, linking musical elements, context and language (AO3/AO4).

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Sonata form: A tripartite structure - Exposition, Development, Recapitulation - governed by tonal conflict (tonic vs dominant) resolved when both subjects return in the tonic; the dominant structural principle of Classical instrumental music.
Exposition: First section: first subject in the tonic, a transition/bridge modulating to the dominant (or relative major in minor-key works), a contrasting second subject, and a codetta; usually repeated.
Development: Middle section that fragments, sequences and modulates expository themes through often remote keys, building harmonic tension before re-establishing the dominant as a retransition to the recapitulation.
Recapitulation: Final section restating first subject, transition (adjusted so it does not modulate) and second subject - now in the tonic - resolving the tonal conflict; often extended by a coda.
First subject: The opening theme group in the tonic; usually rhythmically assertive and structurally firm, supplying most development material.
Second subject: The contrasting theme group in the dominant (major-key works) or relative major (minor-key works); more lyrical, different texture and phrase structure.
Transition (bridge): The passage linking the subjects, shifting key from tonic to dominant and often dissolving texture; in the recapitulation it is rewritten to stay in the tonic.
Idée fixe: Berlioz's term for the recurring melody representing the beloved throughout Symphonie Fantastique; transforms in each movement - a programmatic device that bends sonata form in movement I.

Exposition, development, recapitulation

Sonata form is drama in key: two themes in conflict, tension in the middle, resolution at the end. In a major-key exposition the first subject states the tonic firmly (assertive rhythm, firm cadences), the transition dissolves the first subject's texture and modulates, the second subject answers in the dominant - more lyrical, different phrase structure - and a codetta hammers the dominant home before the repeat. In minor-key works the second subject moves to the relative major: in Clara Wieck-Schumann's Piano Trio in G minor Op. 17 movement 1, the agitated G minor first subject (a theme often compared to the chromatic descent of Bach's Musical Offering royal theme) yields to a more flowing second subject in B-flat major, the relative major.

The development is the engine of the form and the richest source of exam questions. Its stock devices:

  • Fragmentation: breaking themes into small motives and recombining them.
  • Sequence: repeating a motive at different pitches, usually around the circle of fifths.
  • Modulation: remote keys build tension; minor/major mode switches recolour material.
  • Combination: subjects heard against each other - contrapuntally or in new textures.
  • Retransition: a dominant pedal and dying dynamics signal the return; the moment examiners love to ask about.

The recapitulation restates the first subject in the tonic, then rewrites the transition so it no longer modulates, and brings the second subject into the tonic - conflict resolved. A coda may extend the close, often over a tonic or dominant pedal, with interrupted cadences (V-vi) delaying finality.

In Berlioz's Symphonie Fantastique movement I (Réveries - Passions), sonata form is bent, not abandoned: a long slow largo introduction precedes the allegro; the idée fixe enters late, as an extended lyrical melody, and its obsessive returns - including a climactic recall in the coda - replace the tidy balance of Haydn and Mozart with psychological narrative. Explaining how Berlioz adapts the form (proportions, key relationships, the idée fixe's role) is the A* skill in essay questions on this set work.

Origins and logic: sonata form grows from Baroque binary dance form (two repeated halves, tonal modulation in the first) expanded into ternary proportions by Haydn and Mozart - the development is what turns binary into sonata. This matters in essays: when asked 'why does the exposition repeat?', the answer is that the repeat secures both tonal poles before the development destabilises them. Variants to name: sonata form without development (slow movements go straight from exposition to recapitulation), monothematic sonatas (Haydn's second subjects are often first-subject material recast in the new key), and sonata-rondo (ABACABA with a development in place of the C episode). Naming the variant and its effect on the tonal plan earns AO3 knowledge marks.

First and second subjects, transitions and codas

Section A questions on skeleton scores and Section B essays both hinge on subject contrast. Compare systematically across the musical elements:

ElementFirst subjectSecond subject
KeyTonicDominant (major-key) / relative major (minor-key)
MelodyFragmentary, arpeggio- or motive-basedLyric, stepwise, singing quality
RhythmAssertive, repeated-note or drivingFlowing, longer note values
TextureOften unison/octave or bare accompanimentFuller harmony, dialogue between parts
CadencesFrequent, firm (tonic)Elided, deferred (dominant)

The transition earns marks for what it does: liquidates the first subject (often by repetition or dynamic drop), modulates via a pivot chord or the circle of fifths, and ends with a half-close on the dominant preparing the new key. Spot the devices - sequences, dominant pedals, texture changes - and name the moment the second subject actually arrives (its first complete statement over a new accompaniment).

Codas and codettas: a codetta is the short close of the exposition in the dominant (or relative major); a coda is a substantial post-recapitulation extension. Classical codas often restate codetta material, add a dominant pedal, and use interrupted cadences to postpone the end. Berlioz's movement I goes further: the idée fixe returns over a breathless accompaniment in the coda, and the movement ends with a slow, hymn-like confirmation of the tonic - programmatic weighting of a structural function.

Exam drill: when a skeleton score appears, first identify metre and key; second, mark cadences; third, label boundaries - first subject end, transition start, second subject arrival, codetta. In essays (Section B, Q6 - 30 marks), evaluate structure alongside melody, harmony, texture and context: e.g. how the idée fixe's pervasiveness reflects 1830s programme-music aesthetics and Berlioz's semi-autobiographical infatuation with Harriet Smithson, premiered 1830 in Paris.

Spotting the second subject: examiners ask where it begins - the answer is the first complete statement of new material over a new accompaniment in the new key, usually after a half-close on the dominant; everything before it is transition even if melodic. Watch for elision (the second subject begins over the cadence of the transition) and for monothematic writing where the 'new' subject is old material in a new key - describe the contrast that actually exists (key, accompaniment, register) rather than assuming melodic novelty. Transition mechanics to cite: liquidation (repeated fragments of the first subject over a dissolving texture), a pivot chord or circle-of-fifths sequence, and a half-close on the dominant; in the recapitulation, the same passage is rewritten to cadence in the tonic - the clearest single piece of evidence that the form is about tonal resolution.

Classical forms and set instrumental works

Sonata form sits inside larger genre structures you must recognise and compare:

  • Symphony: four movements - fast (sonata form), slow (ternary or sonata form), minuet/trio or scherzo, fast finale (rondo or sonata-rondo). Berlioz expands the cycle to five programmatic movements with a recurring idée fixe and a huge orchestra - a deliberate rupture with the Classical symphony.
  • Concerto: often a double exposition (orchestra states both subjects, soloist restates them) with a written-out cadenza near the end.
  • String quartet / sonata: the sonata cycle in chamber dress; dialogue between equal parts.
  • Rondo: ABACA (or ABACABA) with a refrain in the tonic; final movements favour its liveliness. Sonata-rondo blends sonata tonal plan with rondo recurrence.
  • Ternary (ABA): the slow-movement default; contrast by key and character, return often decorated.

Set-work anchors (Edexcel AoS 2, Instrumental Music): Wieck-Schumann Piano Trio Op. 17 movement 1 (1846) is a minority-key sonata form - G minor first subject, relative major second subject, development including fugal writing on the first subject - Classical architecture stretched by chromatic, third-related harmony; the context: a woman composer asserting herself in a male-dominated profession, writing after her marriage to Robert Schumann. Berlioz Symphonie Fantastique movement I (1830) is a programmatic sonata form: long introduction, late-entering idée fixe, extreme orchestration (huge strings, brass and added harps), vivid dynamic contrast - Romantic narrative grafted onto Classical bones.

Essay technique (9MU0/03 Section B): essay two (Q6, 30 marks) offers four options, each from a different area of study, asking you to evaluate musical elements, context and language of one set work - no audio, but a score excerpt provided, and you must use knowledge of the whole work. Structure answers by element (structure, melody, harmony, texture, context), anchor every point to a bar/moment, and finish each paragraph on effect. AO3 (10% of the qualification) rewards demonstrated knowledge; AO4 (30%) rewards evaluative, critical judgement - so every description needs a 'therefore'.

Distinguishing forms on a score: sonata form has a modulating exposition and a reworking middle; ternary has a contrasting middle section in a related key but no development of the A material; rondo returns a refrain between episodes and closes each episode back on the tonic. Check the returns: if the 'B' material comes back in the tonic inside the final section, you have sonata form or sonata-rondo, not ternary. Symphony movement roles: first movements carry the form's drama, slow movements the expressive depth (often ternary or sonata without development), minuets/scherzos the dance, finales the wit (rondo or sonata-rondo) - use these expectations to structure comparisons, then show how your set work confirms or breaks them (Berlioz breaks almost all of them: five movements, a recurring idée fixe, programme over genre).

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: Evaluate the musical elements, context and language of Movement I of Berlioz's Symphonie Fantastique, with particular reference to how Berlioz adapts Classical sonata form. (30 marks)
Mark-scheme logic: Mark-scheme logic (AO3 + AO4, Edexcel 9MU0/03 Section B essay two): (1) Knowledge - identify the form: long slow introduction (Réveries) then Allegro with first subject, transition, late lyrical second subject (idée fixe), development, climactic coda recall; key structure of C minor/major. (2) Analysis - how each element works: chromatic first subject, idée fixe's stepwise shape and orchestration, huge dynamic and register contrasts, expanded orchestra (four harps, added brass, divided strings). (3) Context and language - 1830 Paris, programme symphony, semi-autobiographical (Harriet Smithson), Romantic aesthetics vs the Classical symphonic model Berlioz knew from Beethoven. (4) Evaluation - judgement throughout: e.g. the idée fixe's late entry and coda return subvert Classical proportions to serve the programme; top answers argue a case rather than list features, using precise terminology (idée fixe, transition, coda, programmatic) anchored to the score.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Explain the tonal structure of the exposition in a Classical sonata form in a major key.
Answer: First subject opens firmly in the tonic, cadencing in that key. The transition (bridge) liquidates the first subject and modulates - by pivot chord, circle of fifths or dominant pedal - to the dominant, closing with a half-close on the new dominant. The second subject is stated in the dominant, contrasting in melody (lyrical vs fragmentary), rhythm, texture and phrase structure. A codetta closes the exposition in the dominant, often with repeated cadences, before the exposition repeat. The tonic-dominant opposition is the form's structural tension, resolved only in the recapitulation.
Describe four devices a composer uses in a sonata-form development and their effects.
Answer: Fragmentation: themes broken into motives, recombined - intensifies and compresses. Sequence: motives repeated at new pitches, usually on the circle of fifths - drives harmonic motion. Modulation: remote keys and mode switches (major to minor) build tension away from the tonic. Contrapuntal combination: subjects or motives layered against each other - new meaning from old material. Most developments end with a retransition: dominant pedal, dropping dynamics, slowing harmonic rhythm - the 'stall' before the recapitulation's return in the tonic.
How does the recapitulation differ from the exposition, and why?
Answer: Same order of events, different key plan: the transition is rewritten so it no longer modulates (it stays in the tonic or touches the dominant without cadencing there), and the second subject is restated in the tonic instead of the dominant (or relative major in minor-key works). The reason is structural: the exposition creates tonal conflict (tonic vs dominant); the recapitulation resolves it by giving both subjects the tonic, so the movement can close conclusively. Codas often extend the resolution with pedal points and interrupted cadences.
Compare the treatment of sonata form in Wieck-Schumann's Piano Trio Op. 17 movement 1 with the Classical model.
Answer: Similarities: the Classical architecture remains - exposition with contrasting subjects, development, recapitulation, coda; first subject in the tonic (G minor), second subject in the relative major (B-flat) as standard minor-key practice. Differences: chromatic, third-related harmony stretches the tonal plan beyond diatonic expectations; fugal textures in the development point to Baroque revival interests; the lyrical breadth and piano-trio textures (piano with violin and cello in dialogue) reflect Romantic expression. Context: 1846, Clara's only piano trio, written as a woman composer staking a claim in a male-dominated profession. Judgement: form is the vehicle; harmonic language and texture carry the Romantic content.
Why does Berlioz place a long slow introduction before the allegro in movement I of Symphonie Fantastique, and how does the idée fixe alter sonata form?
Answer: The Réveries introduction establishes the programmatic frame - reverie, vague passion, melancholy - before the drama of the allegro; in Classical symphonies a slow introduction is optional and shorter, so its scale here signals the work's narrative ambition. The idée fixe (the beloved's melody) enters late as the second subject area's lyrical focus, but unlike a conventional second subject it recurs across all five movements in transformed guise, so its first statement is less a 'contrast' than a psychological leitmotif. In the coda it returns climactically over racing accompaniment, then subsides into a chorale-like close - Berlioz bends exposition, development and coda proportions to serve the programme, prioritising narrative over Classical balance.
