Imperial Image

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level OCR

A* OCR H408/22 revision for Imperial Image: Octavian to Augustus — divi filius, imperator, 'Augustus', culture hero and pater patriae — Res Gestae, Horace, coins, Ara Pacis and the building programme, with 30-mark model answers.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain the benefits and dangers for Augustus of associating himself with Julius Caesar, and how he managed both (H408/22; AO1/AO2)
  • Analyse the presentation of Augustus as imperator — triumphs, Res Gestae, Prima Porta — and evaluate the reality behind it, including Agrippa's role (AO2)
  • Evaluate Augustus' presentation as religious leader: temple restoration, the Lares and genius, Apollo and the Ara Pacis (AO2)
  • Assess the Golden Age imagery, the moral legislation and the imperial family as role models (AO2)
  • Analyse prescribed literary and visual sources for purpose, production, form and audience (AO1/AO2)
  • Deploy secondary scholars (e.g. Zanker, Galinsky, Wallace-Hadrill) to reach substantiated judgements on the effectiveness of the imperial image (AO2)

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

divi filius: 'Son of the Divine (Julius Caesar)' — Octavian's core claim from 42 BC, when the Senate deified Caesar; legitimacy and god-close charisma inherited from his adoptive father.
princeps: 'Leading citizen' — the republican-sounding title behind the principate: first man of the state, not (nominally) king or dictator.
imperator: The acclamation of a victorious commander by his troops; Augustus' use (and monopolisation) of it fused military glory with one-man rule.
triumph: The crowning Roman military-religious ceremony — procession of spoils, captives and the commander in triumphal dress to Jupiter's temple; Augustus celebrated a triple triumph in 29 BC.
Res Gestae Divi Augusti: Augustus' first-person record of his deeds, inscribed at his Mausoleum and copied across the empire (the Ankara Monument survives); the set text for H408/22.
clipeus virtutis: The golden shield (27 BC) the Senate hung in the Curia honouring Augustus' virtus, clementia, iustitia and pietas — the virtues as an official image.
Ara Pacis Augustae: The Altar of Augustan Peace (13–9 BC): procession friezes of the imperial family, myth panels (Aeneas, Roma? — Romulus/Remus, Tellus) and pax-plenty iconography.
genius: The guardian spirit of a man (or the state/household); after 12 BC? (the Lares reform) Augustus' genius was worshipped alongside the Lares at the crossroads shrines.
pater patriae: 'Father of the fatherland' — conferred on Augustus by Senate and people in 2 BC, the culminating title of his self-presentation.
Carmen Saeculare: Horace's hymn commissioned for the Secular Games (17 BC), sung by choirs of boys and girls — the state commissioning poetry for Augustus' new age.

📌 Key Points

H408/22 is 1 hour 45 minutes, 75 marks, 30% of the A Level; Section A compulsory source work, Section B a choice of one from two 30-mark essays (secondary sources credited).
Prescribed literary sources: Res Gestae Divi Augusti; Horace (Epodes 9; Odes 1.37, 3.6, 3.14, 4.4, 4.15; Carmen Saeculare); Ovid (Metamorphoses 15.745–870); Propertius (Elegies 3.4, 3.11, 3.12, 4.6); Suetonius (Life of Augustus, extracts).
Prescribed visual sources: Ara Pacis, Mausoleum, Forum of Augustus, Sebasteion at Aphrodisias, Augustus of Prima Porta, Kalabsha Gate relief, Head of Livia, and eight coins (aurei and denarii, 44–2 BC).
The key topics track his official titles — divi filius, Imperator, 'Augustus', culture hero, Pater Patriae — plus later representations (Suetonius; the Sebasteion).

From Octavian to Augustus: divi filius and the anti-monarchical problem

Caesar's assassination in 44 BC left an 18-year-old heir: Gaius Octavius, Caesar's adoptive son. The association was essential and dangerous. Benefits: the dead Caesar's popularity with the plebs and the army; the settlement of veterans; the glamour of descent from Venus and Aeneas (the Julian clan's illustrious men and gods); and legitimacy — the will made him heir, the deification (Temple of Divus Iulius, 42 BC) made him divi filius. Dangers: Caesar had been murdered for tyranny; 'king' (rex) was poison in a fundamentally anti-monarchical society. Octavian managed both: the comet at the funeral games (Sidus Iulium) backed the apotheosis — the prescribed denarius with the eight-rayed comet beside Augustus' head; while the aureus pairing his bare head with Caesar's laureate one claims inheritance. The triumvirate (43 BC) was openly unconstitutional — hence the justification of civil war in the Res Gestae: those who murdered his father were punished, and those who waged war on the state ('a faction') were conquered in two battles (Philippi). Antony was never named: the war of 32–31 BC was presented as Rome against a woman and her oriental court — Horace's Odes 1.37 makes Cleopatra a fatale monstrum whose death is no ordinary woman's; Epodes 9 celebrates Actium. The settlement of 27 BC renamed him Augustus, 'the revered one', returning (nominally) the res publica to Senate and people — Res Gestae 34 is the set-text core: 'after I had extinguished civil wars... I transferred the state from my own power to the control of the senate and people of Rome'.

The triumvirate itself was openly unconstitutional — a three-man commission of proscription modelled on Sulla's, whose death lists killed Cicero among thousands; the Res Gestae never mentions it. Sextus Pompey's naval blockade starved Italy until Agrippa won Naulochus (36 BC), after which Octavian broke with Lepidus and had only Antony left. The coins are the mass media of the transition: the aureus pairing bare-headed Octavian with the laureate Caesar claims inheritance at the moment of apotheosis; the aureus of Octavian seated on a bench holding a scroll projects clementia and legality; the denarius with Pax holding the olive branch and cornucopia sells peace while civil war still ran. Note how the image meets each audience: the plebs read grain and games, the army read land and pay, the elite read titles, offices and restoration — the same image, different dialects.

Imperator: military glory, its reality and its justification

The image: a capable commander whose wars were glorious, defensive and beneficial. The triple triumph of 29 BC (Dalmatia, Actium, Egypt) staged victory in the Republic's supreme ceremony — its religious pompe, its spoils, its captives; the Res Gestae counts the triumphs, the salutations as imperator, the settlements of veterans and the wars 'won by land and sea'. Reality: Agrippa won Naulochus (36 BC) and commanded at Actium; the Alpine and Balkan campaigns were grinding; the Parthian recovery of the standards (20 BC) was a negotiated settlement, not a war — presented as a triumph (the Prima Porta breastplate's central scene shows a Parthian returning a standard beneath the sky-god, with Apollo and Artemis of Delos below). The prescribed coins package the claims: Pax with olive branch and cornucopia (peace and plenty); sacrificial implements above a tripod and patera (piety as military); the Gaius and Lucius aureus (the heirs as princes of the youth). Justification of civil wars runs through the sources: Philippi avenged the father; the Perusine War is omitted; Sextus Pompey is dismissed as 'a pirate' (Res Gestae 25); Antony's campaign is a war against Cleopatra, not citizens. The Kalabsha Gate relief shows Octavian making offering to Egyptian deities — conqueror as pharaoh, domination dressed as accommodation. For Section B: weigh image against reality — the salutations, triumphs and standard-recovery as image; Agrippa's commands and the body-count as reality; and the Res Gestae's silences as evidence of deliberate shaping.

The triumph's regalia made its message visible: the commander in toga picta and laurel crown on the quadriga, spoils and placards displaying the conquests, captives walking before the chariot, the procession climbing to Jupiter Capitolinus — military glory ending in religion. Augustus' triple triumph spread over three days (Dalmatia, Actium, Egypt), and the Res Gestae counts the salutations as imperator (21 in all), the colonies settled and the sums distributed — each a claim to have converted war into public benefit. The Parthian settlement was dressed as victory twice: the standards' return (20 BC) was celebrated with an arch in the Forum, and the Prima Porta breastplate makes the diplomacy the statue's central image — a negotiated return staged as a conquest won under the sky-god's favour.

'Augustus' and Pater Patriae: religion, the Golden Age, the family

Religious leader. Augustus rebuilt 82 temples (Res Gestae 20), restored lapsed priesthoods, became pontifex maximus (12 BC) and reformed the crossroads cult: the Lares were paired as Lares Praestites and worshipped with the genius Augusti — the state worshipping the ruler's guardian spirit, divinity by association without divinity by claim. His closeness to Apollo was architectural and personal: the Temple of Apollo Palatinus joined his modest house on the Palatine (Suetonius describes the house's plainness as image). The Ara Pacis Augustae (13–9 BC) is the set-text monument: the altar of Augustan peace, its friezes process the imperial family with senators and priests (the real religious elite), its panels flank myth with allegory — Aeneas' sacrifice (pietas), the wolf-and-twins panel and Tellus with babies and plenty (peace's fruits). The Secular Games (17 BC) staged a new age: Horace's Carmen Saeculare, commissioned by the state and sung by choirs of boys and girls, calls on Apollo and Diana for the new saeculum. Culture hero. The myth of Saturn's Golden Age — Augustus as a new Saturn, peace and plenty returning (Horace Odes 4.15; the iconography of Pax, Ceres? — the Ara Pacis' Tellus) — reframed the strongman as restorer.

Pater Patriae and the family. The title (2 BC) completes the Res Gestae's arc — 'father of the fatherland', quoted with the Senate's and people's words (ch. 35). The clipeus virtutis (27 BC) fixed the four virtues: virtus, clementia, iustitia, pietas — clementia after civil war is a pointed image. The moral legislation (lex Iulia de maritandis ordinibus and de adulteriis, 18 BC) incentivised marriage and childbearing, criminalised adultery — and was enforced against his own daughter Julia (Suetonius, Life of Augustus, the extracts on his family discipline). The imperial family as role models: the Gaius and Lucius aureus presents the heirs veiled with shields and priestly symbols; the Head of Livia carries the pudicitia iconography of the Roman matron; Augustus himself modelled modest living — the Prima Porta statue's barefoot pose and Cupid (divine descent via Venus) beside a general's breastplate.

The Secular Games' machinery made the new age official: heralds summoned everyone to a spectacle no one alive had seen; 27 boys and 27 girls of citizen parents sang the Carmen Saeculare by day and by night; sacrifices to Apollo and Diana (and to Juno, the Fates and Ilithyia) closed each sequence. The genius-worship had its own mechanics: the crossroads shrines (compita) rebuilt, with images of the two Lares and the genius Augusti, served by the neighbourhood magistri — the state's religion reaching every street. The moral legislation's penalties were practical as well as symbolic: adultery became a public crime tried by standing courts, with fathers (and in some cases husbands) bound to prosecute, and the childbearing incentives (ius trium liberorum) gave legal privileges to parents of three.

Culture hero, the city of marble, and later representations

The building programme was image made physical. Suetonius' famous claim — he 'found Rome brick and left it marble' (ch. 28–29) — sums the programme: the Forum of Augustus (built from spoils, its Temple of Mars Ultor avenging both Philippi and the standards, its porticoes lined with statues of summi viri and the Julian family — history as image), the Mausoleum (28 BC, a dynastic tomb on the Campus Martius — the Res Gestae's intended home), the Theatre of Marcellus, aqueducts and the Forum's triumphal arches. The Res Gestae closes with the empire's edges: embassies from India, and the closure of the gates of Janus three times (ch. 13) — peace as military achievement.

Evaluating the Res Gestae is the Section B staple. For propaganda: it is first-person, selective and silent where damaging (Antony unnamed; the Perusine War absent; civil wars as 'a faction'); it presents negotiated settlements as victories; its claim to have restored the res publica (ch. 34) coexists with permanent tribunician power and command of army and provinces. For history: its facts check out — the building lists, the dates, the populations enrolled — Mommsen called it 'the queen of inscriptions'; Zanker reads the visual programme (coins, statues, buildings) as a coherent imperial ideology; Wallace-Hadrill stresses its address to every social class; Galinsky treats it as self-conscious self-presentation rather than lying. Its audiences differed: the bronze copies across the empire, bilingual in Greek and Latin, addressed the Greek east as well as Rome; the plebs read grain, games and buildings; the elite read titles and virtue.

Later representations test long-term success. The Sebasteion at Aphrodisias (Claudian era) built three-storey porticoes of reliefs: divinities, emperors, and the ethne — conquered peoples of land and sea — an imperial cult complex that takes Augustus' image for granted a generation after his death. Suetonius' Life of Augustus (2nd century AD) balances the image: clementia, modesty, generosity against adultery, cruelty and luxury in the private extracts — evidence that the self-presentation fixed the terms of debate even when contradicted. A* judgement: the image outlasted its contradictions — that is its success; the final topic is assessing how far it lasted beyond his lifetime, and Suetonius is the proof that it did.

The Forum of Augustus deserves the detail: its porticoes displayed statues of the summi viri — great Romans of the Republic — each with an elogium (career summary), the Julian family at the centre with Aeneas and Romulus flanking; history as a curated image with Augustus' line as its spine. The Mausoleum was begun in 28 BC — the dynastic tomb intended to house the Res Gestae, and the proof that the image was planned for posterity. The Sebasteion at Aphrodisias, built a generation later under Claudius, is the best test of long-term success: three storeys of reliefs — divinities above, emperors below, with panels of the conquered ethne (peoples of land and sea, Britannia among them) — an imperial cult complex in a Greek city that takes Augustus' entire image for granted. Suetonius' extracts make the terms of debate explicit: clementia and modesty in the public chapters against adultery, cruelty and luxury in the private ones — the image survived its contradictions.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: 'The Res Gestae is propaganda rather than history.' How far do you agree with this view? Support your answer with detailed reference to the text. [30]
Mark-scheme logic: AO1 evidence required: RG 1 (punishing Caesar's murderers; the 'faction' that waged war); RG 13 (gates of Janus closed three times); RG 20 (82 temples restored; the Curia, the porticoes); RG 25 (Sextus Pompey as 'a pirate'; the standards recovered); RG 34 ('I transferred the state from my own power to the control of the senate and people'); RG 35 (pater patriae, with the Senate's and people's words). AO2 must weigh both halves. Propaganda: first-person and selective — Antony is never named; the Perusine War is absent; civil wars are a 'faction'; the Parthian settlement (RG 29) is presented as victory without a war; 'restoring the res publica' coexists with tribunicia potestas and permanent command — the language of restoration masks the reality of one-man rule. History: the facts are verifiable and accurate — buildings, offices, dates, populations; Mommsen called it the queen of inscriptions; Galinsky reads it as self-conscious self-presentation, not falsehood; Wallace-Hadrill stresses its address to different audiences (bilingual copies for the Greek east; the plebs, the army, the elite). Scholars to deploy: Zanker (the visual programme as coherent ideology); Galinsky (Augustan Culture); Wallace-Hadrill (Augustan Rome). Top-band judgement: the dichotomy is false — the Res Gestae is history that works as propaganda: accurate facts, selected silences; its power lies in what it omits (Antony, civil-war guilt), so the top answer argues that its silences, not its lies, make it the most effective image-making document of the reign.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

'Augustus' success rested on the image, not the reality, of military victory.' How far do you agree? [30]
Answer: For image: the Parthian standards were recovered by negotiation (20 BC) but presented as victory (Prima Porta breastplate; Res Gestae); Agrippa won Naulochus and commanded at Actium; the triple triumph of 29 BC packaged civil-war victories in republican ceremony; Horace's Odes 1.37 turns a failed opponent into a fatale monstrum. For reality: the settlements were real — veterans settled, frontiers pushed (Alps, Balkans), the empire expanded; Augustus' monopoly of the imperator salutation reflected real command; the gates of Janus closures (RG 13) marked real peace. Scholars: Goldsworthy (Augustus: From Revolutionary to Emperor — the military reality); Zanker (the visual programme as ideology). Judgement: the reality was solid enough to be packaged — the image worked because the victories were mostly real; where they were not (Parthia), the image substituted; a top answer distinguishes the two and shows the packaging was the constant.
How effectively did Augustus present himself as a religious leader? [30]
Answer: Effective: 82 temples restored (RG 20); pontifex maximus from 12 BC; the genius Augusti worshipped with the Lares — the state absorbed the ruler's spirit; the Temple of Apollo Palatinus beside his modest house (Suetonius); the Ara Pacis' processions of the real religious elite with the imperial family; the Carmen Saeculare commissioned for 17 BC. Limits: he never claimed divinity in Rome (divi filius was inherited, not asserted as living divinity); the moral legislation's enforcement (Julia's exile) sat awkwardly with the image of pater familias of the state; Suetonius' private extracts show luxury and superstition beneath the image. Scholars: Zanker (the fusion of religious and political imagery); Wallace-Hadrill (the household as model for the state). Judgement: highly effective as association — piety, Apollo, the genius and the Ara Pacis gave him religious standing without the fatal claim of living divinity; the effectiveness was in the restraint as much as the claim.
'The building programme was Augustus' most successful image-making tool.' How far do you agree? [30]
Answer: For: Suetonius — he found Rome brick and left it marble; the Forum of Augustus staged history (summi viri, Mars Ultor) as a permanent image; the Mausoleum anchored the dynasty; the Ara Pacis fused family, piety and peace in marble; buildings reached every class daily — the plebs read grain, games, aqueducts and baths. Against: the Res Gestae reached the empire through bronze copies and was the culminating text; coins reached every hand and pocket — mass media beyond any building; poetry (Horace, Propertius) reached the elite with Augustan ideas; the building programme's image depended on the texts that interpreted it. Scholars: Zanker (the programme as coherent ideology); Thorpe (Roman Architecture). Judgement: buildings were the most visible tool, but not the only one — the image was a system (text, coin, stone) and a top answer argues the system, not the single tool, was the success; the Res Gestae's role as capstone deserves the counter-case.
How useful are the coins for understanding Augustus' self-presentation to different social classes? [30]
Answer: Useful: coins reached every hand — the one medium that crossed class lines; the comet denarius (divi filius), the Caesar aureus (inheritance), the Pax denarius (peace and plenty), the sacrificial-implements denarius (piety), the Gaius and Lucius aureus (dynasty) — each strand of the image in pocket-sized form; the aurei speak to the elite and army, the denarii to the plebs. Limits: coins carry slogans, not arguments — iconography is compressed and ambiguous; reading them depends on context (hoards, mints, dates); the illiterate majority read images, not words, so the labels are elite readings of visual messages. Scholars: Zanker (coins as the visual programme's core); Wallace-Hadrill (image aimed at every social class). Judgement: uniquely useful for the image's reach and strands, but they show what was projected, not what was received — a top answer pairs them with the buildings and texts and notes the limit of inferring reception.
'Horace's Odes 1.37 shows that Augustan image-making could override a poet's own history.' How far do you agree? [30]
Answer: For: Horace had fought at Philippi on the republican side ('we were a useless band after our leader fled') — yet Odes 1.37 turns Cleopatra into a fatale monstrum and her death into a release ('now drink!'); the ode celebrates Actium in the regime's terms while conceding Cleopatra's nobility ('not like a woman, no ordinary one'); the poet of the defeated adopted the victor's frame. Against: the ode is not simple propaganda — Cleopatra's dignity is genuine, the drink is republicans' wine, the tone is ambivalent; Horace's independence within the image (Odes 3.6's moral criticism) shows the regime buying poets, not silencing them; Propertius' Elegies 3.4 and 4.6 show similar tension. Scholars: Galinsky (Augustan poets as participants, not mouthpieces); Wallace-Hadrill (image as negotiation with the elite). Judgement: the image set the terms, and Horace's own history shaped his compliance — but compliance was nuanced; a top answer reads 1.37's ambivalence as the evidence that the image was negotiable, not imposed.
