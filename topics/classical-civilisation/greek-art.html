Greek Art

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level OCR

A* OCR H408/24 revision for Greek Art: kouroi and korai, classical bronze, the Parthenon sculptures, pediments and metopes, black- and red-figure vases — function, technique, composition and context, with full model answers.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Analyse the functions of free-standing sculpture (cult, votive, grave marker, memorial), architectural sculpture and pot shapes (H408/24; AO1/AO2)
  • Evaluate the effects, advantages and limitations of materials — marble and bronze, limestone, clay and slip — and of damaged, reconstructed and copied pieces (AO2)
  • Trace the stylistic development of the kouros and kore, the bronze statue, and the black- and red-figure techniques (AO2)
  • Analyse composition — dominant axes, chiastic composition, contrapposto, foreshortening, torsion, movement and emotion (AO2)
  • Analyse subject matter — mythological and everyday themes — and how the social, political and religious context shaped the art (AO1/AO2)
  • Deploy secondary scholars (e.g. Boardman, Woodford, Moignard, Jenkins) and reach substantiated judgements on the prescribed sources (AO2)

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

kouros: An archaic Greek free-standing marble statue of a nude youth, standing frontal with one foot advanced; developed from geometric rigidity (New York Kouros, c.600 BC) towards naturalism (Aristodikos, c.500 BC).
kore: The female counterpart — a draped standing maiden, usually a votive offering in a sanctuary (e.g. the Peplos Kore from the Athenian Acropolis); flesh painted white.
contrapposto: The classical stance: weight on one leg, so hips and shoulders tilt in opposite directions — the naturalistic balance of the Doryphoros.
chiastic composition: Symmetrical, A-B-B-A balance of body parts (tense/relaxed alternating) that organises Polykleitos' Doryphoros and his Canon of proportions.
canon (kanon): Polykleitos' treatise on ideal proportions (symmetria), exemplified by the Doryphoros — 'the' statue by which sculptors learned proportion.
foreshortening: Representing depth on a two-dimensional surface — receding limbs and shields drawn in perspective (Euthymides' experiments on the Three Men Carousing amphora).
torsion: Twisting of the torso to imply violent movement in space — Euphronios' Herakles and Antaios; Myron's Diskobolos.
black-figure: Vase-painting technique: figures painted in slip silhouette on the clay, details incised, added white (female flesh) and purple; dominant to c.530 BC.
red-figure: The reverse: figures reserved in clay against a black slip ground; details painted with brushes and diluted slip — finer line, foreshortening, emotion; dominant from c.530 BC.
chryselephantine: A cult statue of gold and ivory over a wooden core — Pheidias' Athena Parthenos (Parthenon) and Zeus at Olympia.

📌 Key Points

H408/24 is 1 hour 45 minutes, 75 marks, 30% of the A Level; it is the only Culture and the Arts component of pure visual/material culture — no set literary texts.
Prescribed free-standing sculpture (16 works): New York Kouros; Kleobis and Biton; Berlin Standing Goddess; Anavysos Kouros; Peplos Kore; Aristodikos Kouros; Delphic Charioteer; Artemision Zeus; Diskobolos (Myron); Doryphoros (Polykleitos); Aphrodite of the Agora; Eirene and Ploutos (Kephisodotos); Hermes and Dionysus; Aphrodite of Knidos (Praxiteles); Apoxyomenos (Lysippos); Antikytheran Youth.
Prescribed architectural sculpture: pediments of Corcyra, Aphaia at Aigina, Zeus at Olympia and the Parthenon; metopes of Temple C (Selinus), Olympia and Parthenon XXVI–XXVIII; Ionic friezes of the Siphnian Treasury, the Parthenon and the Temple of Apollo Epicurius at Bassae.
Prescribed vases: the Gorgon Painter, Sophilos, the François Vase (Kleitias), the Amasis Painter, Exekias, the Lysippides and Andokides Painters, the Kleophrades Painter, the Berlin Painter, Euphronios, Euthymides and the Pan Painter.

Function, materials and technique

Free-standing sculpture had four functions: cult statue (the god's image in a temple — chryselephantine Athena Parthenos, Zeus at Olympia), votive (a dedication to a god — most kouroi and korai stood in sanctuaries), grave marker (the Anavysos Kouros for Kroisos; the Aristodikos) and memorial (the Delphic Charioteer commemorates Polyzalos' victory). Architectural sculpture worked by position: Doric metopes (relief panels between triglyphs, usually fighting scenes), Ionic friezes (continuous narrative bands — the Siphnian Treasury, the Parthenon) and pediments (the gable tympana, where a centrepiece anchors flanking figures that shrink towards the corners). Pot shapes map uses: amphorae for storage and transport, kraters for mixing wine, hydriai for water, oinochoai for pouring, kylikes for drinking, lekythoi for oil — shape dictates which scenes suit which vessel (symposium scenes on kraters and cups; maenads on wine vessels).

Materials: marble (block-carving; luminous, but heavy and inflexible) vs bronze (lost-wax casting, hollow and strong — allowing free-standing action poses, chased detail, added metals for lips, teeth and eyes: the Delphic Charioteer's inlaid glass eyes and copper lashes). Limestone survives at Corcyra's pediment; marble dominates later. In vase-painting, clay, slip and added colour (white, purple) carry the design. Limitations drive the exam: damaged pieces (the Antikytheran Youth; the Peplos Kore's missing arm — attribute debated), reconstructions (the Hermes and Dionysus' right arm is a restoration controversy) and copies (most classical bronzes survive only as Roman marble copies — the Diskobolos and Doryphoros; the copy preserves pose and proportion but loses the bronze's surfaces, eyes and balance). Always ask what a piece can and cannot tell us.

The Parthenon metopes show the four great battles in position: the Gigantomachy (east), the Amazonomachy (west), the Centauromachy (south — the prescribed XXVI–XXVIII among them) and the Ilioupersis (north) — gods over giants, Greeks over Amazons, Lapiths over Centaurs, Greeks over Trojans. Grave markers could be kouroi (the Anavysos) or stelai with relief scenes; memorial sculpture (the Delphic Charioteer) commemorated victory rather than death. Panathenaic prize amphorae are the shape-function link in action: the shape (wide shoulders, black-figure, armed Athena between columns) was fixed by convention for the prize oil, and prize amphorae kept black-figure long after red-figure conquered the market.

Development of style: kouroi, korai, bronzes and vases

The kouros develops from geometric rigidity to naturalism: the New York Kouros (c.600 BC) is frontal, block-like, with geometric anatomy; Kleobis and Biton (c.580 BC, Delphi — Herodotus 1.31 tells their story of a blessed death) are Argive, stockier; the Anavysos Kouros (Kroisos, c.530 BC) shows fleshed musculature and the archaic smile; the Aristodikos Kouros (c.500 BC) is shorter-haired, more proportional — the formula dissolving before the classical pose. The kore develops through drapery and adornment: the Berlin Standing Goddess (c.570 BC) wears layered drapery; the Peplos Kore (c.530–540 BC, Acropolis) is plainer, with painted patterns and added-white flesh — colour, now lost, was central. Bronze moves to action: the Delphic Charioteer (c.470 BC, Severe style, inlaid eyes) stands at rest within his chariot; the Artemision Zeus (or Poseidon — the attribute is debated; c.460 BC) strides with arms extended; the Diskobolos (Myron, c.450 BC) twists at the instant before release; the Doryphoros (Polykleitos, c.440 BC) perfects contrapposto and the Canon; the Antikytheran Youth (c.340 BC) shows late proportional change; the Aphrodite of the Agora, Eirene and Ploutos (Kephisodotos, c.370 BC — allegory of peace and wealth), Hermes and Dionysus (Praxitelean S-curve), the Aphrodite of Knidos (Praxiteles, c.340 BC — the first monumental female nude) and the Apoxyomenos (Lysippos, c.330 BC — smaller head, new eight-head proportions, arms breaking into the viewer's space) complete the arc.

Black-figure develops through line and pattern: the Gorgon Painter's dinos (frontal Gorgon faces, profile bodies); Sophilos' dinos and the François Vase (Kleitias, c.570 BC — miniaturist, labelled figures, multiple myth cycles); the Amasis Painter's neck amphora (refined line); Exekias' Achilles and Ajax playing dice (c.540 BC — psychological tension, dignity, the spears' verticals) and Dionysus sailing on the ocean kylix (full-frontal innovation; the mast becomes a vine). Red-figure inverts the technique and unlocks drawing: the Andokides Painter's bilingual amphora (both techniques on one pot); the Pioneers — Euphronios (Herakles and Antaios: torsion, musculature, foreshortened feet; Herakles and the Amazons) and Euthymides (Three Men Carousing — foreshortening 'as never Euphronios [did]'; Hector arming) — compete in experiments and sign their work; the Berlin Painter (Achilles and Hector/Memnon — single figures on black ground) refines; the Kleophrades Painter (the Trojan War hydria — pathos in the sack of Troy; Dionysus and the Maenads) and the Pan Painter (Perseus and Medusa; Boreas pursuing Oreithyia — wit and movement) extend the range. Mannerists deliberately revive archaic manner in red-figure after 480 BC. For each work: appearance, style, content, original location, and what it can and cannot tell us.

Details the exam expects: Kleobis and Biton were dedicated by Argos at Delphi, and Herodotus (1.31) tells their story — they drew their mother's cart to the temple and died in their sleep, blessed by the goddess; the Peplos Kore's missing arm once held an attribute that is debated (a bow, a pomegranate, a bird); the Artemision Zeus (or Poseidon) is identified by the attribute the empty hand once held — thunderbolt or trident; the Doryphoros survives through copies (Naples; Pompeii) that transmit pose and proportion; the Aphrodite of Knidos' fame is recorded by Pliny (Natural History 36), with the Knidians' refusal to sell her; the Apoxyomenos' scraping pose breaks the figure's bounds — the arms extend into the viewer's space, completing Lysippos' new proportions (smaller head, taller body).

Composition and the method of analysis

Section A commentary questions reward a fixed method. Analyse in this order:

  1. Composition — dominant verticals, horizontals and diagonals: Exekias' dice amphora builds a triangle of cloaks under the spears' verticals; the Doryphoros' chiastic balance (tense right leg/relaxed right arm; relaxed left leg/tense left arm); contrapposto in every classical stance.
  2. Pose, anatomy and movement — portrayal of the body from geometric outline (New York Kouros) to musculature (Anavysos) to idealised harmony (Doryphoros); torsion (Diskobolos; Euphronios' Antaios); foreshortening (Euthymides' shields and feet); movement captured between poses (the Artemision Zeus' stride).
  3. Emotion — pathos: the Kleophrades Painter's Trojan War hydria (women's grief); Exekias' dice players' concentration; the Hermes and Dionysus' tenderness; the archaic smile's ambiguity.
  4. Evaluation — what the piece tells us about function, technique and context, and what it cannot; the range of ancient and modern interpretations.

A* answers link technique to effect and effect to context: contrapposto is not just a stance but a claim about the ideal body; foreshortening is not just a trick but a claim about the picture plane. The paper's question types (short answer, 10-mark stimulus, 20- and 30-mark essays) reward this structure at every length.

Use the method on the whole range: the Berlin Painter refines red-figure to single figures on the black ground (Achilles and Memnon on the volute krater — the figures' edges against the black field are the technique's clarity at its height); the Kleophrades Painter's Trojan War hydria (a water pot — the sack of Troy on the vessel women used) places pathos at the centre: Neoptolemos swings Astyanax onto Hector's blood; the women grieve; Priam's figure closes the scene. Emotion's portrayal develops with the techniques: the archaic smile's ambiguity, the Severe style's gravity, the classical pathos of the late 5th century BC (the hydria) and the 4th century's grace (Hermes and Dionysus).

Subject matter and contextual analysis

Mythology dominates, but everyday life is reflected inside it: the Wedding of Peleus and Thetis (Sophilos; the François Vase) mirrors Athenian wedding processions; maenads and symposiasts decorate wine vessels for symposium use; Achilles arming (Euthymides) mirrors the hoplite's own kit. Free-standing themes: mortals and gods (the Delphic Charioteer as a mortal's dedication; the Artemision Zeus' attribute debate); 4th-century concepts — the Aphrodite of Knidos (sensuality and religion; Pliny records the Knidians refusing to sell her), Eirene and Ploutos (peace and wealth as allegory after the Peloponnesian War). Architectural themes: Medusa (Corcyra's west pediment — apotropaic, terrifying at the threshold); the Centauromachy (Olympia and the Parthenon metopes — order over barbarism, read by many as a Persian Wars subtext); the Trojan War (Aphaia's east and west pediments — Greek vs Trojan heroes; the Parthenon's metopes); Olympia's pediments (the chariot race of Pelops and Oenomaus; the Lapiths and Centaurs) and Herakles' labours in its metopes. Vase themes: Achilles (Exekias' dice players; the Berlin Painter's Achilles and Memnon), Athena (on the Panathenaic-type amphorae), Dionysus (Exekias' sailing kylix; the Kleophrades Painter's maenads), Herakles (Euphronios' Antaios and Amazons), and the Trojan War (the Kleophrades Painter's hydria).

Contextual analysis is the A* skill. The social, political and religious context shaped the art: the Persian Wars (480–479 BC) turned battle myths (Centauromachy, Amazonomachy, Gigantomachy) into allegories of Greek order over Persian chaos; the Acropolis' rebuilding after the Persian destruction (the Perserschutt buried and preserved the korai); symposium culture dictated vessel shapes; sanctuary economies funded votives. Limits: original paint is lost — the modern white marble and terracotta are illusions; workshop practice is inferred; female painters are unknown. Interpretations differ ancient and modern: cult statues awed worshippers, museums aestheticise them; the Aphrodite of Knidos scandalised and attracted in equal measure (Pliny's anecdote); modern scholarship (Woodford, Boardman, Moignard) reads style chronologically. A* answers evaluate usefulness — what the sources prove about technique, function and context — and mark their limitations honestly.

Olympia's pediments pair a local myth with a Panhellenic one: the east pediment's chariot race of Pelops and Oenomaus (the sanctuary's founding myth of the games) and the west's Centauromachy (Lapiths vs Centaurs — order over barbarism); the metopes show the labours of Herakles (the Cretan Bull, the Garden of the Hesperides, the Stables of Augeas among the prescribed) — the Panhellenic hero at the Panhellenic games. The Perserschutt detail is the exam's best example of context preserving art: the Athenians buried the damaged korai and kouroi of the destroyed Acropolis, which preserved them for us; the rebuilt Parthenon replaced the damaged old temple of Athena. On interpretations: cult statues' awe (Pheidias' Zeus) vs museum aesthetics; the Aphrodite of Knidos' scandal and fame (Pliny); and the modern scholarly readings (Woodford's chronological method, Boardman's stylistic surveys, Moignard's vase analysis, Jenkins' architectural sculpture) — cite them to support, not to decorate.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: 'The Doryphoros embodies the ideals of Polykleitos' Canon.' How far do you agree with this view? Support your answer with detailed reference to the sculpture. [30]
Mark-scheme logic: AO1 evidence required: the Canon (Polykleitos' treatise on symmetria — ideal proportions); the Doryphoros' contrapposto (weight on the right leg, left foot back on its toes); the chiastic balance of tense and relaxed limbs; the spear resting on the left shoulder; the head's proportion to the body; the work's fame in antiquity as the exemplar of proportion. AO2 must weigh the claim. For: the pose embodies the Canon — every limb counterbalances another (chiastic composition); the proportions were so celebrated that the statue was called the Canon in bronze (the ancient view, quoted by Galen); contrapposto resolves the kouros' frontal rigidity — this is the classical ideal realised. Against: we know it only through Roman marble copies — the bronze original's surfaces, inlaid eyes and balance are lost, so the copy preserves proportion but not material effect; the spear is a modern restoration (the original's angle is debated); whether the surviving copies match the treatise's ratio exactly is unknowable; and 'ideals' implies moral as well as formal perfection — the statue offers the formal claim, the moral reading is modern. Scholars: Boardman (Greek Sculpture: the Classical Period — the Canon and its copies); Woodford (how copies transmit and distort). Top-band judgement: agree substantially on the formal claim — contrapposto and chiastic balance are the Canon made visible — but the evidence is a copy of a lost original, so the claim rests on proportion, not on the bronze itself; the top answer separates what the copies prove from what the treatise asserts.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

'The Parthenon metopes are propaganda for Athens.' How far do you agree? [30]
Answer: For propaganda: the metopes (XXVI–XXVIII among the prescribed) show the Gigantomachy, Amazonomachy and Centauromachy — order defeating barbarism, read since antiquity as a Persian Wars allegory; the Parthenon as a whole (frieze, pediments, chryselephantine Athena) was built from Delian League funds after Persian destruction; the west pediment's contest (Athena vs Poseidon) claims the city's founding myth. Against: the metopes' subjects are traditional myths — Panhellenic, not specifically Athenian; the temple honoured Athena, and the frieze's procession is religious, not military; the allegorical reading of myth-as-Persia is modern inference, not inscription; Olympia's Centauromachy (pre-Parthenon) used the same theme without an Athenian frame. Scholars: Boardman (the Parthenon as Athenian self-image); Jenkins (Greek Architecture and its Sculpture — the programme's unity). Judgement: the myths are Panhellenic but the building is Athenian — context, not subject matter, makes the propaganda reading work; a top answer argues the composite programme (frieze + pediments + metopes + cult statue) rather than the metopes alone.
How successfully does the Exekias amphora depicting Achilles and Ajax playing dice convey character and emotion? [30]
Answer: Composition: the spears' verticals frame a triangle of cloaks — two warriors at rest inside a formal design; the eyes: one profile, one frontal — Exekias' signature mix; the inscription of the throws (four and three; Achilles winning) stages the outcome. Character: Achilles' helmet on his head, Ajax's on his shield — readiness vs relaxation; the dignity of great heroes at play; the tragedy foreshadowed (both die after this moment). Emotion: concentration, quiet rivalry, the tension of the game — no pathos, but supreme psychological control. Evaluation: Exekias' black-figure line achieves what red-figure later does with brushwork; the frontal eye and profile mix show the technique's limits and his mastery of them. Scholars: Boardman (Athenian Black Figure Vases — Exekias as the peak of the technique); Moignard (Greek Vases — reading the composition). Judgement: highly successful — the scene is the proof that character, not action, could carry a vase; a top answer ties every formal detail (verticals, cloaks, eyes, inscriptions) to the emotional effect.
'Red-figure was a technical advance, but black-figure never lost its appeal.' How far do you agree? [30]
Answer: Technical advance: red-figure frees the line — details painted with brushes and diluted slip rather than incised; finer anatomy, foreshortening (Euthymides' shields), emotion (the Kleophrades Painter's Trojan War hydria); the bilingual amphora of the Lysippides and Andokides Painters shows the difference on one pot; the Pioneers (Euphronios, Euthymides) competed in experiments. Black-figure's appeal: Exekias' dice amphora and Dionysus kylix (c.540 BC) are the technical peak of any period — his line, dignity and design outclass most red-figure; bilingual pots show both surviving; Panathenaic amphorae kept the technique into the 4th century BC (the prescribed BM 1856,1001.1 is red-figure, but the Panathenaic prize series remained black-figure); the Gorgon Painter and François Vase remain the key sources for early narrative. Scholars: Boardman (both volumes — the techniques' relative strengths); Moignard (the market's tastes). Judgement: agree on the advance, but the appeal is proven by the bilinguals and the Panathenaic series — black-figure persisted where its strengths (silhouette, decoration) suited; a top answer weighs technique against market and function.
'Roman copies of Greek bronzes tell us more about Rome than about Greece.' How far do you agree? [30]
Answer: For Rome: the copies are Roman in material (marble, not bronze), context (villa and gymnasium decoration) and purpose (collecting); the copyists' mistakes, restorations (the Hermes and Dionysus' right arm; the Diskobolos' additions) and supports reflect Roman taste; Pliny's rankings are Roman judgements. For Greece: the copies preserve pose, proportion and composition of lost originals (the Doryphoros, Diskobolos, Apoxyomenos) — without them the classical bronze tradition is nearly invisible; they transmit the Canon, contrapposto and torsion; inlaid eyes and chased detail can be inferred. Scholars: Boardman (the copy problem); Woodford (An Introduction to Greek Art — what copies can and cannot tell us). Judgement: the copies are Roman objects transmitting Greek information — a top answer separates the two layers and argues the Greek information survives (pose, proportion) while the Greek material (bronze, eyes, surfaces) does not; the claim 'more about Rome' underrates the transmission.
'The Aphrodite of Knidos marks a revolution in the portrayal of women.' How far do you agree? [30]
Answer: For revolution: the first monumental female nude (Praxiteles, c.340 BC) — the female body as subject, not drapery; the modesty gesture makes the nude self-aware; Eirene and Ploutos (Kephisodotos, c.370 BC) shows the earlier draped maternal type it replaced; the Aphrodite of the Agora shows the transitional pose; Pliny records its fame and the Knidians' refusal to sell it. Against: the revolution is male-authored — the nude female body is the male gaze objectified (the modesty gesture is part of the display); divine and mythological subjects, not real women, were represented; korai (the Peplos Kore) had shown draped, idealised women centuries earlier; the 4th century's other female statues (Hermes and Dionysus' imagery) stay conventional. Scholars: Boardman (Greek Sculpture: the Classical Period); Woodford (the nude's tradition). Judgement: a revolution in form, not in gender politics — the female nude was new, but it served male viewers and divine subjects; a top answer distinguishes the formal innovation from its social meaning.
