The World of the Hero

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level OCR

A* OCR H408/11 revision for The World of the Hero: Homer's Iliad and Odyssey — oral tradition, epithets and formulae, heroism (kleos/timē), gods, xenia and family — with 30-mark model essays citing the prescribed scholars.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain the oral-formulaic nature of the Homeric epics and evaluate competing theories of composition (H408/11; AO1/AO2)
  • Analyse how epithets, formulae, type-scenes, speeches and similes shape meaning in the Iliad and Odyssey (AO2)
  • Analyse the concept, values and behaviour of the hero — timē, kleos, mēnis, nostos, mētis — using the prescribed books (AO2)
  • Evaluate the role of the immortals and their relationship with mortals in both epics (AO2)
  • Analyse xenia, family, the position of women and the role of slaves across the societies depicted (AO2)
  • Deploy secondary scholars (e.g. Parry and Lord, Griffin, Nicholson, Weil) to support analysis and argument (AO2)
  • Reach substantiated judgements on possible ancient and modern interpretations of both epics (AO1/AO2)

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

kleos: Glory or fame won through great deeds, especially in song; kleos aphthiton ('imperishable glory') is the epic's promise that heroic deeds live on in poetry after death.
timē: Honour — material and social standing expressed through prizes (gera), booty, seats of precedence and public recognition; its loss triggers the plot of the Iliad.
mēnis: Wrath — the first word of the Iliad; the divine-scale anger of Achilles, distinguished from ordinary anger by its cosmic consequences.
nostos: Homecoming; the governing theme of the Odyssey, which follows Odysseus' long-delayed return to Ithaca and the restoration of his oikos.
mētis: Cunning intelligence; Odysseus' defining quality (epithet polutropos, 'of many turns'), deployed through disguise, lies and stratagem rather than force.
xenia: Guest-friendship: a reciprocal pact protected by Zeus Xenios obliging hosts to shelter, feed and gift guests before questioning them, and guests to behave with restraint.
aristeia: A hero's peak of battle-excellence; Achilles' aristeia spans Iliad 20–22, culminating in the killing of Hector.
formula: A repeated phrase fixed to a character or action (e.g. 'swift-footed Achilles', 'rosy-fingered Dawn') that fits the dactylic hexameter and enabled oral composition.
type-scene: A recurring narrative template (arming, sacrifice, supplication, arrival) varied for each occasion — an oral-compositional building block.
moira: Fate or allotted portion; it bounds even the gods, as when Zeus weighs the fates of Sarpedon (Iliad 16) and Hector (Iliad 22) on his golden scales.

Prescribed Iliad books: 1, 3, 4, 6, 9, 10, 16, 17, 18, 19, 22, 23, 24. Prescribed Odyssey books: 1, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 16, 17, 18, 19, 21, 22, 23. Prescribed Aeneid books: 1, 2, 4, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12.

The qualification: what the paper demands

H408/11 The World of the Hero is the compulsory component: 2 hours 30 minutes, 100 marks, 40% of the A Level. You study one of Homer's Iliad or Odyssey in depth, and Virgil's Aeneid, which all candidates study. The paper has three sections: Section A focuses solely on Homer and contains two sets of questions — one on the Iliad, one on the Odyssey — and you answer on the text you studied; Section B contains questions on the Aeneid, all compulsory; Section C offers a stimulus (a passage from Homer and one from Virgil) plus a choice of essays, in which you are expected to make use of secondary sources and academic views.

There are three question types: a 10-mark stimulus question, a 20-mark essay and a 30-mark essay. The 30-mark essays are marked against Levels of Response grids that explicitly credit the use of secondary sources, scholars and academic works — so A* answers must weave in scholarship (e.g. Parry and Lord on oral tradition; Griffin; Nicholson's The Mighty Dead; Weil's The Iliad, or the Poem of Force) rather than bolt it on.

Prescribed books: Iliad 1, 3, 4, 6, 9, 10, 16, 17, 18, 19, 22, 23, 24; Odyssey 1, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 16, 17, 18, 19, 21, 22, 23; Aeneid 1, 2, 4, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12. Any translation is permitted; printed passages are taken from Rieu (Penguin) and Kline (poetryintranslation.com) for Homer, and West (Penguin) and Kline for Virgil.

Plan the paper by weight: the 30-mark essays are marked on Levels of Response grids (knowledge, analysis, evaluation and scholars), the 20-mark essays on knowledge and analysis, the 10-mark stimulus on direct response to the printed passage. On this component AO1 is worth 18% and AO2 22% of the A Level — analysis and evaluation dominate, so A* preparation means practising arguments, not recapping plots. Section C's stimulus questions require you to read the printed Homer and Virgil passages cold and to write with secondary views in support; its essays expect comparison across the epics.

Oral tradition, composition and literary technique

The epics were composed orally in dactylic hexameter long before writing was used for literature. Milman Parry and Albert Lord demonstrated that the poems are built from formulae (fixed phrases that fill metrical slots), type-scenes and recurring themes — the toolkit of bards who compose-in-performance, as Lord observed among Yugoslav guslars. Rhapsodes performed Homer at festivals such as the Panathenaia; tradition credits the Athenian tyrant Peisistratus with a 6th-century BC 'recension' that standardised the text. The great unresolved question — one poet or many? — divides Analysts (who detected seams and inconsistencies), Unitarians (who stressed artistic unity) and the oral-formulaic middle ground: consistent characterisation and design (Griffin, Edwards) coexist with formulaic diction, which most scholars now treat as compatible with a single monumental poet working within an oral tradition (Nicholson stresses how the poems preserve Bronze Age memory within an 8th-century BC composition).

For analysis, always ask what the technique does:

  • Epithets ('swift-footed Achilles', 'Odysseus of many wiles', 'rosy-fingered Dawn') serve metre first, but also fix characterisation — Hector is still 'glorious' even in defeat, reinforcing the code he dies by. Some formulae are deliberately context-stretching: dead warriors speak words 'fitting the moment'.
  • Type-scenes (arming, sacrifice, supplication, arrival) create expectation and then variation: Achilles' arming in Iliad 19 dwells on the new armour made by Hephaestus; Priam's supplication in 24 inverts the heroic refusal of the embassy in 9.
  • Similes create a second world: lions, storms and fires transfer emotion from battlefield to nature, while domestic similes (the weeping woman of Iliad 22; Odysseus crying like a widow captured at the fall of her city, Odyssey 8) reverse the comparison to expose what war costs. Extended similes can foreshadow and mark significance.
  • Speeches stage debate — the embassy in Iliad 9 is a structured agon of offer and refusal; divine speeches (Athena's restraint of Achilles' sword, Iliad 1) drive dual motivation.

Odyssey technique adds flashback: the poem opens in medias res, and Books 9–12 (the Apologoi) retrospectively recount the wanderings. The structure is itself thematic: nostos delayed by divine hostility, achieved by mētis.

Ring composition frames both epics: the Iliad opens with Chryses' supplication (a priest dishonoured) and closes with Priam's (a father dishonoured) — the frame is the theme of timē and its limits; Achilles' two speeches in Books 1 and 24 mirror each other. Type-scenes build the structure: the supplication chain runs through the poem (Chryses 1; Lycaon 21; Priam 24), and the arming scenes (Achilles in 19 with divine armour) mark the turning points. The dactylic hexameter itself (six feet, long-short-short) is the engine of formulae — names and phrases chosen for their metrical shape — and the Peisistratean recension tradition is the transmission's evidence that the text was fixed for Panathenaic performance by the 6th century BC.

Heroism: kleos, timē and the redefinition of the hero

Iliad. The heroic code demands timē — material honour and public standing. Achilles' mēnis begins when Agamemnon strips Briseis, a geras that embodies his timē (Iliad 1); his withdrawal is a protest against dishonour, and Athena's intervention makes his anger simultaneously divine and human (dual motivation). In the embassy of Book 9, Odysseus, Phoenix and Ajax offer restitution, and Achilles articulates the two fates: a long, obscure life at home, or a short life with imperishable kleos (kleos aphthiton). Crucially, he questions the code itself — 'one and the same lot for the man who hangs back and the man who battles hard' — the first crack in the heroic worldview. Patroclus' death converts withdrawal into revenge; the aristeia of Books 20–22 (including the killing of the suppliant Lycaon in 21) shows heroism at its most brutal, before Book 24 transfigures it: Priam's supplication, the kiss of the hands that killed his son, Zeus' two urns of good and evil, and the shared weeping of two men bound by mortality produce pity beyond the code. Weil reads the Iliad as 'the poem of force' — force turns people into things — while Griffin stresses the poem's pathos and humanity.

Odyssey. The poem revalues heroism around nostos and mētis. Odysseus' polutropos intelligence — disguise, false tales, the stratagems of Ithaca — replaces brute valour; he refuses Calypso's offer of immortality (Odyssey 5), choosing wife and home over kleos among the dead, an explicit reversal of Achilles' choice in Iliad 9. Heroism is also endurance (the tortures of Books 5–12; the restraint of the Beggar in his own hall) and justice: the slaughter of the suitors in Book 22 punishes their systemic abuse of xenia. Recognition is progressive — Telemachus (16), Eurycleia's scar (19), the bed test (23), Laertes in the orchard (24) — each testing identity against memory and knowledge. Penelope is the moral centre: her weaving trick (2) and her test of the bed (23) show mētis equal to her husband's, and her fidelity wins 'a fame that reaches the heavens' (24) — kleos for a woman won through virtue, not arms.

A* judgement should ask whether the two ideals conflict: the Iliad's hero wins glory by dying young; the Odyssey's by surviving home — Homer seems to expose the cost of the first by dramatising the value of the second.

The prescribed books supply the evidence: Patroclus' aristeia and death (16) drive Achilles back to war; the shield of Achilles (18) — Hephaestus' two cities at peace and at war — is the poem's image of the world the war is destroying; Hector's farewell (6) and the ransom (24) frame the family theme. The embassy (9) stages the code's three voices: Odysseus (offer and timē), Phoenix (the story of Meleager — the parable of the hero who waits too long) and Ajax (the appeal to friendship); Achilles' reply questions the code — and Book 24 answers him with pity rather than honour.

Gods, xenia, family and society

The immortals are anthropomorphic — they wound (Aphrodite, Iliad 5; Ares), grieve, quarrel and even lose their horses — yet their scope of power exceeds mortals', and they keep the plot in motion. Their relationship with mortals is reciprocal: honour flows upward through sacrifice, prayer and libation; aid flows downward. Motivation is often dual: Athena rouses and restrains Achilles (Iliad 1, 22), but his rage is his own; Zeus weighs the fates of Sarpedon (16) and Hector (22) on the golden scales — fate (moira) binds even Zeus. In the Odyssey, Zeus opens with a theodicy: humans blame the gods for woes they bring on themselves through their own recklessness (1). Athena is Odysseus' advocate and mask-maker; Poseidon the avenging antagonist; Calypso and Circe test the pull of home against divine beds; the slaughtered cattle of Helios (12) prove that divine justice cannot be evaded.

Xenia is the Odyssey's moral architecture, protected by Zeus Xenios. The code obliges the host to shelter, feed, bathe and gift a guest before even asking his name, and the guest to respond with gratitude and restraint:

  • Positive: Telemachus received by Nestor (Odyssey 3 — not a prescribed book; cite Menelaus, Odyssey 4) and the Phaeacians (7–8), where the palace sequence stages ideal hospitality; Eumaeus the swineherd offers perfect xenia to a beggar he does not recognise (14–16); Odysseus repays it with life and reward.
  • Negative: Polyphemus eats his guests and has no assemblies or laws (9) — civilisation defined by its absence; the suitors devour Odysseus' oikos and plot his son's murder (1–2, 13–22); Paris' breach of Menelaus' hospitality begins the Trojan War itself.

Family is the oikos — the household as social, economic and emotional unit. Hector, Andromache and Astyanax (Iliad 6) give the war its human price: Hector prays his son may outshine him, while the baby fears his horsehair crest — the helmet of timē terrifies the family it protects. Priam and Achilles in Book 24 exchange grief across enemy lines through the shared facts of fatherhood and mortality. In the Odyssey, four generations of one oikos reunite — Penelope, Telemachus, Laertes — through tests of memory and knowledge.

Women hold real authority within the oikos but no public role: Penelope's weaving trick and bed test win kleos; Helen is 'dog-faced', beauty and blame inseparable; Clytemnestra is the ever-present foil for female fidelity; Arete's judgement carries weight among the Phaeacians. Slaves are differentiated: the loyal swineherd Eumaeus and cowherd Philoetius are named, trusted and rewarded; the disloyal Melanthius and the twelve maids are executed (22); Briseis' lament for Patroclus (Iliad 19) gives a captive woman interiority. Homer humanises all of these groups while depicting a patriarchal world — the tension A* answers should exploit.

The suitors' compound is the anti-oikos: they consume, they court Penelope by force, they plot Telemachus' murder — the poem defines the household by its violation. Helen's self-blame at Hector's funeral (24 — 'shameful me, that Zeus planted a doomed destiny') gives the war's other side a woman's voice; Arete's authority among the Phaeacians (7) shows a queen whose judgement the king obeys; Nausicaa (6 — not a prescribed book) stages guest-friendship from the girl's side. Slaves' loyalty is tested to the end: Eumaeus and Philoetius are rewarded (wives, property and a place in the restored oikos, 24); Melanthius and the twelve maids are executed (22) — the oikos restored by violence as well as reunion.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: 'In the Iliad, Achilles' true heroism is shown not in battle but in Book 24.' How far do you agree with this view? Support your answer with detailed reference to the text. [30]
Mark-scheme logic: AO1 evidence required: Iliad 1 (Briseis taken; Athena restrains the sword); Book 9 (embassy; the two fates; refusal of Agamemnon's compensation); Books 20–22 (aristeia; killing of Lycaon the suppliant, 21; slaying and abuse of Hector, 22); Book 24 (Priam's supplication, the kiss of hands, Zeus' two urns, shared weeping, ransom and truce). AO2 argument must weigh both halves of the claim. For battle-heroism: the code of timē and kleos makes aristeia the measure of the hero — Achilles' fighting is what his timē was stolen for; his wrath (mēnis) only becomes heroic once redirected; his pity for Lycaon in 21 is refused, and the corpse-abuse of 22 is excessive even for the code. For Book 24: the poem deliberately transfigures him — he returns Hector's body, weeps with his enemy over mortality, and performs the pity the code had suppressed; the 'two urns' speech universalises suffering; Priam's supplication is a type-scene inverted. Scholars to deploy: Weil (the poem of force — Book 24 as the moment force is overcome by shared humanity); Griffin (pathos and the humanisation of Achilles); Silk (the Iliad as a poem of reversal). Top-band judgement: agree substantially but avoid a binary — Book 24 only works because Books 20–22 have shown heroism at its worst; the poem needs both faces of Achilles, and the 30-mark levels reward a sustained evaluation of that tension rather than a one-sided case.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

'The gods in the Iliad are little more than immortal humans with superpowers.' How far do you agree? [30]
Answer: For: anthropomorphism is total — they wound (Aphrodite, Iliad 5), bleed ichor, quarrel like a dysfunctional family, and take sides for personal reasons (Hera and Athena against Aphrodite and Ares); they lust and deceive (Zeus and Hera, Iliad 14). Against: their scope of power is categorically different — Zeus weighs the fates of Sarpedon (16) and Hector (22), suggesting moira binds even the chief god; they act invisibly at will, and their interventions shape a war mortals cannot control; the poem's dual motivation (Athena stops Achilles' sword in Book 1) is ambiguous — did he need divine restraint, or was his own self-control emerging? Scholars: Griffin (the gods as magnified humans who nonetheless give the epic its cosmic scale); Edwards (the gods as narrative devices that stretch time and create pathos). Judgement: the phrase captures their psychology but not their function — Homer uses the gods simultaneously as comic relief, cosmic order and dramatic machinery; the tension between divine play and mortal seriousness is the point, so a top answer refuses to settle the question one way.
'Xenia in the Odyssey divides the world into the civilised and the savage.' How far do you agree? [30]
Answer: For: the negative tests define savagery by failing the code — Polyphemus eats his guests and lacks assemblies and laws (Odyssey 9), making xenia the boundary of civilisation itself; the suitors devour Odysseus' oikos and plot parricide (13–22), so the massacre of Book 22 is presented as justice, not vengeance; Paris' breach of Menelaus' hospitality caused the war, showing the code's cosmic importance. Against: xenia also grades the civilised — Nestor and Menelaus (Odyssey 4) are generous but garrulous; the Phaeacians (7–8) are ideal hosts yet their island is a pre-Homeric fantasy; Eumaeus (14–16), a slave, is the poem's best host, so xenia tracks virtue, not status. The code also has tensions: hosts give before asking names, yet Odysseus' lies are part of good xenia; Circe and Calypso breach and then perfect hospitality. Scholars: Griffin (the Odyssey as a poem about a society and its values); Jones (xenia as the ethical spine of the poem). Judgement: xenia is a spectrum rather than a binary — the poem uses failed, adequate and perfect hospitality to measure every society it depicts, and a top answer ranks the suitors' compound, the Cyclopes' cave and Alcinous' palace against the code.
'Penelope is Odysseus' equal in mētis.' How far do you agree? [30]
Answer: For: the weaving-and-unpicking trick (Odyssey 2, 19) delays the suitors for three years — a stratagem of pure cunning; her test of the bed (23) is the poem's final recognition scene, forcing Odysseus to reveal knowledge only the true husband has; her staging of the bow contest (21–22) may be read as a deliberate trap for the man she hopes will return. Against: the poem genders cunning — Odysseus' mētis operates in war, travel and disguise, Penelope's within the oikos; she is deceived by the Beggar's story (19) where Telemachus is not; her kleos is won through fidelity (24), a virtue by the poem's standards, whereas Odysseus' is won through action. Scholars: Griffin (Penelope as the moral centre whose fidelity mirrors and measures her husband's); Jones (a commentary-level case for the bed test as the proof of equality). Judgement: equal in quality, unequal in scope — Homer grants Penelope matching intelligence but confines its theatre to the household; a top answer uses the bed test as the proof that the poem itself judges them equals.
'The oral tradition explains everything important about the composition of the Homeric epics.' How far do you agree? [30]
Answer: For: Parry and Lord showed the diction is formulaic — epithets, repeated lines and type-scenes fit the hexameter and enable composition-in-performance; the consistency of the story-pattern (Owen, The Story of the Iliad) is explicable as a tradition, not an author; transmission via rhapsodes and the (traditionally) Peisistratean recension explains how the text stabilised. Against: oral-formulaic technique does not explain the design — the ring structure of the Iliad, the symmetry of the Odyssey (Telemachy, Apologoi, revenge), the sustained irony and the psychological development of Achilles (Griffin, Edwards) look like the work of a monumental poet, even one working within the tradition; Nicholson argues the poems preserve Bronze Age memory (the boar-tusk helmet, Mycenaean geography) that no single bard could invent; the one-poet-or-many debate remains genuinely open. Scholars: Parry and Lord (formulae); Nicholson (The Mighty Dead); Fowler (Cambridge Companion to Homer — composition and transmission). Judgement: the tradition explains the diction and the performance context, but not the artistry; the best answers treat Homer as a monumental poet working within an oral tradition, and note that OCR accepts any reasoned position so long as evidence supports it.
'Hector is the true hero of the Iliad.' To what extent do you agree? [30]
Answer: For: Hector fights for family and city, not personal kleos — his farewell to Andromache and Astyanax (Iliad 6) fuses heroic duty and domestic love; he accepts his fate rather than raging against it; in Book 22 he refuses his parents' pleas to shelter inside the walls, heroism as responsibility; his death and the ransom of 24 centre the poem's pity. Against: the poem's title character is Achilles, whose mēnis frames the whole plot; Hector runs from Achilles three times round the walls (22), a flight the poem does not hide; his heroism is reactive and dependent on the code he defends; Achilles alone questions the code and transcends it (9, 24). Scholars: Weil (force degrades both sides — Hector is its most complete victim); Silk (the Iliad's structure of reversal makes Hector's fall the pivot); Griffin (Hector as the poem's humane hero). Judgement: 'true hero' depends on which heroism you value — physical kleos (Achilles) or the defence of oikos and polis (Hector); a top answer shows the poem deliberately pairs them so each definition is tested by the other, and that Book 24 forces the reader to honour both.
