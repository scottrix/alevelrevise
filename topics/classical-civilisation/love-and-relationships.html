Love and Relationships

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level OCR

A* OCR H408/32 revision for Love and Relationships: Sappho and Ovid's Ars Amatoria 3, Plato and Seneca on love and desire — Greek and Roman views of marriage, gender and sexuality, with 30-mark model answers.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Analyse Greek and Roman thinking on the ideal qualities and relative status of men and women — domestically, civically and legally (H408/32; AO1/AO2)
  • Evaluate Plato's ideas on love and desire — definitions, physical symptoms, the love/desire distinction, control and resistance, homoerotic relationships — in his contemporary context (AO2)
  • Evaluate Seneca's ideas on love and desire and the influence of Stoicism on them (AO2)
  • Analyse the prescribed Sappho fragments — purpose, devices, mythology, humour, the issues of fragmentary material (AO1/AO2)
  • Analyse Ovid's Ars Amatoria Book 3 — didactic style, literary devices, themes and humour (AO2)
  • Compare the views in Sappho and Ovid with Plato and Seneca and with societal ideas about men, women and marriage (AO2)
  • Reach substantiated judgements on ideal vs reality, and on 'good' and 'bad' ways to love or be loved (AO2)

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

epithumia: Desire or appetite — the bodily longing that Plato and Seneca distinguish from love (which they give a rational or spiritual definition).
sophrosyne: Self-control, moderation — the virtue Greek thought demanded of both sexes, but especially policed in women.
paiderastia: Institutionalised Greek homoerotic courtship between an older lover (erastes) and a younger beloved (eromenos), with norms of gift, shame and restraint.
kyrios: The male guardian (father, husband, nearest male kin) with legal authority over an Athenian woman and her property.
patria potestas: The Roman father's legal power over his descendants — life and death authority that marriage (cum manu or sine manu) modified for the wife.
pudicitia: Roman sexual modesty and chastity — the matrona's defining virtue, policed by law and iconography (the veil; the Head of Livia's imagery).
lex Iulia de adulteriis: Augustus' adultery law (18 BC): criminalised adultery, gave fathers and husbands the duty to prosecute — the legal double standard against female sexuality.
monodic lyric: Sappho's genre: songs for solo performance with lyre/aulos accompaniment, composed for her circle of female companions — distinct from public choral poetry.
praeceptor amoris: 'Teacher of love' — Ovid's didactic persona in the Ars Amatoria, parodying the instructional manuals of farming and rhetoric.
apatheia: The Stoic ideal of freedom from passion (pathos) — passions as false judgements to be corrected by reason; the frame for Seneca's treatment of love.

📌 Key Points

H408/32 is 1 hour 45 minutes, 75 marks, 30% of the A Level; content is split equally between classical thought (ethics and values relating to love, desire and relationships) and literature (Sappho and Ovid).
Prescribed literary sources: Sappho, the extant works listed in the OCR Literary Sources for Love and Relationships booklet (e.g. frr. 1, 16, 31, 44, 58, 94, 96) and Ovid, Ars Amatoria Book 3.
Plato and Seneca are the key thinkers: their ideas (from the Symposium and Seneca's Stoic writings) are assessed as ideas — including a 10-mark idea question — not as prescribed sources for commentary.
Section A is compulsory (short answer + commentary + shorter essay); Section B is a choice of one from two 30-mark essays, with secondary sources and academic views credited.

Men, women and marriage: Greek and Roman thought

Greek ideals. The ideal man is self-controlled, brave, publicly useful (andreia and sophrosyne); the ideal woman is chaste, obedient, silent indoors — Pericles' funeral oration gives the classic statement: the best woman's reputation is not to be talked of among men, good or bad (Thucydides 2.45). Status is legally and civically unequal: an Athenian woman needs a kyrios (guardian), cannot vote or own property outright, and her citizen status matters only as the conduit of citizen children (Pericles' citizenship law, 451/450 BC); her public outlets are religious (the Thesmophoria, priesthoods). Roman women sit under patria potestas, though marriage sine manu (without hand) left them in their birth family and, by the late Republic, legally freer than Greek counterparts; the matrona's virtue is pudicitia, the ideal the univira (one-man woman) embodies.

Marriage. A good marriage produces legitimate children, joins properties (dowry) and joins families; its joys are companionship and civic continuity. Sexual freedom is double-standard: men in Greece could frequent hetairai and slaves and practise paiderastia; Roman men meretrices; women in both cultures faced enforced monogamy — Augustus' lex Iulia de adulteriis (18 BC) criminalised female adultery and placed prosecution on the family. Adultery is thus theft of another man's property-rights in the Greek frame, and a crime against the state's moral order in the Roman one. Ideal vs reality is the recurring evaluation: funerary monuments and laws praise the silent matron, but literature records real voices — Medea's speech on the desperate bargain of marriage (Euripides, citable as context), Sappho's women who feel, Ovid's women who manipulate. The thought half's question — what makes a 'good' or 'bad' way to love — runs through all four topics.

Greek women's religious outlets were real power: the Thesmophoria (the women's festival of Demeter) was organised and run entirely by citizen women; the priestess of Athena Polias (from the Eteoboutad family) held one of the city's most important posts. Xenophon's Oeconomicus gives the Greek training of the wife: Ischomachus marries a girl of fifteen and trains her to run the household — the ideal in a manual, citable as context. Roman women's legal position improved across the Republic: marriage sine manu left them in their birth family's potestas; they could appear in court and manage property; the matrona's veil (the flammeum) and pudicitia remained the ideal, and Augustus' laws (the ius trium liberorum privileges; the adultery courts) policed the ideal by law.

Plato on love and desire (Symposium)

Definitions of love — the Symposium stages a competition of speeches: Eryximachus (love as harmony balancing opposites, medical and cosmic); Pausanias (two Aphrodites — Common Love, bodily and venal, vs Heavenly Love, the noble love of character that grounds respectable paiderastia); Aristophanes (the myth of split beings: humans were doubles, cut apart by Zeus, and love is the desire and pursuit of the whole — the physical symptom is the yearning of halves to melt together); Agathon (love as the youngest, most beautiful and virtuous god); and Socrates, reporting Diotima: love is not a god but a great daimon, intermediate between mortal and immortal — the desire for the perpetual possession of the good, expressed as reproduction in beauty, and ascending by the ladder of love from one beautiful body, to all bodies, to souls, to practices and laws, to knowledge, to Beauty itself.

Love vs desire. Desire (epithumia) is appetite — for food, drink, sex; love, on Diotima's account, is desire rationally redirected to the good and the everlasting. The distinction grounds the control and resistance of desire: desire should be controlled because it misjudges the good (aiming at the mortal, not the everlasting) and resisted through education, shame and philosophy — the beloved in respectable paiderastia restrains the lover with shame (aidos) and law (nomos), and Socrates resists even Alcibiades' attempt to seduce him (the final speech, where the roles reverse). Homoerotic relationships are thus both the norm being theorised (erastes/eromenos courtship, age asymmetry, gifts) and the object of Plato's refinement: Heavenly Love ennobles them, Common Love disgraces them.

Context and reception. Plato's contemporary context shaped the ideas: classical Athens' symposium culture, its elite paiderastia, the aftermath of the Sicilian expedition (Agathon's victory dinner, c.416 BC). His contemporaries would have heard much that was familiar (Pausanias' defence of paiderastia) and much that was new (Diotima's ladder; love as philosophy rather than sex); the women's absence — the flute-girl sent away — is itself evidence of whose relationships the theory is about. A* answers weigh the ladder against the myth: Aristophanes' romantic picture has proved more famous than Diotima's rigorous one — which reception itself is examinable.

The Symposium's frame matters for reception: Apollodorus retells the party from a friend's memory; the speeches are staged at Agathon's victory dinner (c.416 BC), with the flute-girl sent away so the men can talk — the women's absence is itself evidence of whose relationships the theory theorises. Socrates' resistance of Alcibiades is the final speech's climax: Alcibiades' drunken entrance (beauty's prize arrives to indict beauty's teacher), his attempt to seduce Socrates (under the cloak), and Socrates' refusal — desire resisted by reason, the theory performed in a character. Note the ambiguity: Socrates' resistance is not pure — he praises and flirts; the exam expects you to weigh the performance against the theory.

Seneca on love and desire

Stoicism frames everything. The Stoics define passion (pathos) as a false judgement — an impulse that runs ahead of reason; the four categories are distress, fear, appetite and pleasure. Love, for Seneca, belongs to the appetites unless disciplined into rational affection: the Stoic ideal is apatheia, freedom from passion, with virtue as the only good and the body, beauty and pleasure as indifferents (adiaphora). Definitions: love is the desire for union with what appears good — but since beauty is not good (it is indifferent), erotic love is a misjudgement; friendship, by contrast, is rational affection between virtuous partners. Physical symptoms of desire — pallor, trembling, sleeplessness — are evidence of the mind's assent to a false appearance; the first involuntary movements (the blush, the startle) are not yet passions: assent makes them so, and reason can stop the assent. Control and resistance: Stoic technique — premeditation of future ills, habituation away from banquets and luxury, the refusal of the first assent — makes desire resistible; the answer to 'why should desire be controlled' is that passion enslaves the sage's sovereignty of reason.

Homoerotic relationships meet the Roman frame: male-male sex was tolerated only in the active role; the passive adult male (the cinaedus of Roman invective) was disgraced — Seneca's moralising reflects and sharpens this; the Stoic critique cuts across both: passion of any direction enslaves. Context and reception. Seneca's contemporary context is Neronian Rome — imperial luxury, the court's excesses, and his own ambiguous position (a Stoic moralist of vast wealth); his contemporaries received the ideas as elite ethics against the court's example, and the irony (Nero as the pupil) is examinable. Good and bad conduct: marriage is a rational partnership (contrast Ovid's game); desire should be governed by judgement, not extinguished by force; the ideal is the sage who feels the first movement and refuses the second. A* answers evaluate the Stoic programme against the reality of Neronian Rome and against Plato: Seneca offers control as therapy; Diotima offers redirection as philosophy.

Seneca's Stoic sources matter: the passion taxonomy (distress, fear, appetite, pleasure) comes from the Stoic school; his De Ira is the model treatment — anger as a passion governed by the same techniques (delay, avoid the stimuli, judge the appearance before assent); his Letters apply the method to wealth, grief and love. The context sharpens the irony: Neronian Rome's court (Nero's excesses, the pupil-teacher relationship) makes the therapy political — the sage's self-control against the emperor's license; and Seneca's own vast wealth is the examinable contradiction.

Sappho and Ovid: love in poetry

Sappho (Lesbos, late 7th–early 6th century BC) is the component's female voice: monodic lyric composed for performance by and for her circle of female companions. The prescribed fragments show the range: fr. 1, the Hymn to Aphrodite — a prayer in ring composition, the goddess's amused epiphany ('once again?'), and the request 'whom shall I persuade to lead you back?'; fr. 16 — 'some say cavalry, others infantry... but I say the most beautiful thing is whatever one loves', proved by Helen's choice of Paris over family; fr. 31 — the triangular scene of desire's physical symptoms: the voice fails, fire runs under the skin, the eyes see nothing, the ears hum, sweat pours, 'I am greener than grass, and little short of dying'; fr. 44 — the wedding of Hector and Andromache in epic style; fr. 58 — old age, via Tithonus, with the claim that love of the sun's light persists; frr. 94 and 96 — parting and the absent companion remembered through sensuous detail. Devices to cite: apostrophe, imagistic compression, myth as exemplum, irony and humour (Aphrodite's teasing; Sappho's self-deprecation). The issues of fragmentary material are examinable: papyrus scraps, torn lines and restored words mean interpretation is provisional — and that fragility is part of the poems' modern fame.

Ovid, Ars Amatoria Book 3 (c.AD 1–2) reverses the didactic frame: Books 1–2 taught men to seduce; Book 3 teaches women — the praeceptor amoris addresses the pupil female. Cite the didactic style: imperatives, catalogues, mock-scholarly digressions (cosmetics, hair, dress in moderation); the venues — the theatre, the porticos, the games as hunting grounds; the tactics — reading and writing elegies, timed arrivals, calculated coldness; the mythological examplars (Myrrha; Pasiphae; Atalanta run down by Hippomenes' apples); and the humour — irony, self-mockery, bathos (the teacher who admits his own failures). Themes: love as art and game, not fate; manipulation as skill; gender roles reversed inside a patriarchal frame — the pupil is empowered to hunt, but the quarry is still male approval. The Roman context is Augustan morality legislation (the same laws Imperial Image studies) — Book 3's tolerance of affairs was, tradition says, part of the carmen et error behind Ovid's exile (AD 8).

Comparisons are the Section B core: Sappho's earnest, imagistic desire vs Ovid's playful technique; Plato and Seneca's control vs Ovid's indulgence; Diotima's objective Beauty vs fr. 16's subjective 'whatever one loves'; Greek paiderastia vs Roman marriage economics; and the societal frame — ideal vs reality — against which every text measures 'good' and 'bad' love.

Sappho's devices reward close reading: fr. 1's ring composition (prayer — epiphany — request — closing invocation) and Aphrodite's teasing tone; fr. 16's exemplum structure (the general list, Helen's choice, the personal application to Anactoria); fr. 31's escalating symptoms (sight, sound, fire, sweat) broken off in the fragment's gap; fr. 58's Tithonus myth (the dawn-goddess's lover aged into immortality without youth) turned to Sappho's own old age. Ovid's Book 3 rewards the same: the didactic structure (cosmetics, dress, deportment, then tactics), the exemplars (Myrrha and Pasiphae as desire's warnings; Atalanta run down by Hippomenes' apples — the praeceptor's approval of the trick), and the humour that undercuts the teacher (his own failures admitted, the irony of a man teaching women to outwit men).

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: 'Ovid's Ars Amatoria Book 3 subverts, rather than supports, Roman gender norms.' How far do you agree with this view? Support your answer with detailed reference to the text. [30]
Mark-scheme logic: AO1 evidence required: the reversal of the didactic frame (Books 1–2 for men; Book 3 addresses women); the praeceptor amoris persona and its parody of instructional literature; the venues (theatre, porticos, games as hunting grounds); the beauty and dress advice in moderation; the tactics (elegies read and written, timed arrivals, calculated coldness); the mythological examplars (Myrrha, Pasiphae, Atalanta and Hippomenes); the humour (irony, self-mockery, bathos); the Augustan moral context (lex Iulia de maritandis ordinibus and de adulteriis; the tradition of Ovid's exile — carmen et error, AD 8). AO2 must weigh both halves. Subversion: the teacher is female-facing — women are taught agency, tactics and authorship (writing their own elegies); the hunting metaphor reverses the pursuit; the male strategies of Books 1–2 are exposed and answered; the parody mocks the male didactic tradition itself. Support: the beauty standards serve male consumption; the pupil is trained to win and keep a lover, not to live outside the affair-market; marriage is mocked, not reformed — the frame of female availability and male judgement stays intact; the examplars are women undone by desire (Myrrha, Pasiphae), not by society. Scholars: Hardie (Cambridge Companion to Ovid — the Ars as parodic didactic); Skinner (Sexuality in Greek and Roman Culture — the gender frame). Top-band judgement: Book 3 subverts the didactic form while supporting the gender order — women gain tactics, not freedom; the subversion is real but bounded, and the exile shows the state read it as subversive of morals rather than of gender; the top answer separates the two registers and holds both.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

'In the Symposium, Plato makes love rational rather than passionate.' How far do you agree? [30]
Answer: For: Diotima's definition — love as the desire for perpetual possession of the good, expressed as reproduction in beauty; the ladder of love from bodies to Beauty itself; love as a great daimon, intermediate between mortal and immortal — philosophy, not feeling; the control of epithumia through education and shame. Against: the other speeches are passionate — Aristophanes' halves yearning to melt together is romantic; Agathon's youngest god celebrates beauty; Alcibiades' attempted seduction of Socrates is desire undisciplined; even Socrates' account leaves desire as the motor — reason redirects it rather than replacing it. Scholars: Goldhill (Love, Sex and Tragedy — the Symposium's tensions); the ancient context (symposium culture, 416 BC). Judgement: reason subsumes desire — the Symposium is a redefinition, not an elimination: passion is the starting-point, the ladder is the destination; a top answer weighs Diotima against Aristophanes and shows Plato's own dialogue dramatising the tension.
'Sappho fragment 31 shows desire as a form of dying.' How far do you agree? [30]
Answer: For: the physical symptoms escalate to death — the voice fails, the tongue breaks, fire runs under the skin, the eyes see nothing, the ears hum, sweat pours, 'I am greener than grass, and little short of dying'; the persona is both observer and sufferer; the closing clauses on boldness and the near-dead break off in the fragment's gap — the form itself enacts collapse. Against: the symptoms are classic desire's physiology, not death — the fragment's hyperbole is lyric convention; the frame is triangular (the persona watches the beloved and the man beside her) so jealousy, not love, drives the collapse; the poem's structure (sight, sound, sweat) is clinical — an anatomy of desire. The fragmentary state matters: the ending is restored and debated, so the death reading is provisional. Scholars: Freeman (Searching for Sappho — the persona's position); Skinner (the Greek frame of female desire). Judgement: 'little short of dying' is the poem's own claim — hyperbole that names desire's extremity; a top answer reads the symptoms' escalation, notes the fragment's gaps, and distinguishes the lyric convention from the experience.
'Seneca's Stoicism leaves no room for love.' How far do you agree? [30]
Answer: For: passion (pathos) is a false judgement — appetite for the indifferent (beauty, pleasure) enslaves the reason; the ideal is apatheia; erotic love is a misjudgement since beauty is not good; the techniques (premeditation, habituation, refusal of the first assent) aim at freedom from passion, not its refinement. Against: Seneca distinguishes rational affection (friendship) from passion — love disciplined, not destroyed; the sage feels the first movement (the blush) and refuses the second — sensation is human, assent is the fault; marriage as a rational partnership has room for affection; the Roman context (Neronian luxury) makes the therapy practical, not monastic. Scholars: Goldhill (the ethics of love across Greek and Roman thought); Skinner (Stoicism in its Roman frame). Judgement: Stoicism leaves room for rational love but not for passion — the distinction (affection vs appetite) is the answer's hinge; a top answer weighs the therapy against the ideal and notes that Seneca's own wealth makes the programme's irony examinable.
'Roman marriage was an economic contract, not a love affair.' How far do you agree? [30]
Answer: For contract: dowry and property define the union; marriage sine manu left the wife in her birth family — legal ties outranked domestic ones; the lex Iulia de adulteriis policed the contract criminally; funerary monuments praise the univira — an economic-moral ideal; patria potestas framed the wife as under male authority. For love: the monuments also record affection (spouses' epitaphs praising companionship); Ovid's Book 3 presumes love affairs inside and outside marriage — the market is emotional as well as economic; Sappho's Greek frame (comparably) values desire against arrangement; Seneca's rational partnership idealises companionship. Scholars: Lefkowitz and Fant (Women's Life in Greece and Rome — the sourcebook's epitaphs and laws); Skinner (Sexuality in Greek and Roman Culture). Judgement: both registers coexist — the contract is the legal form, the affection the human content; a top answer uses the epitaphs against the laws and argues the ideology (univira, pudicitia) served the economics.
'Sappho's voice is more valuable because she is a woman writing about women.' How far do you agree? [30]
Answer: For value: she is one of very few female voices from the ancient world — the interiority of female desire (fr. 31; fr. 94's parting) has no male equivalent; the circle of female companions is her audience and subject; fr. 16's subjective claim ('the most beautiful is whatever one loves') against the male heroic list (cavalry, infantry) reframes value itself; monodic lyric for female performance is a genre the male tradition does not record. Against: 'value' is a modern critical judgement — the ancient canon ranked her with the male lyricists (the tenth Muse by tradition); her fragments are preserved (and torn) by male editorial history; the component's comparisons (Plato, Seneca, Ovid) are male-authored frames through which her work is examined; her context (an aristocratic circle on Lesbos) is elite, not representative of women. Scholars: Freeman (Searching for Sappho — the biographical frame); Skinner (the female voice in its Greek context). Judgement: her gender makes her voice rare and her subject-matter unique — but 'more valuable' depends on the criterion: historically unique, literarily excellent, representationally unmatched; a top answer grades the claim by criterion rather than accepting or rejecting it wholesale.
