Greek Religion

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level OCR

A* OCR H408/31 revision for Greek Religion: Olympian gods and reciprocity, mystery cults and oracles, sacrifice and priests, Panathenaia and Delphi/Olympia, Xenophanes and Socrates — with full 30-mark model answers.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain the anthropomorphic nature of the Olympian gods, the reciprocal relationship with mortals, and the significance of Homer and Hesiod (H408/31; AO1/AO2)
  • Analyse personal experience of the divine — mystery cults, the healing cult of Asclepius, the oracle at Dodona — against communal practice (AO2)
  • Assess levels of religious participation (household, deme, polis, Panhellenic), religious authority and impiety, and the significance of the Panathenaia for Athens (AO2)
  • Explain the purpose, process and significance of blood sacrifice, libations and votive offerings, and the role of priests and priestesses (AO2)
  • Analyse the layout, significance and functions of the Athenian Acropolis, Delphi and Olympia as prescribed sites (AO1/AO2)
  • Evaluate the rise of philosophical thinking — Xenophanes' critique and Socrates' impiety — and its impact on Greek religion (AO2)
  • Deploy secondary scholars (e.g. Parker, Price, Brut Zaidman) and the prescribed vases to support judgements (AO2)

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

reciprocity (do ut des): 'I give so that you give' — the contractual logic of Greek cult: honour (sacrifice, libation, votives) flows upward; aid, protection and fertility flow downward.
anthropomorphism: The gods' human-like form and psychology — body, family, jealousy, deceit — as established for the Greeks by Homer and Hesiod (cf. Herodotus 2.53).
hero cult: Worship of an exceptional dead mortal at or near their tomb, with chthonic-style offerings; heroes protected communities and were prayed to for aid (e.g. Theseus, Brasidas at Amphipolis).
mystery cult: A cult of initiation (e.g. the Eleusinian Mysteries) promising benefits through secret rites known only to initiates; participation was a matter of personal choice.
incubation: Ritual sleep in a god's sanctuary — especially that of Asclepius at Epidaurus — during which the god healed the sleeper or prescribed a cure in a dream.
asebeia: Impiety — a prosecutable offence at Athens (e.g. Socrates, 399 BC); religious authority lay with the polis, its laws and its customs rather than a scripture or church.
hecatomb: A sacrifice of (originally) a hundred cattle; the great public burnt offerings of state festivals, e.g. on the ash altar of Zeus at Olympia.
libation: The pouring of wine, water or oil to the gods — at symposia, oaths and sacrifices, in both state and private contexts; the regular renewing of reciprocity.
votive offering: A dedication (statue, relief, armour, anatomical model) made to a god in thanks or hope — from private anatomical votives at the Asclepieion to state treasures at Delphi and Olympia.
Panathenaia: Athena's state festival: annual (Lesser) and four-yearly Greater (with games from 566 BC), culminating in the procession to the Acropolis and the hecatomb; the peplos for Athena was woven and presented.

📌 Key Points

H408/31 is 1 hour 45 minutes, 75 marks, 30% of the A Level; the content is split equally between classical thought (religion and belief) and visual/material culture.
There are no literary set texts: prescribed sources are the vases (e.g. the BF Panathenaic amphora BM 1856,1001.1; the Nausicaa Painter sacrifice amphora; the Kodros Painter Pythia kylix; the cult-of-Eleusis plaque; the anatomical votive) and the sites — the Athenian Acropolis (Parthenon, Erechtheion), Delphi (Temple of Apollo, Theatre, Sacred Way, stadium) and Olympia (Temple of Zeus, Pheidias' statue, ash altar, treasuries, Echo Stoa, stadium).
Section A is compulsory (short answer + commentary on one prescribed stimulus + shorter essay); Section B is a choice of one from two 30-mark essays with secondary sources credited.
Key thinkers for the thought half: Xenophanes (critique of anthropomorphic gods) and Socrates (impiety, 399 BC) — ideas assessed as ideas, not as sources.

The Olympian gods and the reciprocal relationship

The Olympians are anthropomorphic: they have bodies, genealogies (Hesiod's Theogony), moods, jealousies and limitations — but not omnipotence or omniscience. Their scope of power is bounded: Zeus weighs fates on his scales (Iliad 16, 22) rather than simply commanding outcomes; gods cannot reverse what is fated. The relationship with mortals is reciprocal: mortals give honour — sacrifice, libation, votives, festivals — and gods give protection, fertility, victory and healing. Greek cult is contractual (do ut des), not creedal: there is no revealed truth to believe, only powers to honour correctly.

Homer and Hesiod were foundational: Herodotus (2.53) credits them with teaching the Greeks the gods' genealogies, epithets and forms barely four centuries before his own writing — the literary sources of the gods' characters. Common epithets map the gods' different roles and contexts: Zeus Agoraios (of the agora), Zeus Phratrios (of the brotherhoods), Zeus Philios (of friendship), Zeus Herkeios (of the courtyard) — one god with many functions; the exam asks how far these were thought of as separate, distinct entities (they were not: one Zeus, many cult titles — but each with its own cult, icon and priest).

Hero cults are the other half of Greek worship: exceptional dead mortals — founders, leaders, the glorious dead — received offerings at their tombs and were prayed to for aid, in a chthonic (underworld) register contrasting with Olympian sacrifice. Examples: Theseus' bones translated to Athens by Cimon (c.470s BC); Brasidas buried and honoured as founder-hero at Amphipolis. Worship ranged across Panhellenic (Zeus at Olympia, Apollo at Delphi), localised (Athena Polias at Athens, city-specific cults) and personal (household gods, private vows) — the spectrum the whole component measures.

The Olympian roster is fixed by tradition: Zeus, Hera, Poseidon, Demeter, Athena, Apollo, Artemis, Ares, Aphrodite, Hephaestus, Hermes and (in the Athenian list) Hestia or Dionysus — twelve names worshipped Panhellenically, but each with local cults, iconographies and festivals. Hesiod's Theogony supplies the genealogy that orders them; his Works and Days supplies their justice — Zeus who watches all and punishes wrongdoing. Hero cult fills the space between gods and the dead: exceptional mortals received tombs, offerings and prayers for aid — Herakles was worshipped as both hero and god across Greece; city founders (Theseus at Athens) and war dead (Brasidas at Amphipolis) drew cult; and the chthonic register (offerings poured into the earth, at dusk, to the dead below) contrasts with Olympian sacrifice (burnt on the altar, by day, to the gods above).

Personal experience of the divine: mysteries, healing, oracles

Mystery cults inverted the communal norm: participation was a matter of personal choice and initiation, open across the usual barriers. The Eleusinian Mysteries of Demeter and Persephone (whose Homeric Hymn explains the myth) promised initiates better prospects — for the harvest and for death; the ritual was secret, with two grades (mystes, then epoptes at a later Great Eleusinia). The prescribed red-figure plaque of the cult of Eleusis (c.400–300 BC) shows Demeter and Persephone with initiates — the evidence base. Mystery cults offered what the polis could not: individual religious experience, hope and a personal relationship with the divine.

The healing cult of Asclepius blurs the god/hero boundary: a mortal healer deified, worshipped as both god and hero, with incubation (ritual sleep) and miracle cures recorded in the iamata inscriptions at Epidaurus. The prescribed marble anatomical votive from the shrine of Asclepius (British Museum) shows the body parts dedicated in thanks — private piety made material.

The oracle at Dodona (Zeus and Dione, in Epirus) served private individuals: questions scratched on lead tablets — will the child be mine? whom should I marry? should I sail? — asked for personal help and advice, contrasted with Delphi's state business. The prescribed Kodros Painter kylix (c.440–430 BC) depicts a consultation of the Pythia at Delphi — the oracle in use, not just in myth. Together these three show the personal face of Greek religion that the polis-centered festivals hide; A* answers use them to complicate any claim that religion was purely civic.

The Eleusinian Mysteries rest on the myth of the Homeric Hymn to Demeter: Persephone abducted by Hades, Demeter's grief and withdrawal, the famine that forces Zeus' settlement, and the gift of the Mysteries to Eleusis. The officials are hereditary priestly families: the hierophant (revealer of the sacred things) and the dadouchos (torch-bearer), with the kykeon drink and the two grades of initiation. Asclepius' cult spread from Epidaurus across the Greek world (a temple in Piraeus by 420 BC); his characterisation as both god and hero — a deified mortal healer — and the iamata inscriptions (patients' cures recorded for public display) make the healing cult the best-documented personal religion. At Dodona, the oak's rustling and the bronze cauldrons' humming were read as Zeus' voice; the lead tablets record questions on marriage, children, travel and property — the private questions the polis' festivals never ask.

Religion and society: participation, authority, politics and the Panathenaia

Levels of participation: household (Zeus Ktesios, Hestia, the herm at the door, ancestors), deme (local festivals — the Rural Dionysia, Thesmophoria for women), polis (state sacrifices and festivals, funded by deme and polis) and Panhellenic (Olympia, Delphi, Isthmia, Nemea). Religious authority lay with the polis, its laws and customs — there was no church, no scripture, no dogma; asebeia was a legal offence (Socrates 399 BC; the Mysteries scandals of 415 BC). Politics and religion were intertwined: assemblies consulted oracles before wars (the Sicilian expedition's omens); Sparta's kings needed Delphic approval; sacred wars were fought over Delphi — political decisions were sometimes made on the basis of divine intervention.

The Panathenaia is the component's set festival. Structure: the Lesser (annual) and Greater (every four years, penteteric) Panathenaia; contests in athletics, music and (from the 6th century BC) the apobates race, with prizes of Panathenaic amphorae filled with sacred olive oil (the prescribed black-figure Panathenaic amphora, 333–332 BC, British Museum 1856,1001.1, shows the armed Athena between columns and the prize event); the procession (pompe) from the city gate up the Panathenaic Way to the Acropolis, with the hecatomb, the girls (kanephoroi, arrephoroi, ergastinai) who carried baskets and wove the peplos, and the presentation of the robe to Athena Polias. Significance for Athens: Panhellenic spectacle (athletes and visitors from across the Greek world), civic unity (the procession represented all Athenians — the Parthenon frieze depicts it as an eternal procession), and political power (Peisistratus used it; the fifth-century BC Acropolis made it the image of the polis).

The impiety cases define the limits of authority: Andocides was prosecuted over the Mysteries scandal of 415 BC (the herms' mutilation and the profanation of the rites); Socrates was tried and executed in 399 BC. Politics and religion intertwined in the other direction too: the Athenian assembly consulted oracles before the Sicilian expedition, Nicias' lunar eclipse delayed the retreat, and Sparta's kings needed Delphic approval for their wars — political decisions were sometimes made on the basis of divine oracular intervention. The Panathenaia's programme also included the ship-cart — the peplos of Athena hoisted like a sail and processed to the Acropolis — and the musical contests added under Pericles; the festival's representation of all Athenians (all tribes, ages and sexes in the procession) is monumentalised in the Parthenon frieze.

Places of worship, ritual, priests and philosophy

The prescribed sites are three sanctuaries with different functions. The Athenian Acropolis (Parthenon, Erechtheion, Propylaia) is the polis' civic and religious heart: Athena Polias' cult statue, the city's treasury, the image of Athens to the world. Delphi is the Panhellenic oracle: the Temple of Apollo with its adyton and the Pythia, consulted in a fixed process (purification, fee, lot-order, state business first); the Sacred Way lined with state treasuries and votives (the Siphnian Treasury); the theatre and stadium (Pythian games) — prophecy embedded in festival. Olympia is the Panhellenic games: the Temple of Zeus with Pheidias' chryselephantine statue, the ash altar of Zeus (the hecatombs' ash mound), treasuries, the Echo Stoa and stadium — competition between poleis under a sacred truce (ekecheiria), Panhellenic identity in action.

Ritual and priests. Blood sacrifice (thusia) is the central rite — purpose: honour, thanksgiving, communion, divination; process: victim selected and processed, water and barley scattered, the knife concealed, the throat cut, the gods' portion (bones and fat) burned, the meat eaten as a communal feast; significance: the whole social order rehearsed. Libations (wine, water, oil) renewed reciprocity daily, at oaths and symposia; votive offerings — from anatomical models at the Asclepieion to state treasuries — made gratitude permanent. Priests and priestesses were part-time ritual experts, not a preaching class: the priest conducted the rite, often as a hereditary or elected honour; aristocratic families monopolised key posts (the Eteoboutads held the priestess of Athena Polias and the priest of Poseidon-Erechtheus; the Eumolpids and Kerykes ran the Mysteries); women held prominent posts (the Pythia; Athena Polias; the Thesmophoria's priestesses) — religion was the one public arena open to women.

Religion and philosophy. From the 6th century BC, philosophical thinking challenged the traditional gods: Xenophanes mocked anthropomorphism — Ethiopians draw their gods snub-nosed, horses would draw horse-gods; there is one god, not like mortals in body or thought. Socrates spoke of a personal divine sign (daimonion), rejected the Homeric gods' immoral behaviour, and was tried and executed for impiety in 399 BC (not believing in the city's gods, introducing new divinities, corrupting the young) — ideas the exam treats as genuinely radical in context, though note the counter-view: philosophers inherited the gods' vocabulary (Xenophanes still posits a god; Socrates still swears by Zeus), temples kept being built, and philosophy coexisted with cult rather than destroying it. The tension — a religion of ritual and reciprocity meeting a philosophy of reason — is the thought half's core evaluation.

Delphi's consultation process was fixed and public: purification in the Castalian spring, the pelanos fee, the order by lot with state business first, the Pythia's response from the adyton — and the scholarly debate over how she prophesied (the vapours, the laurel, the verses' ambiguity) is itself examinable. Olympia's games ran under the sacred truce (ekecheiria) with events from the footrace through the combat sports to the chariot races; the ash altar's mound grew with each hecatomb, and Pheidias' Zeus — one of the ancient world's wonders — sat within the temple. On philosophy, the exam's evaluation is the extent to which the ideas were 'truly new': Xenophanes' monotheism still posits a god; Socrates still swears by Zeus and dies obeying the city's gods; Anaxagoras' nous still orders a cosmos — philosophical thinking inherited the gods' vocabulary even as it criticised their stories, and temples kept being built.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: 'Religion in the Greek world was primarily a communal activity.' How far do you agree with this view? Support your answer with detailed reference to the sources you have studied. [30]
Mark-scheme logic: AO1 evidence required: communal — state festivals (the Panathenaia and its procession, hecatomb and contests), polis sacrifice and the communal feast, deme festivals, the Panhellenic sanctuaries (Olympia's ash altar and games; Delphi's state consultation), the Parthenon frieze as the procession in stone; personal — the Eleusinian Mysteries (initiation by personal choice, secrecy, individual hope), the healing cult of Asclepius (incubation, anatomical votives), the oracle at Dodona (private questions on lead tablets), private libations and household cult. AO2 must weigh both halves. For communal: religion's funding (choregoi-style liturgies, deme budgets), its calendar and its politics (assemblies consulting oracles) made it the polis' public business; the Panathenaia represented all Athenians and the Parthenon frieze monumentalises that civic ideal. Against: the Mysteries were voluntary and open to individuals across status; Dodona served private questions; incubation and anatomical votives were intensely personal; the prescribed vases (the Kodros Painter's Pythia; the anatomical votive) document private experience. Scholars: Parker (Polytheism and Society at Athens — participation across levels, personal choice within communal structures); Price (Religions of the Ancient Greeks — a spectrum, not a binary). Top-band judgement: religion operated on a spectrum from household to Panhellenic, and the communal and personal faces were complementary rather than opposed — the same individual sacrificed with the polis and slept in the Asclepieion; the top answer refuses a binary and grades the evidence across the levels of participation.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

'The Eleusinian Mysteries show that Greek religion was a matter of personal choice rather than state control.' How far do you agree? [30]
Answer: For personal choice: initiation was voluntary and open (Greek-speakers, across status lines); the promise was personal (better harvests, better death); the rites were secret, experienced individually; the two grades (mystes, epoptes) made progression a personal journey. Against state control: Eleusis was a state sanctuary run by state-appointed officials and hereditary priestly families (Eumolpids, Kerykes); the Great Eleusinia was a polis festival in the state calendar; Athens policed the Mysteries (the 415 BC scandals — profanation trials); admission was regulated, not free. Scholars: Parker (mystery cults within, not against, polis religion); Brut Zaidman (Religion in the Ancient Greek City — ritual as civic structure). Judgement: both — initiation was chosen but administered by the polis; the Mysteries show Greek religion's unusual capacity for personal experience within a civic frame, and a top answer holds the tension rather than resolving it.
'Delphi mattered more for politics than for private worshippers.' How far do you agree? [30]
Answer: For politics: state consultations preceded wars and colonisations (the Sicilian expedition's omens; colony-founders' approvals); Sparta's kings and laws needed Delphic approval; sacred wars were fought over the sanctuary; the Sacred Way's treasuries are states' dedications; the Pythia's prophecies steered policy (Croesus, Herodotus 1). For private worshippers: the prescribed Kodros Painter kylix shows the Pythia in consultation with private inquirers; individuals sought advice on marriage, travel, property; Dodona's lead tablets show the personal oracle market beside Delphi; the fee (pelanos) made consultation open to ordinary people. Scholars: Price (Religions of the Ancient Greeks — Delphi's dual clientele); Emerson (Greek Sanctuaries — the sanctuary's civic layout serving both). Judgement: Delphi's fame and its revenues depended on both — state business gave it power, private consultation gave it volume; a top answer uses the prescribed kylix against the treasury evidence and argues the sanctuary's design (adyton, Sacred Way, treasuries) served both audiences.
'Xenophanes and Socrates destroyed traditional Greek religion.' How far do you agree? [30]
Answer: For: Xenophanes' critique struck at anthropomorphism — the core of the traditional understanding (gods drawn like men, like Ethiopians, like horses); Socrates' daimonion and rejection of the Homeric gods' behaviour undermined the literary foundation Herodotus credited (Homer and Hesiod, 2.53); the trial and execution of 399 BC (impiety, new divinities, corrupting the young) shows the clash was real and fatal; Anaxagoras' prosecution shows the pattern. Against: the critique never destroyed practice — temples kept being built, sacrifices kept being offered, festivals continued; Xenophanes still posited a supreme god; Socrates still swore by Zeus and obeyed the city's gods (as his final request shows); Plato's Socrates defends traditional piety in the Euthyphro; philosophy adopted the gods' vocabulary rather than abolishing it. Scholars: Parker (the persistence of polis religion); Price (coexistence of philosophy and cult). Judgement: they challenged, not destroyed — traditional religion absorbed the critique, and the impiety trials mark the tension, not a victory; a top answer weighs the prosecution evidence against the persistence of cult and argues coexistence.
'Temples were built to honour the gods, not to serve worshippers.' How far do you agree? [30]
Answer: For honouring gods: the temple houses the god's statue and property (the Acropolis as Athena's treasury; the Parthenon's chryselephantine Athena; Olympia's Zeus by Pheidias); sacrifice happens at the altar outside — the temple is not a church; the god, not the congregation, is the building's occupant. For worshippers: the sanctuary around the temple served worshippers — Delphi's adyton for consultation, the treasuries, theatre and stadium (the prescribed parts of both Delphi and Olympia); the Parthenon frieze depicts worshippers (the procession) on the god's own building; the Erechtheion housed multiple cults for civic use; temples anchored festivals that were the polis' social glue. Scholars: Emerson (Greek Sanctuaries — layout and function); Jenkins? (use Parker instead: sanctuaries as civic infrastructure). Judgement: the temple is the god's house but the sanctuary is the worshippers' — the two are designed together; a top answer distinguishes temple from sanctuary (the prescribed sites' layout is the evidence) and argues the composite served both.
How significant was the Panathenaia for Athens? [30]
Answer: Civic unity: the procession represented all Athenians (kanephoroi, arrephoroi, ergastinai, athletes, magistrates) and the Parthenon frieze monumentalises it as an eternal image of the polis; religion: the hecatomb, the peplos presented to Athena Polias and the city's defining act of reciprocity; politics: Peisistratus' use of the festival (contests from 566 BC), the Greater Panathenaia's Panhellenic reach (athletes and visitors from across the Greek world — Athens as imperial capital by the 5th century BC), prizes of Panathenaic amphorae (the prescribed BM 1856,1001.1) spreading Athens' image; economy: the sacred oil, the contests, the visitors. Limits: other festivals (the Dionysia) mattered for drama and empire; Delphi and Olympia mattered Panhellenically. Scholars: Parker (Polytheism and Society at Athens — the festival's civic role); Simon? (use Price: the polis' calendar as political order). Judgement: the Panathenaia was Athens' defining festival — civic, religious and political at once; its significance is best shown by the Parthenon frieze, which turns the procession into the city's self-image; a top answer ranks the festival against the polis' other religious business.
