Greek Theatre

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level OCR

A* OCR H408/21 revision for Greek Theatre: Oedipus Tyrannus, Bacchae and Frogs — the City Dionysia, theatre buildings and vases, Aristotle's tragedy theory and comic technique — with full 30-mark model answers.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain the role and significance of drama in Athenian society, including the religious context and organisation of the City Dionysia (H408/21; AO1/AO2)
  • Analyse the structure of the theatre space and its machinery (crane, wheel platform) and how these developed in the 5th and 4th centuries BC (AO2)
  • Evaluate Aristotle's theories of tragedy (peripeteia, hamartia, catharsis) applied to Oedipus Tyrannus and Bacchae (AO2)
  • Analyse the nature of old comedy and Aristophanes' comic technique in Frogs (AO2)
  • Interpret the prescribed vases (e.g. the Pronomos Vase, the Choregos Vase) as evidence for staging and for Athenian society (AO1/AO2)
  • Deploy secondary scholars (e.g. Easterling, Wiles, Csapo and Slater, Dugdale) to support analysis and reach substantiated judgements (AO2)

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

City Dionysia: The great Athenian festival of Dionysus Eleuthereus (late March/April) at which tragedies, satyr-plays and comedies competed; the dramatic context of the set plays.
choregos: A wealthy citizen who funded and trained a chorus as a public duty (liturgy/litourgia) — the financial backbone of Athenian drama.
skene: The stage building providing the scene's backdrop, doors for entrances/exits and (from the 5th century) a roof usable as a playing area.
ekkylema: The wheel platform rolled out through the skene doors to reveal interior scenes, usually tableaux of violence (e.g. Agave with Pentheus' head).
mechane: The crane by which gods appeared above the stage — hence deus ex machina; used for Euripides' divine endings (Bacchae).
parabasis: The choral interlude in old comedy where the chorus, speaking for the poet, addresses the audience directly and drops the fiction.
agon: A formal, structured debate in drama (e.g. Aeschylus vs Euripides in Frogs; Dionysus and Pentheus in Bacchae).
hamartia: Aristotle's term for the protagonist's tragic mistake or error — best read as a miscalculation rather than a moral flaw; central to interpretations of Oedipus.
catharsis: Aristotle's claim that tragedy purges the emotions of pity and fear in the audience; the standard ancient defence of tragedy's value.
peripeteia: The reversal of fortune in a tragedy; in Oedipus Tyrannus it coincides with the anagnorisis (recognition) at the shepherd's revelation.

📌 Key Points

H408/21 is 1 hour 45 minutes, 75 marks, 30% of the A Level; set texts: Sophocles' Oedipus the King (Oedipus Tyrannus), Euripides' Bacchae, Aristophanes' Frogs.
Section A (compulsory): short-answer and commentary questions responding to prescribed visual stimuli, plus a shorter essay. Section B: a choice of one from two 30-mark essays, where secondary sources and academic views are credited.
Prescribed visual sources include the Theatre of Dionysus at Athens, the Theatre of Thorikos, the Pronomos Vase, the Choregos Vase (Comic Angels), the Basel Dancers krater, the British Museum bird-chorus chous (B509) and the Douris kylix of Pentheus' death.
Aristotle's Poetics supplies the tragic theory (peripeteia, hamartia, catharsis) that must be applied — not merely recited — to the set plays.

Drama in Athenian society and the theatre space

Drama at Athens was a civic and religious institution, not secular entertainment. The City Dionysia honoured Dionysus Eleuthereus with a procession (pompe), sacrifice and the display of tribute and orphans before competition began: three tragedians each presented a tetralogy of three tragedies plus a satyr-play, then five comedians a single play each. The archon selected the poets and allotted each a choregos, a rich citizen funding and training the chorus as a liturgy — theatre ran on private wealth spent for public honour. The audience, filling the Theatre of Dionysus (traditionally c.14,000–17,000), comprised male citizens alongside metics, foreigners and (debated) women; ten judges drawn by lot ranked the plays — democracy in the seating, adjudication by lottery.

The theatre space evolved through the 5th and 4th centuries BC. The koilon (auditorium, initially wooden banks, later stone) curved round the orchestra (dancing floor for chorus and dithyramb); the skene gave doors and backdrop; the proskenion and its roof extended playing space. Machinery shaped staging: the ekkylema (wheel platform) revealed interior violence, and the mechane (crane) lowered gods — Euripides' Bacchae ends with Dionysus ex machina. The Theatre of Thorikos, a coastal deme theatre with an irregular, elongated auditorium, shows drama rooted below the polis level. The prescribed vases picture the performance itself: the Pronomos Vase shows a victorious team — actors, costumes, masks, aulos-player, playwright, Dionysus; the Choregos Vase (Comic Angels) a comic chorus with choregoi; the Basel Dancers krater a chorus in costume; the British Museum chous (B509, c.480 BC) two chorus-men dressed as birds; the Douris kylix the death of Pentheus; the Phiale Painter pelike tragic actors dressing. These are the evidence base for Section A commentary questions — know appearance, style, content and original location.

Competition ran over several days: dithyrambic choruses of 50 men and boys (one for each tribe) opened the festival; tragedy filled the following days in tetralogies; comedy closed it. Before performance the poets held a proagon — presenting their casts without costume — and after it the judges awarded crowns and prizes, with the didaskalika records preserving results. The audience composition is debated: the war orphans paraded in armour, and women, metics and foreigners attended alongside citizens — but the precise proportion of women is genuinely open, since the evidence (Aristophanes' jokes about women in the audience; the vases' seating scenes) is comic or ambiguous. Theatre was also political space: announcements were made to the assembled city — the tribute display, and in Frogs' earlier version the debate on restoring citizen rights to the disenfranchised.

The nature of tragedy and the tragedians

Origins and development. Aristotle (Poetics) traced tragedy to improvisation by dithyramb leaders — a hymn to Dionysus — and defined it as the imitation (mimesis) of a serious, complete action in elevated language, effecting catharsis of pity and fear. From satyr-play roots it developed through the 5th century BC: Aeschylus introduced the second actor, reduced the chorus and built connected trilogies (his Agamemnon shows the form at its most ceremonial); Sophocles added the third actor, developed scene-painting and built plays on character in crisis; Euripides brought psychological realism, rhetoric and gods onstage by crane. All three expanded actors' roles against the chorus: tragedy's history is the progressive victory of dialogue over song.

Aristotle's theories — peripeteia (reversal), hamartia (tragic mistake), catharsis (purgation) — are tools, not verdicts. Applied to Oedipus Tyrannus: the peripeteia (the shepherd's revelation) coincides exactly with the recognition, which Aristotle thought the plot's ideal shape; but hamartia is contested — is Oedipus rash (killing at the crossroads, cursing the murderer publicly), or simply ignorant of facts no enquiry could spare him? Note also the plot's religious frame: Apollo's oracles drive every character, and the plague in the prologue punishes the unavenged murderer's pollution (miasma).

Bacchae: Dionysus' revenge on Thebes for denying his divinity. The prologue epiphany, the palace miracle (Dionysus' escape from bonds), Pentheus' agon with the disguised god, the messenger speeches from Cithaeron, the sparagmos (Agave with her son's head) and the deus ex machina ending structure a plot in which resistance to a god, not ignorance, destroys the house. The two plays give Section B essays their material: fate and free will, prophecy, the polis and its kings, tragic heroism, and the role of women (Jocasta; Agave) and foreigners (the Lydian god and his Asian maenads).

Satyr-play is the fourth member of each tragic tetralogy: burlesque with a chorus dressed as satyrs under Silenus, on the same myths treated absurdly — the only complete survivor is Euripides' Cyclops. The development can be tracked through the tragedians' innovations: Aeschylus' second actor (Aristotle credits him), his connected trilogies (the Oresteia's legal and religious resolution), his choral writing; Sophocles' third actor, scene-painting and skene development, and plays built on a single character in crisis; Euripides' rhetorical debates, realistic characters, and prologues that state the myth in advance. Aristotle also transmits the tradition that tragedy's subject matter moved from the satyric and Dionysiac to the heroic — 'it was not long before serious subjects ousted the trivial' (Poetics) — which the exam expects you to weigh against the plays themselves: the Bacchae is nothing if not Dionysiac.

Tragedy in performance: Oedipus Tyrannus and Bacchae

Oedipus Tyrannus is the exam's set-text anchor. Plot devices to cite: the plague prologue; Teiresias' accusations; Jocasta's dismissal of prophecy (which triggers the fatal enquiry); the Corinthian messenger's 'relief' that becomes the catastrophe; the old Theban shepherd's refusal to speak, then confession — the recognition; the blinded Oedipus revealed by the ekkylemma. Dramatic irony governs the play: every speech about the murderer is spoken by the murderer to an audience that knows the myth. For religion: Apollo's oracle at Delphi (and to Laius before him) structures fate against free will — Oedipus acts to escape prophecy and so fulfils it; for the polis: the king is saviour (the Sphinx) then pollution; Thebes' health and the king's oikos are one. For tragic heroism: Oedipus the detective — his relentless pursuit of truth is both his greatness and his destruction; punishment falls on eyes that saw too late.

Bacchae answers themes of divine justice and the polis: Pentheus, king of Thebes, denies Dionysus' divinity, imprisons him, mocks his rites, and is led — dressed as a maenad — to death on Cithaeron by the god himself; his mother Agave carries back his head. Cite: the prologue (Dionysus states the plot in advance — fate, not suspense); the bonds-miracle of the palace scene; the herdsman's two messenger speeches (the miracle on the mountain, then the sparagmos); the chorus' piety against Pentheus' rationalism; the god's cruelty in the ex machina ending (exile for the survivors — divinity beyond human ethics). The prescribed Douris kylix shows Pentheus' death in red-figure c.480 BC — before either surviving play — proving myth preceded drama and giving AO3-style comparison of visual and literary treatment; the Capodarso krater fragment pictures Jocasta and Oedipus.

For both plays, weigh ancient vs modern reception: Aristotle admired Oedipus as the tragic plot; modern readers stress its detective structure and moral ambiguity, or read Bacchae as psychodrama of repression and ecstasy. A* answers hold ancient theory and modern interpretation together.

For Oedipus Tyrannus, also cite: the Sphinx backstory — Oedipus' salvation of Thebes is the reason he is king, so his rise and fall are one arc; the self-blinding with Jocasta's brooches, revealed by the ekkylemma; Creon's regency and the exile question; the chorus' closing wisdom — 'count no man happy until he has died free of pain'. For Bacchae, cite: the Teiresias-and-Cadmus scene (old men dressing as worshippers — religion as choice against ridicule); the two herdsman speeches (the mountain miracle, then the sparagmos report); Agave's recognition scene, where the god corrects her illusion with cruelty; and the chorus' hymns — piety in lyric against the palace's rationalism.

Old comedy and Aristophanes' Frogs

Nature of old comedy. Aristophanes' own parabases claim comedy began in komos revelry and phallic song; its 5th-century BC development produced a form whose tools are personal invective, obscenity, parody and topical satire, aimed at politicians (Cleon), intellectuals (Socrates in Clouds), poets and the audience itself. Structure: prologue (fantastic premise), parodos, agon (formal debate), parabasis (chorus speaks for the poet), episodes, exodos; plot devices include parody of tragedy, choral interludes and breaking the illusion.

Frogs (405 BC) is the set text. Premise: Dionysus, starved of good tragedy since Euripides' death, descends to Hades with slave Xanthias (whose loaded-jokes opening parodies tragedy's solemnity) to bring back a poet — Heracles' street-map of the underworld, the frog-chorus, the Mysteries procession, then the contest: Aeschylus vs Euripides weighed in the scales — style, prologues, lyrics, and finally political utility: which poet can save the city? Aeschylus wins; Pluto sends him home with instructions to advise Athens. Cite for comedy's themes: the satire of tragedy (parody of Euripides' prologues; the weighing of verses); religion (the katabasis, the Eleusinian-flavoured mystic chorus, death and the afterlife as comic geography); the polis (the play was performed in a city losing the Peloponnesian War — its civic advice and reported effect were famous, and later tradition credited it with Athens' decision to recall the exiles). Humour types to name: personal abuse, knockabout slave comedy (Xanthias), literary parody, obscenity, and topical political jokes whose modern loss you should note as an interpretive limit.

Styles compared: Aeschylus' grandeur vs Euripides' wit inside the play mirrors the tragedians' real contrast; Aristophanes' Frogs stages the debate the whole component is about — what drama is for. A* answers use the play as evidence for how a 5th-century audience understood tragedy, and weigh it against Aristotle's later theory.

For Frogs, also cite: the disguise contest (Dionysus and Xanthias both dressed as Heracles, the lion skin swapped back and forth — a running gag that parodies epic heroism); the underworld geography (Charon's ferry, Aeacus' gatekeeping, the frog-chorus on the lake); the Mysteries chorus whose initiation language frames the descent; the judging scenes — Euripides' prologues tripped up by Aeschylus' oil-flask interruptions, the scales in which verses are physically weighed, and the final test of political utility: Aeschylus wins because his poetry makes men fighting-fit ('Seven Against Thebes filled them with courage') and returns to advise the city. The play's civic advice was topical in a city losing the war, and the reported effect was famous enough for later tradition to credit it with Athens' recall of the exiles.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: 'The faults lie with Apollo, not Oedipus.' How far do you agree with this view of Oedipus Tyrannus? Support your answer with detailed reference to the text. [30]
Mark-scheme logic: AO1 evidence required: the plague prologue (Thebes' pollution from the unavenged murder); the Delphic oracle to Laius and to Oedipus; Teiresias' scene (Oedipus' anger, the accusation 'you are the murderer'); Jocasta's dismissal of prophecy and the memory of the crossroads; the Corinthian messenger's report; the shepherd's confession (recognition/peripeteia); the blinding and Oedipus' final speeches. AO2 must weigh both halves. Apollo's responsibility: the prophecy predates the play; every character acts to escape or serve it; Oedipus is born cursed — fate is set before any choice; the plague is Apollo's punishment for a crime committed in ignorance. Oedipus' responsibility: he kills an old man and his retinue at the crossroads when provoked (a real choice); he curses the murderer publicly and refuses Teiresias' warnings; his relentless self-incriminating enquiry is deliberate; Aristotle's hamartia locates the error in the protagonist, and readings from hubris (Knox) to innocence (Vellacott's 'faultless Oedipus') exist. Scholars: Easterling (tragedy's tension between human choice and divine order); Aristotle (Poetics — the coincidence of recognition and reversal as the ideal plot). Top-band judgement: the play engineers both readings — Apollo ordains, Oedipus chooses how to seek the truth, and his greatness (the intelligence that beat the Sphinx) is precisely what destroys him; the Levels of Response reward a sustained evaluation of that design, not a verdict for one party.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

'In the Bacchae, Pentheus is destroyed for resisting a god.' How far do you agree? [30]
Answer: For: the prologue has Dionysus announce the punishment in advance — resistance is the crime and the plot; Pentheus insults the god, jails him and mocks the rites; the palace miracle proves divinity he still denies; his death is staged and narrated as divine justice; Agave's sparagmos shows the house destroyed by the god's design. Against: the god is cruel and excessive — he deceives a king into cross-dressing and a mother into butchery; the ex machina ending exiles the innocent (Cadmus, Agave); Pentheus' doubts look like rationalism rather than impiety, and Euripides makes the god's behaviour the scandal; Aristotle's categories barely fit — this is epiphany, not hamartia. Scholars: Easterling (Euripides' treatment of the divine as problematic); Wiles (theatre space and ritual — the play as initiation drama). Judgement: agree that resistance triggers the plot, but the play's disturbing feature is that the god who punishes impiety behaves impiously — a top answer holds divine justice and divine cruelty together.
'The Pronomos Vase and the Choregos Vase tell us more about Athenian society than about staging.' How far do you agree? [30]
Answer: For society: the Pronomos Vase names and shows actors, the poet, the aulos-player, Dionysus and Herakles in costume — evidence for star actors, the dramatic team as a social unit, and the choregos-funded celebration; the Choregos Vase (Comic Angels) shows choregoi and a comic chorus — the liturgy system in material form; both prove theatre's civic prestige and the social reach of Dionysiac celebration. For staging: the Pronomos Vase shows masks, costumes (short sleeves under the tragic long-sleeve robe), padded tights? and the aulos — performance practice; the Choregos Vase shows the chorus's costumes and comic body-suits; but neither shows the theatre space, the orchestra or the skene — staging questions (scenic change, chorus position) need the buildings and archaeology instead. Scholars: Csapo and Slater (The Context of Ancient Drama — the vases as evidence for production); Wiles (performance space). Judgement: the vases are best for the people of the theatre (teams, choregoi, costume, celebration) and weakest for architecture; a top answer allocates each source to what it proves and notes the limits.
'Frogs is nothing more than cheap laughs.' How far do you agree? [30]
Answer: For: slapstick (Xanthias and the donkey), obscenity, personal invective aimed at contemporary politicians and poets, and the fantastic premise of a god in disguise are pure comic material; the frog-chorus is a song of nonsense syllables. Against: the play's core is serious — a formal agon judging tragedy's masters, a parabasis advising the city in a losing war, a weighing of verses that is real literary criticism, and a political outcome (advice to recall the exiles) tradition credited with effect; the Mysteries chorus and the katabasis treat death, initiation and piety seriously within comedy. Scholars: MacDowell (Aristophanes and Athens — comedy as engaged political commentary); Storey and Allan (comedy's civic function). Judgement: the jokes are the vehicle; the cargo is a city's crisis — Aristophanes uses laughter to argue that poetry shapes politics, which is exactly why the play survives; a top answer distinguishes the humour types and shows the civic argument inside them.
'The theatre of Dionysus was built for the audience, not for the actors.' How far do you agree? [30]
Answer: For the audience: the koilon curves to seat thousands (traditionally c.14,000–17,000) with sightlines to the whole orchestra — civic spectacle before drama; seating by tribe (cleisthenic rows) and the presence of citizens, judges, orphans and tribute made it a political assembly; Thorikos' elongated irregular auditorium shows audience capacity shaped even deme theatres. For the actors: the skene's doors, roof (proskenion) and machinery — ekkylema and mechane — are actor technology; the raised stage (4th century) gave actors visibility against a huge chorus space; masks and costumes (Pronomos Vase; Phiale Painter pelike) solved projection. Scholars: Wiles (space as meaning — the orchestra as Dionysiac dancing floor vs the skene as fiction); Csapo and Slater (production evidence). Judgement: the space serves both, in tension — the chorus's orchestra shrinks as the actors' stage rises, and that development (5th to 4th centuries BC) is the component's key narrative; a top answer traces it chronologically.
'Aristotle's Poetics explains everything about Greek tragedy.' How far do you agree? [30]
Answer: For: Poetics gives the working theory — mimesis, plot with peripeteia and anagnorisis, hamartia, catharsis of pity and fear; Oedipus Tyrannus fits it so well that Aristotle uses it as his ideal example; the theory structures exam analysis of every set play. Against: Poetics was written generations after the 5th-century plays (c.335 BC vs 430–405 BC); it ignores satyr-plays' role, music, masks and staging; Bacchae fits its categories badly (a god as protagonist? the villain wins and exiles the survivors?); Frogs is comedy, outside Poetics' tragic theory entirely; and Aristotle's catharsis is notoriously undefined. Scholars: Easterling (the gap between Aristotle and performance); Wiles (Poetics as theory of literature, not of theatre). Judgement: Poetics is the best ancient theory and a superb analytic tool, but it is one reader's 4th-century BC reconstruction — a top answer applies it, tests it, and marks where the plays escape it.
