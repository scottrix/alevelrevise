The Late Republic

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level OCR

The Roman Republic's fall: the Gracchi, Marius and Sulla, Caesar, Pompey and Cicero — political structures, land reform, the civil wars and Cicero's letters, with OCR H407 essay technique.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain the Roman Republic's political structures (consuls, senate, assemblies, tribunes)
  • Trace the crisis: the Gracchi's land reform and its consequences
  • Analyse Marius, Sulla and the civil wars' impact on the Republic
  • Evaluate Caesar, Pompey and the Republic's fall (49-44 BC)
  • Assess Cicero's letters and the sources' reliability

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Consul: One of two annually elected chief magistrates of the Republic; commanded armies, presided over the senate — the Republic's executive.
Senate: The council of ~300 ex-magistrates (seniores); advised the magistrates, controlled finance and foreign policy — the Republic's real power.
Tribune: One of ten tribunes of the plebs; protected plebeians (veto, auxilium), proposed laws to the plebeian assembly — the Gracchi's office.
Dictator: The six-month emergency magistracy; Sulla's and Caesar's violations (dictator perpetuus) broke the Republic's rules.
Gracchi: Tiberius (tribune 133 BC) and Gaius (123-122 BC) Gracchus — land reformers whose deaths began the Republic's crisis.
Optimates: The senatorial 'best men' faction — defended the senate's traditional power.
Populares: Politicians who appealed to the people (tribunes, assemblies) against the senate — Caesar's method.
Proscription: Sulla's purge (82 BC) — lists of enemies executed, property confiscated; the civil war's terror.

The Republic's political structures

Magistracies. The consuls (two annually elected, the Republic's executive — armies, the senate's presidency); the praetors (judges); the censors (the census, morality); the tribunes of the plebs (ten — the plebeians' protectors, veto power, proposed laws); the dictator (six months, emergency only). The senate (~300 ex-magistrates): advised (senatus consulta — 'decrees of the senate' = the magistrates' advice), controlled finance, foreign policy and the provinces; the Republic's real power. The assemblies: the comitia centuriata (military — elected consuls, declared war), the comitia tributa and the concilium plebis (tribal — elected tribunes, passed laws — the plebiscites).

The cursus honorum. The ladder of offices: quaestor (finance), aedile (games, streets), praetor, consul — with age minima and the two-year gap; competition for office drove faction (the optimates vs populares). Patronage: the clientela (patrons and clients) — the social glue; the nobiles' (nobles') dominance (consulships in families).

The crisis: Gracchi, Marius, Sulla

The Gracchi (133-121 BC). Tiberius Gracchus (tribune 133): land reform (the lex Sempronia agraria — the public land's limit, 500 iugera), funded by Attalus' bequest; bypassed the senate (took the bill to the plebeian assembly, deposed his fellow tribune Octavius — unconstitutional), sought re-election; killed in the senate-house riot (133 — the first political murder in Rome since the kings). Gaius Gracchus (tribune 123-122): grain law (subsidised grain), courts to the equites, citizenship proposals; died in the senatus consultum ultimum's crackdown (121 — the 'final decree', the emergency powers). Significance: the Gracchi normalised political violence, faction and the tribunate's use against the senate — the Republic's crisis begins here (Cicero's and Sallust's claim).

Marius and Sulla. Marius (consul 107, 104-100, 86): the military reform (recruited the propertyless — the capite censi; the professional army, loyal to its general, not the state — the Marian reforms' consequence: civil war); the Jugurthine and Cimbrian wars. Sulla (consul 88, dictator 82-79): the march on Rome (88 — the first, against Marius; the civil war 83-82); the proscriptions (82 — ~2,000 senators/equites listed and killed); the dictatorship (unconstitutional duration); the reforms (the senate's powers restored, the tribunate's castration). Significance: Sulla showed that armies could take Rome — Caesar learned from him.

Caesar, Pompey and the Republic's fall

The First Triumvirate (60 BC). Caesar, Pompey and Crassus — an unofficial alliance (Caesar's consulship 59, the land bills, the Gallic command). Cicero's opposition: the consul of 63 (the Catiline conspiracy), the optimates' voice; his letters (Ad Atticum, Ad Familiares) preserve the Republic's fall from the inside — the standard source.

The fall (49-44 BC). Crassus' death (Carrhae, 53) broke the triumvirate; Julia's death (54) broke the Caesar-Pompey bond; the senate (Pompey's party) ordered Caesar to disband (49); Caesar crossed the Rubicon (49 — civil war); Pompey defeated at Pharsalus (48) and murdered in Egypt; Caesar's dictatorship (dictator perpetuus, 44 — the unconstitutional title); the Ides of March (44 — the assassination by Brutus and Cassius, the Republic's last hope). Caesar's significance: the populares' method (the tribunician laws, the people's support), the army's loyalty (the Marian legacy), the dictatorship's normalisation — the Republic's institutions could not contain him.

Sources and essay technique

Cicero's letters (Ad Atticum, Ad Familiares, Ad Quintum): the Republic's fall from the inside — contemporary, candid, but partisan (Cicero's self-image) and fragmentary (the letters survive unevenly). Cicero's speeches (the Catilinarians, Pro Milone): published and edited — not verbatim. Plutarch (c. AD 100): the Lives (Caesar, Pompey, Cicero, Marius, Sulla) — biographical, moralising, from earlier writers (Sallust, Pollio); 500 years' distance. Suetonius (c. AD 120): the Twelve Caesars — anecdotal, gossipy. Appian (2nd century AD): the Civil Wars — the fullest narrative of the period, from earlier sources. Sallust (c. 40 BC): the Catiline conspiracy — contemporary, moralising (the Republic's decadence theme). Reliability: the standard pairing — 'Cicero for the inside view, Sallust for the moralising, Plutarch/Suetonius for the anecdotes, Appian for the narrative — none neutral'.

Essay technique. A 30-mark essay: thesis, four developed points, counter-considerations, judgement. Standard questions: 'Was the Republic doomed before Caesar?' (FOR: the Gracchi's violence, the Marian army, Sulla's example; AGAINST: the Republic survived crises, the institutions held until 49); 'Was Caesar a tyrant?' (the dictatorship's normalisation vs the reforms' popularity); 'How reliable are Cicero's letters?' (candid but partisan). Use the sources against each other and the events.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: 'The fall of the Republic was caused by the Gracchi, not Caesar.' How far do you agree? [OCR H407 style, 30 marks]
Mark-scheme logic: AO1: the Gracchi (133-121: political violence, the tribunate's use), Marius' army (the professional, loyal to generals), Sulla's example (the march, the proscriptions), Caesar's fall (49-44). AO2: FOR — the structural damage was done before Caesar: the Gracchi normalised violence and faction; the Marian army made civil war possible; Sulla showed it (the proscriptions); AGAINST — the Republic survived 133-49 (the institutions held); Caesar's ambition (the Rubicon, the dictatorship perpetuus) was personal; the optimates' failures (Pompey's party) mattered. Judgement: the Gracchi planted the causes, Caesar harvested them — the claim is half-right: the structural causes were early, but the fall needed Caesar's ambition; say why both were necessary.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Explain the role of the tribunes of the plebs. [10 marks]
Answer: Ten annually elected tribunes: the plebeians' protectors (auxilium — the right to intercede); veto power over the senate and magistrates; proposed laws to the concilium plebis (the plebiscites bound all citizens after 287, the lex Hortensia); sacrosanct (inviolable). The Gracchi used the tribunate against the senate (Tiberius 133, Gaius 123-122) — the tribunate became the populares' weapon; Sulla castrated it, Caesar used it (the tribunicia potestas).
How far was Marius' military reform responsible for the Republic's fall? [30 marks]
Answer: FOR: recruiting the propertyless (capite censi) created a professional army loyal to its general (land, booty, pensions) — not the state; the Marian legions fought for Caesar and Pompey; the civil wars (Sulla's march, 88) followed. AGAINST: the Republic survived Marius (107-86) — the reform's effects took decades; Sulla's example (the march, the proscriptions) was the direct trigger; the optimates' failures (the senate's faction) mattered. Judgement: the Marian reform created the instrument (a general's army) — Sulla and Caesar used it; the fall needed the ambition and the crisis; the reform was necessary but not sufficient.
Assess Cicero's letters as a source for the fall of the Republic. [20 marks]
Answer: AO1 (value): contemporary (58-43 BC), candid (to Atticus — private opinions), the inside view of the senate's faction, Pompey's party, Caesar's rise; unique detail. AO2 (problems): partisan (Cicero's self-image, the optimates' cause), fragmentary (the letters survive unevenly), private correspondence (not the public record). Judgement: uniquely valuable for the inside view — but Cicero's self-presentation colours everything; weigh the letters against Sallust's moralising and Appian's narrative.
Explain the significance of Sulla's dictatorship. [10 marks]
Answer: Sulla's dictatorship (82-79 BC): unconstitutional (the six-month rule broken; the proscriptions ~2,000 killed, property confiscated); the reforms (the senate's powers restored, the tribunate castrated, the courts reorganised); the example — an army could take Rome and remake the constitution; Caesar learned from him (the dictatorship perpetuus, 44). Significance: the Republic's rules broken openly; the civil war's terror normalised; the optimates' restoration failed (the Republic fell within 40 years).
'Caesar was a tyrant who destroyed the Republic.' How far do you agree? [30 marks]
Answer: FOR: the dictatorship perpetuus (44) — the unconstitutional title; the Rubicon (49) — civil war against the Republic; the senate's bypassing (the tribunician laws); the assassination's motive (the Republic's last hope). AGAINST: Caesar's reforms were popular (the calendar, the grain dole, the colonies, citizenship grants); the optimates' intransigence (Pompey's party) caused the war; the Republic's institutions were already broken (Sulla). Judgement: Caesar was the Republic's destroyer — but the Republic he destroyed was already broken; the standard balanced answer: tyrant by the Republic's rules, popular by the people's; say which matters.
