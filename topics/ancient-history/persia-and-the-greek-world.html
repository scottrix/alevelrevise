Persia and the Greek World

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level OCR

The Persian Wars from Ionian Revolt to Plataea: Herodotus' account, Marathon, Thermopylae, Salamis and Plataea, with source-reliability analysis and OCR H407 essay technique.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain the causes of the Persian Wars from the Ionian Revolt (499-494 BC) to Darius' expedition
  • Analyse the battles of Marathon, Thermopylae, Salamis and Plataea with their strategic significance
  • Evaluate Herodotus' reliability as a source (bias, chronology, numbers, speeches)
  • Assess why the Greeks defeated Persia and the wars' impact on Athens and Sparta
  • Construct OCR essay and source-based answers with AO1 knowledge and AO2 analysis

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Ionian Revolt: The rebellion of the Greek city-states of Ionia (499-494 BC) against Persian rule, led initially by Aristagoras of Miletus; its suppression triggered Darius' invasion of Greece.
Herodotus: The 'father of history' (c. 484-425 BC); his Histories are the principal narrative source for the Persian Wars, written in the generation after the events.
Hoplite: A Greek citizen-soldier equipped with shield (aspis), spear and bronze panoply, fighting in the phalanx formation.
Trireme: The Greek warship with three banks of oars and a bronze ram; decisive in the naval battle of Salamis.
Hellenotamiai: The treasurers of the Delian League, which later became the Athenian empire.
Spartiate: A full citizen of Sparta, entitled to membership of the phalanx and the syssition (mess).
Ephor: One of five annually elected Spartan magistrates who overseen the kings and exercised extensive power.
Satrapy: One of the provinces of the Persian Empire, governed by a satrap responsible to the Great King.

Causes: the Ionian Revolt and Darius' invasion

Background. The Persian Empire under Darius I (522-486 BC) controlled Ionia — the Greek city-states of the Asia Minor coast — through local tyrants and satraps. In 499 BC Aristagoras of Miletus, having failed in an attack on Naxos, launched the Ionian Revolt, appealing for help to mainland Greece. Athens and Eretria sent ships; the Greeks burned Sardis (498 BC) before the revolt was crushed by 494 (the naval defeat at Lade). The revolt matters for three reasons: it gave Persia a pretext to punish Athens and Eretria; it taught Persia the difficulty of coastal campaigning; and Herodotus frames it as the origin (aitiai) of the wars.

Darius' expedition (492-490 BC). Mardonius' campaign (492) re-subdued Thrace and Macedonia before the fleet was wrecked at Athos. In 490 a seaborne force under Datis and Artaphernes destroyed Eretria and landed at Marathon. Herodotus' cause-analysis is partial: he omits Persia's wider strategic interest in the Aegean and presents the wars as personal revenge narratives — a standard AO2 evaluation point.

Marathon (490 BC). c. 9,000-10,000 Athenians and 1,000 Plataeans under Miltiades defeated a larger Persian force. Herodotus gives the Greek dead as 192 against 6,400 Persian — numbers most historians doubt. The Athenian phalanx charged the Persians (running the mile of arrow-range), thinned its centre and enveloped the wings. The battle's significance: it ended the first invasion, made Athens the saviour of Greece, and — with Themistocles' silver-mines windfall (483 BC, Laurion) — funded the fleet that won Salamis. The lone Spartan march (arrived after the battle, Pheidippides' Spartans celebrating the Carneia) illustrates Greek disunity.

The second invasion: Thermopylae, Salamis, Plataea

Xerxes' invasion (480-479 BC). Xerxes crossed the Hellespont on pontoon bridges with a massive land force (Herodotus' 1,700,000 is impossible; modern estimates 150,000-300,000) and a large fleet, fed by a supply chain along the Thracian coast.

Thermopylae (480 BC). Leonidas held the pass with c. 300 Spartiates, 700 Thespians and allied contingents; the pass fell after the local betrayal (Ephialtes' path), and the rearguard died to cover the retreat. Strategically the delay achieved little, but the moral exemplum (Simonides' epitaph: 'go tell the Spartans') shaped Greek memory. The simultaneous naval action at Artemisium damaged the Persian fleet and prepared Salamis.

Salamis (480 BC). Themistocles evacuated Athens and trapped the Persian fleet in the narrow strait — either by genuine strategy or by secret message to Xerxes (Herodotus records the alleged trick; the standard essay debate). The Persian fleet, outflanked in confined waters where the heavier Greek triremes and disciplined rams excelled, was destroyed. Salamis' significance: it secured the Greek mainland's sea communications and forced Xerxes to withdraw with Mardonius.

Plataea (479 BC). The largest hoplite army yet assembled (Herodotus: 38,700 Spartans and helots among c. 110,000 Greeks) under Pausanias destroyed Mardonius' army and ended the invasion. The parallel victory at Mycale freed Ionia. The wars' aftermath: the Delian League (478 BC) turned Athens' leadership into empire (thesaurus at Delos, phoros tribute), setting up the Peloponnesian War; Sparta's prestige declined.

Herodotus: reliability and historiography

Method. Herodotus (writing c. 440-425 BC) claims autopsy, enquiry (historia) and reportage of what he heard — he names his sources and distinguishes versions. Problems. (1) Chronology: he wrote a generation after the events; oral tradition distorted detail. (2) Numbers: Persian army sizes (1,700,000+) and casualty figures are rhetorical exaggerations. (3) Bias: pro-Athenian framing (Athens as saviour of Greece); anti-Persian moralising (barbarian decadence, e.g. Xerxes' flogging of the Hellespont); the speeches are composed, not recorded. (4) Narrative patterning: divine causation, dreams and omens structure the story — Herodotus balances human and divine causation but the patterns may be literary. Value. Despite this, Herodotus is indispensable: he preserves the only connected narrative, names his informants, and his critical method (testing versions, noting disagreements) is genuine historiography. OCR essays reward the pairing: 'Herodotus says X — but consider the source's problems — the judgement must weigh both'.

Essay technique: why did Greece win?

Arguments: Greek advantages. (1) Hoplite and naval excellence: the phalanx's discipline (Marathon) and the trireme's ram in confined waters (Salamis). (2) Strategy: Themistocles' fleet, the evacuation of Athens, the choice of Salamis' narrows. (3) Motivation: defence of homeland vs conscripted multi-national levies. (4) Unity at crisis points: the Hellenic League's combined command at Plataea.

Arguments: Persian weaknesses. (1) Logistics: Xerxes' supply chain along the coast was vulnerable; the fleet needed the army. (2) Command: Xerxes' departure after Salamis; Mardonius' fall at Plataea. (3) Diversity of the levies: reluctant conscripts, language barriers (Herodotus' catalogue). (4) Naval limits: Persian ships crewed by levied Phoenicians, Egyptians, Ionians — quality uneven.

Counter-considerations. Greek disunity (the Spartans' Carneia delay, Themistocles' threats to the allies); Persian successes (Eretria's destruction, the Acropolis burn); the wars were close-run (Herodotus' own theme: fortunes reverse). OCR technique: a 30-mark essay needs a thesis, four developed points with named battles/sources, counter-considerations, and a judgement (e.g. Greek tactical brilliance — Salamis' narrows and the phalanx — mattered more than Persian logistical weakness, though both were necessary; the decisive factor was leadership: Themistocles' strategy and Pausanias' command). For source questions, use Herodotus' specifics (numbers, speeches) against the reliability analysis above.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: 'The Greeks defeated Persia because of superior leadership, not superior resources.' How far do you agree? [OCR H407 style, 30 marks]
Mark-scheme logic: AO1: deploy Marathon (Miltiades' envelopment), Salamis (Themistocles' narrows strategy), Plataea (Pausanias' combined command) against Persian logistics (Xerxes' supply chain), numbers (Herodotus' exaggerations) and command failures. AO2: FOR — leadership decided the crises: Themistocles built and deployed the fleet, chose the strait; Miltiades' tactics at Marathon; Pausanias held the alliance at Plataea. AGAINST — resources and structure mattered: the hoplite system and triremes were collective, funded institutions (Laurion's silver); Persian weakness was systemic (logistics, levies), not merely commanded. Judgement: leadership converted existing Greek advantages into victories — but without the fleet (a resource decision) and the phalanx (a social system), leadership alone would have failed; the claim is half-right, and saying which half carries the marks.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Explain why Darius invaded Greece in 490 BC. [10 marks]
Answer: Three developed causes: (1) revenge and punishment for the Ionian Revolt — Athens and Eretria had sent ships that burned Sardis (498 BC), and Herodotus presents Datis/Artaphernes' expedition as aimed at Athens and Eretria specifically; (2) imperial expansion — the Aegean and Macedon were within Persia's reach (Mardonius' 492 campaign re-subdued Thrace and Macedonia before the Athos wreck); (3) regime security — Persian kings justified rule through victory; a punitive expedition displayed the Great King's power after the revolt's suppression.
How useful is Herodotus as a source for the battle of Salamis? [20 marks]
Answer: AO1 (value): Herodotus preserves the only full narrative — the evacuation of Athens, the alleged secret message to Xerxes, the confined waters, the Greek victory order; he names informants and reports variant versions. AO2 (problems): he wrote c. 40-50 years later from oral tradition; his numbers are unreliable; his pro-Hellenic framing and composed speeches (Themistocles') are literary; the alleged trick may be invention to credit Athens. Judgement: useful for the battle's course and Greek unity, less so for numbers and causation — every claim should be weighed against the reliability analysis.
Analyse the significance of Themistocles in the defeat of Persia. [30 marks]
Answer: Developed points: (1) the naval programme — Persuaded Athens to spend Laurion's silver (483 BC) on 200 triremes; without the fleet, no Salamis; (2) strategy — the evacuation of Athens, the choice of the narrows, the alleged secret message; (3) diplomacy — held the fractious allies together (his threats to the Peloponnesians, per Herodotus); (4) aftermath — the Delian League's naval foundation (478 BC) made Athens' empire possible. Counter-considerations: the phalanx's victories (Marathon, Plataea) were not naval; Aristides and Pausanias commanded on land; Sparta's contribution (Thermopylae's exemplum) mattered to Greek morale. Judgement: Themistocles was the decisive individual — the fleet and Salamis were his — but the wars were won by systems and coalitions; his exile (471 BC) shows Athens' suspicion of his success.
Evaluate the view that Herodotus' account of the Persian Wars is more literature than history. [30 marks]
Answer: FOR: the Histories are structured by literary patterns — divine dreams, reversals of fortune, composed speeches; the numbers (1,700,000) are rhetorical; the ethnographic digressions (Egypt, Scythia) serve narrative pleasure; Homer's epics shape the battle narratives (duels, catalogues). AGAINST: Herodotus names sources, distinguishes versions, tests claims (his autopsy in Egypt), and balances human and divine causation; his chronology is broadly sound where cross-checkable; the analytical method (historia = enquiry) is genuine history's foundation. Judgement: the Histories are both — a literary artefact built on real enquiry; the historian's task is to extract the enquiry from the artistry, and OCR essays should say so.
'Salamis was the decisive battle of the Persian Wars.' How far do you agree? [30 marks]
Answer: FOR: Salamis destroyed the Persian fleet, forcing Xerxes' withdrawal — without sea control the supply chain failed; the psychological effect (the Great King checked) transformed Greek confidence. AGAINST: Plataea (479) destroyed the army and ended the invasion — Salamis alone did not; Marathon (490) ended the first invasion and shaped the myth; Thermopylae's delay had moral force. Judgement: Salamis was decisive strategically (fleet = logistics = withdrawal), but the wars' outcome required Plataea's land victory; say why the naval victory mattered more — the army could not stay without the fleet.
