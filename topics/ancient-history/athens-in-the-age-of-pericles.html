Athens in the Age of Pericles

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level OCR

Athenian democracy, empire and Pericles' leadership: institutions, the Delian League to empire, the building programme and Plutarch's Life of Pericles, with OCR H407 essay technique.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain the Athenian democratic institutions and their radical nature
  • Trace the Delian League's transformation into empire (478-454 BC)
  • Analyse Pericles' leadership, building programme and relationships (Plutarch, Thucydides)
  • Evaluate the reliability of Plutarch and Thucydides as sources for Pericles
  • Construct OCR essays on Athenian democracy and empire

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Ekklesia: The Athenian citizen assembly, meeting on the Pnyx; the sovereign body — all male citizens over 18 could attend, speak and vote.
Boule: The council of 500 (50 from each of the ten tribes), prepared the assembly's agenda and administered the state; membership by lot for a year.
Heliaia: The people's jury-courts (dikasteria); large juries (up to 501) of citizen volunteers paid from c. 451 BC (2 obols, later 3).
Ostracism: Annual vote to exile for ten years any citizen deemed dangerous to the democracy (needed 6,000 quorum); used against Themistocles (471) and Cimon (461).
Phoros: The tribute paid by Delian League members, assessed by Aristides as 460 talents; the financial base of Athens' navy and building programme.
Misthophoria: Payment for public service (jury service, later office-holding) — Pericles' reform that opened democracy to the poor (thetes).
Kleroterion: The stone allotment machine that randomised selection to office and juries — democracy by lot.
Pericles: Athenian statesman (c. 495-429 BC), dominant from c. 461-429; strategos re-elected annually; Plutarch's subject.

Athenian democracy: institutions

The institutions. Athenian democracy rested on three bodies: the ekklesia (assembly — the sovereign decision-making body, ~40 meetings a year, quorum 6,000 for sensitive votes); the boule (council of 500 — agenda-setting and administration, by lot, 50 per tribe, rotated through the pyrtaneis); and the heliaia (jury-courts — up to 501 jurors, paid 2 obols from c. 451, 3 later). Magistracies were filled by lot (the kleroterion) except the strategoi (generals), elected annually and re-electable — Pericles held the generalship continuously. Pericles' reforms: jury pay (misthophoria) opened the courts to thetes (the poorest class), radicalising participation.

Radical nature and limits. Direct democracy: citizens voted laws personally, no representatives. Limits: citizenship restricted by Pericles' citizenship law (451/450 BC — both parents Athenian, excluding ~half the population from the democracy: women, metics, slaves had no role); the assembly's susceptibility to demagogues; ostracism's misuse for faction. Thucydides' Pericles (funeral oration) calls Athens 'the school of Hellas' — the standard pro-democracy quotation; the Old Oligarch (Pseudo-Xenophon) supplies the hostile case (the demos rules in its own interest).

Empire: the Delian League to Athenian hegemony

League to empire. Founded 478 BC (naval defence against Persia), the Delian League's members paid phoros tribute or supplied ships. Athens' leadership hardened: Naxos' secession crushed (c. 470); the treasury moved to Athens (454 BC); tribute became compulsory. The Peace of Callias (c. 449 BC, disputed) ended hostilities with Persia, but Athens kept the tribute — the empire's money now bought Pericles' building programme.

The empire's instruments. Cleruchies (Athenian settlements on allies' land), garrisons, the tribute assessment (460 talents by Aristides), the federal courts pulled to Athens, and the Athens Coinage Decree (c. 450 — allies used Athenian weights, measures and silver). Kallias' peace and the empire's economics: Plutarch records the allies' resentment (the 'enslaved' League, per the rhetors); the standard essay debate: protective hegemony vs exploitation — use the tribute lists, the Coinage Decree, and the revolts (Naxos, Thasos 465) as evidence both ways.

Pericles: leadership and the building programme

Leadership (461-429 BC). Pericles dominated Athens for three decades — elected strategos annually, never a tyrant but 'first citizen' (Thucydides). His ascendancy: the Ephialtic reforms (461 — stripped the Areopagus of powers, radicalised democracy); ostracising rivals (Cimon 461, Thucydides son of Melesias 443); his marriage-alliance politics (Aspasia's salon, per Plutarch). Thucydides' assessment: Athens under Pericles 'in name a democracy, in fact rule by the first man' — the standard evaluative quotation.

The building programme (447-429 BC). Funded by the phoros: the Parthenon (447-432, Iktinos and Kallikrates, Pheidias' sculptures), the Propylaia, the Temple of Athena Nike, the Hephaisteion. Plutarch's Life defends the programme (the money was for war — spending it on beauty and the gods was legitimate; the allies' resentment is the counter-case). The programme's significance: Periclean Athens' self-image (the imperial capital of Hellas); employment (the poor benefited); the arts' golden age (tragedy: Sophocles, Euripides).

Plutarch and Thucydides as sources. Plutarch (c. AD 46-120, writing 500 years later): biographical, moralising, drawn from earlier writers (Stesimbrotus, Ion of Chios); his Life of Pericles preserves anecdotes (Aspasia, the ostracisms, the building programme's defence) but is not a political history — reliability limited by date and moral purpose. Thucydides (c. 460-400): contemporary, exiled general, analytical; his Pericles (Books 1-2) is admiring but written with hindsight and against the post-Periclean demagogues — his praise of Pericles serves his thesis (Athens failed when it lost him). OCR essays weigh both: 'Plutarch preserves the anecdotes, Thucydides the political analysis — neither is neutral'.

Essay technique: democracy and empire

'How democratic was Athenian democracy?' FOR: direct participation (ekklesia, heliaia), sortition, pay (misthophoria), the power of the courts (ostracism, euthynai audits). AGAINST: restricted citizenship (451/450 law), women/metics/slaves excluded, demagogue influence, the elite's informal power (Pericles' ascendancy). Judgement: radically democratic for its citizens, unrepresentative of its population — the standard balanced answer.

'Was the Athenian empire tyrannical?' FOR: tribute without consent (post-Callias), crushed revolts (Naxos, Thasos), cleruchies, the Coinage Decree, federal courts in Athens. AGAINST: Persia's continued threat (the Peace of Callias disputed), protection and stability, the League's original purpose, Athens' naval supremacy as the allies' interest. Judgement: the empire began as protection and became extraction — the 454 treasury move is the turning point; say why.

OCR method. A 30-mark essay: thesis, four developed points (institutions, empire, individuals, sources), counter-considerations, judgement with the question's wording. For source questions (Plutarch extracts), test the anecdote against Thucydides and the institutional record.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: 'Athenian democracy in the age of Pericles was radically democratic.' How far do you agree? [OCR H407 style, 30 marks]
Mark-scheme logic: AO1: institutions (ekklesia, boule, heliaia, sortition, misthophoria) + citizenship law (451/450) + Pericles' ascendancy. AO2: FOR — direct participation, paid juries, sortition's fairness, the courts' power; AGAINST — the restricted franchise (both-parents law excluded ~half the population), women/metics/slaves excluded, demagogues, Pericles' informal dominance (Thucydides: 'rule by the first man'). Judgement: radically democratic for citizens; for the polis's whole population, narrow — the phrase needs unpacking: democratic by the standards of its world, unrepresentative by modern ones.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Explain how the Athenian assembly (ekklesia) functioned. [10 marks]
Answer: The ekklesia met ~40 times a year on the Pnyx; all male citizens over 18 could attend, speak and vote; it decided war, peace, laws, magistracies' scrutiny; a 6,000 quorum for sensitive votes (ostracism, citizenship grants); the boule prepared its agenda (probouleusis) — the assembly could only vote on what the council put to it, though amendments were possible; votes by show of hands, secret ballots by pebbles for some decisions.
How far does Thucydides' Pericles give an accurate picture of Pericles' leadership? [20 marks]
Answer: AO1: Thucydides (contemporary, exiled general) presents Pericles as 'first citizen', his funeral oration as Athens' self-image, his strategy in the war. AO2: Thucydides wrote with hindsight and against the post-Periclean demagogues — his praise (Athens failed without him) serves his thesis, not neutral history; the hostile case (the Old Oligarch, Plutarch's anecdotes of Aspasia and faction) balances. Judgement: accurate for the strategy and self-image, coloured by Thucydides' admiration and agenda — weigh the source.
Assess the significance of the building programme in Periclean Athens. [30 marks]
Answer: Developed points: (1) self-image — the Parthenon and Propylaia made Athens the imperial capital (Pericles' own claim, per Plutarch); (2) economics — phoros-funded, employment for the poor; (3) the arts — Pheidias' sculpture, the tragic stage's golden age; (4) politics — the programme as Pericles' prestige. Counter-considerations: the allies' resentment (tribute spent on Athens' buildings); the Peace of Callias' disputed end of the Persian threat (why keep the tribute?); the standard debate: legitimate spending vs imperial extraction. Judgement: the programme was both Athens' glory and the empire's provocation — the treasury's move to Athens (454) explains why allies saw it as their money spent on Athens.
Explain the role of the boule in Athenian democracy. [10 marks]
Answer: The council of 500 (50 per tribe), chosen by lot for a year; prepared the assembly's agenda (probouleusis); administered the state through the pyrtaneis (50-member rotating committee, fed in the Tholos); received embassies, oversaw magistrates, managed finances and fleets; its members underwent dokimasia (scrutiny) and euthynai (audit on exit) — the boule's year of service made administration democratic (rotation, sortition) while keeping the assembly sovereign.
'The Delian League became an Athenian empire by 454 BC.' How far do you agree? [30 marks]
Answer: FOR: Naxos crushed (c. 470) and Thasos (465) — secession punished; the treasury moved to Athens (454) — the League's money under Athenian control; tribute (phoros, 460 talents) became compulsory; cleruchies and garrisons installed. AGAINST: the League's purpose (Persian threat) remained until the disputed Peace of Callias (449); Athens' navy served the allies' security; the League's formal structure persisted (synods, phoros assessments). Judgement: by 454 the League was an empire in substance (treasury, crushed revolts, tribute) though not yet in name — the turning point is the treasury move; the Peace of Callias removed the last justification, making the empire explicit.
