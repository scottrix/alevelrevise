Alexander the Great

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level OCR

Alexander's campaigns, leadership and deification: Granicus to Gaugamela, the relationship with his Macedonians, and Plutarch, Arrian and Curtius' accounts, with OCR H407 essay technique.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Trace Alexander's campaigns from Granicus (334 BC) to Gaugamela (331 BC) and beyond
  • Analyse Alexander's military leadership and tactics
  • Evaluate Alexander's relationship with his Macedonians and the mutinies
  • Assess Alexander's deification and self-presentation
  • Evaluate the sources (Plutarch, Arrian, Curtius) and their reliability

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Granicus (334 BC): Alexander's first major victory over the Persians in Asia Minor, against a satrapal army under Memnon and Arsames.
Issus (333 BC): Alexander's victory over Darius III in person, in a narrow coastal plain; Darius fled, his family captured.
Gaugamela (331 BC): The decisive victory over Darius III near Arbela; Alexander's oblique attack broke the Persian line.
Companion cavalry: The hetairoi — Alexander's elite cavalry, the hammer of his battles; the somatophylakes were his bodyguards.
Phalanx: The Macedonian infantry formation with the sarissa (5-6m pike); the anvil to the Companion cavalry's hammer.
Proskynesis: The Persian ritual prostration before the Great King; Alexander's attempt to impose it (327 BC) — Callisthenes' resistance and death.
Callisthenes: Aristotle's grand-nephew, Alexander's official historian; resisted proskynesis (327 BC), executed in the Pages' Conspiracy.
Hephaestion: Alexander's closest friend and second-in-command; died 324 BC — Alexander's grief extreme (Plutarch).

The campaigns: Granicus, Issus, Gaugamela

Granicus (334 BC). Alexander crossed the Hellespont (c. 35,000 Macedonians and allies) and defeated the satrapal army under Memnon and Arsames at the Granicus river — the Companion cavalry's frontal charge across the river broke the Persian line; Alexander fought in the front line (his helmet split, per Plutarch). The victory opened Asia Minor; the Greek cities' liberation (the panhellenic claim).

Issus (333 BC). Darius III met Alexander in person in the narrow coastal plain near Issus — the terrain neutralised Persian numbers; Alexander's oblique attack (the Companion wedge at the weakened centre) broke the line; Darius fled (his mother, wife and children captured — treated honourably, the standard propaganda point). The victory opened the Levant and Egypt (the oracle of Siwah, 331 — Alexander declared son of Ammon).

Gaugamela (331 BC). The decisive battle: Darius' massive army on open ground (chosen by the Persians); Alexander's oblique advance (the 'Hammer and Anvil' — the phalanx held, the Companion cavalry's wedge found the gap); Darius fled again; Babylon and the Persian capitals (Susa, Persepolis — burned 330, revenge for Athens or accident?) fell. The wars' end: Darius murdered by Bessus (330) — Alexander's pursuit and avenging of Darius (the legitimate successor claim).

Leadership: the king in battle and the mutinies

Military leadership. Alexander fought in the front line (Granicus' split helmet, the siege of Malli's arrow — 325 BC, near-fatal wound); his tactics: the oblique advance, the Companion wedge, the phalanx's discipline; the sieges (Halicarnassus, Tyre's mole — 332, the seven-month siege). His leadership: sharing hardship (the march through Gedrosia's desert — though the cost was extreme), promoting Persians (the epigonoi — 30,000 Persian youths trained in Macedonian style), the mass wedding at Susa (324 — Macedonians married Persians).

The mutinies. Alexander's relationship with his Macedonians soured: the Hyphasis mutiny (326 BC — the army refused to cross the Ganges; Alexander sulked in his tent, then turned back); the Opis mutiny (324 BC — the army mutinied at the Persian promotions; Alexander's speech (Arrian), the reconciliation banquet); the purges (Philotas and Parmenion, 330 — the conspiracy; Cleitus, 328 — killed by Alexander in a drunken quarrel). The standard essay debate: Alexander the great commander vs Alexander the autocrat — the mutinies and purges as evidence.

Deification and self-presentation

Divine sonship. Siwah's oracle (331 — son of Ammon); the panhellenic claim (Zeus-Ammon); the deification push: proskynesis (327 — the Persian prostration imposed on Macedonians; Callisthenes' resistance and execution); the divine honours (the cities' cults); the Gedrosian march's costs. Self-presentation: Homer's model (Achilles — Alexander slept with the Iliad, per Plutarch); the Homeric kingship (the leader in front); Herakles' lineage (the Argead claim). The standard essay: Alexander's deification — genuine belief or propaganda? The sources disagree (Arrian admiring, Curtius hostile).

The sources. Plutarch (c. AD 100): biographical, moralising — his Life preserves the anecdotes (Bucephalus, the Gordian knot, Cleitus' murder, the grief for Hephaestion); reliability limited by date (400 years) and moral purpose. Arrian (c. AD 130-150): wrote from Ptolemy and Aristobulus (eyewitnesses) — the most military-detailed; admiring (Alexander 'philitatos' — the divine call), but the admiration colours the account. Curtius Rufus (1st century AD): hostile — the corruption theme (the king corrupted by success); his sources unknown. Reliability: all three wrote centuries later; the eyewitness tradition (Ptolemy, Aristobulus, Callisthenes) survives only in fragments; the standard OCR pairing: 'Arrian for the battles, Curtius for the corruption, Plutarch for the anecdotes — none is neutral'.

Essay technique

'Alexander was a great military commander.' FOR: Granicus, Issus, Gaugamela's tactics (the oblique advance, the wedge); the sieges (Tyre); the front-line leadership (Malli); the march to the Indus. AGAINST: the mutinies (Hyphasis, Opis) and purges (Philotas, Cleitus) — the command's human cost; the Gedrosian march's losses; the empire's fragility after his death. Judgement: the campaigns were brilliant — the oblique tactics and personal leadership unmatched; but 'great' must weigh the cost (the army's suffering, the purges) — the standard balanced answer.

'Alexander's deification was political propaganda.' FOR: Siwah's convenient timing, proskynesis' imposition (Macedonian resistance), the divine honours' political use. AGAINST: Alexander's genuine belief (his mother's cults, the Iliad's model); the Macedonian acceptance of divine kingship. Judgement: both — genuine belief deployed politically; say why the distinction matters.

OCR method. A 30-mark essay: thesis, four developed points with named battles/sources, counter-considerations, judgement. For source questions: use the extract's specifics (Arrian's numbers, Curtius' moralising) against the reliability analysis.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: 'Alexander's success was due to his military genius.' How far do you agree? [OCR H407 style, 30 marks]
Mark-scheme logic: AO1: Granicus, Issus, Gaugamela (the oblique advance, the Companion wedge), Tyre's siege, the front-line leadership. AO2: FOR — the tactics were innovative (the hammer-and-anvil, the oblique attack at Gaugamela); the personal leadership (the front line) inspired; AGAINST — Philip II's army (the sarissa phalanx, the Companion cavalry) was his father's creation; the officers (Parmenion) managed the fronts; the mutinies show the limits. Judgement: genius in tactics and leadership — but the instrument (Philip's army) and the context (Persia's weakness) mattered; the claim needs the father's contribution acknowledged.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Explain Alexander's tactics at Gaugamela. [10 marks]
Answer: Open ground (the Persians' choice, flat for the scythed chariots); Alexander's oblique advance (the line refused the centre, drew the Persian cavalry's wing); the Companion wedge (Alexander leading) found the gap at the Persian centre; the phalanx held the line (the sarissa's discipline); the chariots neutralised (the gaps in the Macedonian line); Darius fled — the oblique attack and the cavalry's decisive charge; the pursuit abandoned to save the centre — tactics = leadership + discipline + timing.
How far do the mutinies show Alexander's failure as a leader? [30 marks]
Answer: FOR: Hyphasis (326) — the army refused the Ganges; Alexander's sulking retreat — the campaign's end by mutiny; Opis (324) — the Persian promotions triggered mutiny; the purges (Philotas, Cleitus) — the court's fear. AGAINST: the mutinies came after 8 years and 18,000 miles — the army's exhaustion; Alexander's reconciliations (the Opis banquet, Arrian's speech) restored command; the empire held until his death. Judgement: the mutinies show the human limits — the command held but the men's loyalty was conditional; 'failure' is too strong — the standard line: Alexander's leadership was brilliant and brittle.
Assess Alexander's deification. [30 marks]
Answer: FOR (genuine): the Siwah oracle's sonship (331); the Iliad's Homeric model; the mother's cults; the divine honours accepted. FOR (political): proskynesis' imposition (327) — Callisthenes' resistance; the Persian court's etiquette; the empire's legitimacy (the Great King's role). AGAINST: the Macedonian resistance (the mutinies, Callisthenes); the Greeks' suspicion; the sources' disagreements. Judgement: genuine belief deployed politically — the standard balanced answer; say why proskynesis mattered (the crossing of Greek and Persian kingship).
Compare Arrian and Curtius as sources for Alexander. [20 marks]
Answer: AO1: Arrian (c. AD 130-150) wrote from Ptolemy and Aristobulus (eyewitnesses) — the most military-detailed, admiring; Curtius Rufus (1st century AD) — hostile, the corruption theme, sources unknown. AO2: Arrian's admiration colours the account (the divine call); Curtius' hostility (the corruption) is moralising; both wrote centuries later; Plutarch preserves the anecdotes. Judgement: Arrian for the campaigns, Curtius for the court's politics — neither neutral; OCR answers weigh the biases.
'Alexander's empire was doomed by his death.' How far do you agree? [30 marks]
Answer: FOR: the empire's fragility (the Successor Wars — 40 years of fighting); no heir (Alexander IV born posthumously); the Persians' epigonoi unresolved; the army's divisions. AGAINST: the empire's structures (the satrapies, the army) held; the Successors' kingdoms (Ptolemy, Seleucus, Antigonus) continued Alexander's work; his achievement (the Hellenistic world) endured. Judgement: the personal empire (one king) died with him — but the Hellenistic world (the kingdoms, the culture) was his legacy; say why the distinction matters.
