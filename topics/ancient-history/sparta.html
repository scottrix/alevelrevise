Sparta

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level OCR

Spartan society, the agoge, helots and the dual kingship: institutions, military culture and Xenophon's and Plutarch's accounts, with OCR H407 essay technique.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain the Spartan social structure: Spartiates, perioikoi, helots
  • Describe the agoge (education system) and its role in military society
  • Analyse the Spartan constitution: dual kingship, ephors, gerousia, assembly
  • Evaluate Xenophon's and Plutarch's accounts of Sparta (idealisation problem)
  • Assess why Sparta's military system was both strength and weakness

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Agoge: The Spartan state education: boys taken at 7 into packs, trained for obedience, endurance and war; the institution that made the Spartiate.
Helot: The enslaved population of Laconia and Messenia, bound to the land; outnumbered Spartiates several times over and controlled by annual war declarations and the krypteia.
Perioikoi: Free inhabitants of Spartan towns ('dwellers-around'); craftsmen and traders, no political rights, obliged to military service.
Dual kingship: Two hereditary kings (Agids and Eurypontids), religious, military and judicial leaders, checked by the ephors and gerousia.
Gerousia: The council of 28 elders (over 60, elected for life) plus the two kings; prepared business for the assembly and tried capital cases.
Ephors: Five annually elected magistrates; overseen the kings (oath of monthly swap), could prosecute kings, controlled the krypteia.
Krypteia: The secret service of young Spartiates who policed the helots — annual war declaration (Plutarch) institutionalised control.
Syssition: The common mess — Spartiates ate together, contributing food monthly; membership was the mark of full citizenship.

Spartan society: Spartiates, perioikoi, helots

Structure. Spartan society had three tiers: the Spartiates (full citizens, ~8,000 adult males at the peak, declining to ~1,000 by Aristotle's day), the perioikoi (free 'dwellers-around' — craftsmen and traders with no political rights, military service obligatory) and the helots (the enslaved majority of Laconia and, after the Messenian Wars, Messenia — outnumbering Spartiates perhaps 7:1). The helots farmed the kleroi (allotments) that fed each Spartiate's syssition contribution — the economic base of the Spartan citizen's freedom for war.

Control of the helots. The helots' numbers dictated the constitution's character: Sparta was a garrison state. Controls: the annual war declaration on the helots (Plutarch — institutionalised murder without pollution), the krypteia (young Spartiates' secret police), the obligatory hoplite readiness, and the systemic terror (Xenophon records the helots' willingness to die — their fear of the Spartans). The Messenian revolt (464-460) — the Third Messenian War — showed the system's fragility and Sparta's dependence on allies.

The agoge and military culture

The agoge. Boys taken at 7 into age-packs (agelai); trained in endurance (one cloak, barefoot, short rations), obedience (the eirens' discipline), theft tolerated (resourcefulness) but being CAUGHT punished — the standard paradox; the ritual theft of cheese (Plutarch); at 20 into the syssition; at 30 full citizenship (homologoi). Girls too: gymnastics (the running races, per Xenophon and Plutarch) — Sparta's women were famously different (Plutarch on their freedom, the 'thigh-flashers' joke of the comic poets).

Military culture. The syssition's messes built the phalanx's cohesion; the agoge's obedience built the discipline that won Plataea's day. Xenophon's Constitution of the Lacedaemonians idealises the system (Lycurgus' design) while admitting decline in his own day (c. 360s). The standard essay debate: Sparta's military excellence (the finest hoplites of Greece) vs its demographic fragility (declining Spartiate numbers, helot control) — the system that made Sparta strong made it rigid.

The Spartan constitution

Institutions. The dual kingship: two hereditary kings (Agids and Eurypontids) — religious leaders, military commanders (one on campaign), judges in some cases; checked by the ephors' oath (monthly swap of the mutual oath: king rules by the laws, Sparta keeps the king). The gerousia: 28 elders (over 60, elected for life by acclamation) plus the kings — prepared business, tried capital cases. The ephors: five annually elected — extensive power (prosecuting kings, the krypteia, foreign policy), the real check. The assembly (apella): Spartiates over 30 — voted (by acclamation, no debate) on the gerousia's proposals.

Nature. The Spartan constitution (the 'Great Rhetra', per Plutarch/tyrtaios? — Tyrtaios' poems) was a mixed constitution (kingly, aristocratic, democratic elements) — admired by Aristotle and the philosophers as stable. The reality: oligarchic dominance (gerousia + ephors) with a passive assembly. Xenophon idealises Lycurgus' design; the standard essay: how 'Lycurgan' was Sparta really — the reforms attributed to a legendary lawgiver, the institutions evolving over time.

Sources and essay technique

Xenophon (c. 430-354 BC). An Athenian aristocrat who admired Sparta (exiled, lived in Scillus near Olympia); his Constitution of the Lacedaemonians is the fullest ancient account of the agoge and syssition — idealising (Lycurgus' divine design) but contemporary-adjacent and detailed; his Hellenica covers Spartan hegemony (404-362). Reliability: idealisation (Sparta as the model constitution), but his detail is unmatched — the standard pairing with Plutarch.

Plutarch (c. AD 46-120). Wrote 500+ years later; his Lycurgus and Agis/Cleomenes preserve the traditions (the war declaration, the krypteia, the cheese theft) from earlier writers (Aristotle, Sosibius) — biographical and moralising; reliability limited by date. Tyrtaios (7th century BC, elegies) — the earliest poetic evidence (the messes, the phalanx's ethos, the Messenian Wars).

Essay technique. A 30-mark essay: thesis, four developed points (society, agoge, constitution, sources), counter-considerations, judgement. Standard questions: 'How far was Sparta's strength military?' (FOR: the phalanx, the agoge, Plataea; AGAINST: the helot fragility, the declining Spartiate numbers, the rigidity — Leuctra 371 broke the myth); 'How reliable are Xenophon and Plutarch for Spartan society?' (weigh idealisation against detail); 'Was the Spartan constitution democratic?' (the apella's acclamation vs the ephors' power — oligarchic reality, mixed theory).

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: 'Spartan society was organised for war above all else.' How far do you agree? [OCR H407 style, 30 marks]
Mark-scheme logic: AO1: agoge (age-packs, endurance, obedience), syssition, helot economy (kleroi), dual kingship, ephors, the war declaration and krypteia. AO2: FOR — the whole system aimed at hoplite readiness: the agoge's endurance, the messes' cohesion, the helots' control (the annual war, the krypteia); AGAINST — the system also produced civil stability (the mixed constitution, the gerousia), religious life (the festivals, the kings' religious role), and the girls' gymnastics served reproduction as much as war. Judgement: war-readiness was the organising principle — but it was the system's CONSTRAINT (the helot terror) as much as its goal; the standard line: Sparta's military excellence was inseparable from its social rigidity.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Explain the role of the ephors in Sparta. [10 marks]
Answer: Five annually elected magistrates: overseen the kings (the monthly mutual oath — king rules by the laws); could prosecute kings in the gerousia; controlled the krypteia and the helot system; managed foreign policy (receiving embassies); convened the gerousia and apella. Their power made them the constitution's real check — the standard evaluative point: annually elected commoners checking hereditary kings.
How far was the agoge responsible for Sparta's military excellence? [30 marks]
Answer: FOR: the agoge built the phalanx's cohesion (age-packs to syssition), endurance (one cloak, short rations), obedience (the eirens' discipline) — Plataea's discipline. AGAINST: the helot economy (kleroi feeding the syssitia) freed the Spartiate for war — economic, not educational; the messes' social function mattered as much as the training; other poleis' hoplites fought well without the agoge (Marathon). Judgement: the agoge gave Sparta its edge (discipline and cohesion) — but the system's base was economic (helot labour) and social (the messes); the agoge was the visible institution, not the whole cause.
Assess Xenophon's reliability as a source for Spartan society. [20 marks]
Answer: AO1 (value): Xenophon (c. 430-354 BC) lived near Sparta, admired the system; his Constitution of the Lacedaemonians details the agoge, syssition, kingship — the fullest account; his Hellenica covers Spartan hegemony. AO2 (problems): idealisation (Lycurgus' divine design), his aristocratic admiration, his admission of decline (c. 360s) colours the account; he omits the helots' experience. Judgement: valuable for detail and contemporary-adjacent witness, coloured by admiration — pair with Plutarch's traditions and Tyrtaios' poetry.
Explain how Sparta controlled its helot population. [10 marks]
Answer: The annual war declaration (Plutarch) — institutionalised killing without religious pollution; the krypteia — young Spartiates' secret police; the obligatory hoplite readiness (the garrison state); the kleros system binding helots to the land; terror (Xenophon's testimony); the Messenian revolts' suppression. The control shaped the constitution — the helot numbers (7:1) dictated Sparta's militarisation.
'The Spartan constitution was oligarchic, not democratic.' How far do you agree? [30 marks]
Answer: FOR: the gerousia (28 life-elected elders) and ephors dominated; the apella voted by acclamation without debate; Spartiates over 30 only — the majority (helots, perioikoi, women) had no role; the kings' hereditary power. AGAINST: the apella was the sovereign body in theory (voting on the rhetra's proposals); the ephors were annually elected commoners; the mixed constitution's democratic element was real (Aristotle's classification). Judgement: oligarchic in practice (the gerousia's dominance, acclamation votes), mixed in theory — the Spartan 'democracy' was narrower than Athens' by every measure; say why the acclamation rule mattered (no debate = no persuasion).
