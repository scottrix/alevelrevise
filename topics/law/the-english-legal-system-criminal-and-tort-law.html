LW1: The English Legal System, Criminal and Tort Law

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level All Boards (AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC, CCEA) AQA

Judicial precedent, statutory interpretation, criminal liability (Actus Reus & Mens Rea), tort of negligence (Duty, Breach, Damage).

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Stare Decisis: The legal principle of adhering to established precedent in subsequent similar cases.
Mens Rea: The guilty mind or fault element required for criminal liability (intention or subjective recklessness).
Actus Reus: The guilty act, omission or prohibited result; liability requires a voluntary act except where a duty to act arises (contract, relationship, or Dangerous Driving creating danger).
Caparo Three-Part Test: Duty of care arises where harm was reasonably foreseeable, there was proximity of relationship, and imposing a duty is fair, just and reasonable (Caparo v Dickman).
Statutory Interpretation (Purposive Approach): Judges seek Parliament's legislative purpose using intrinsic aids (long title, schedules) and extrinsic aids (Hansard under Pepper v Hart), favouring EU- and ECHR-compatible readings.
Aims of Sentencing: Retribution, deterrence (individual and general), rehabilitation, protection of the public and reparation, codified in s.142 Criminal Justice Act 2003.

🔍 Key Principles & Specification Requirements

  • Statutory interpretation rules: Literal rule, Golden rule, Mischief rule (Heydon's Case), Purposive approach.
  • Criminal liability: Actus Reus (conduct/result) + Mens Rea (intention/recklessness) without valid defence = Crime.
  • Negligence elements: 1. Duty of care (Caparo / Robinson test). 2. Breach (Blyth reasonable person standard). 3. Damage caused (Barnett 'but for' test) and not too remote (Wagon Mound).

💡 Worked Example Question

Exam-Style Question

Question:

Explain the 'but for' test of causation in tort law with reference to Barnett v Chelsea & Kensington Hospital.

Model Solution & Mark Scheme:

The 'but for' test establishes factual causation: but for the defendant's negligent breach of duty, would the claimant have suffered the harm? In Barnett, hospital staff negligently sent a sick patient home without examination. The patient died of arsenic poisoning. Evidence showed he would have died even with prompt medical treatment; hence negligence was not the factual cause.

Q3: Evaluate the effectiveness of the Caparo three-part test in establishing a duty of care in negligence.

Show Model Answer

Answer: Caparo provides a flexible, incremental framework that allows novel duties (such as against psychiatrists for suicide in White v Chief Constable context debates) to be assessed case by case. However, evaluation must note Robinson (2018) reasserted the primacy of established categories over Caparo's 'fair, just and reasonable' limb, which critics argue is an unpredictable judicial policy judgment; the test therefore offers consistency in settled areas but uncertainty at its margins.

Q4: Explain how the system of judicial precedent operates, including the role of distinguishing, and evaluate whether it promotes certainty.

Show Model Answer

Answer: Stare decisis binds lower courts to the ratio decidendi of higher courts; binding precedent can be avoided by distinguishing (finding material differences of fact, as in Balfour v Craig), overruling, or the 1966 Practice Statement permitting the Supreme Court to depart from its own previous decisions. Evaluation: certainty and equality are major strengths, yet rigidity risk is reduced by these escape devices, though heavy reliance on distinguishing can make the law voluminous and hard to research; on balance precedent promotes certainty while retaining controlled flexibility.
