Remedies and Sentencing

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA, OCR, WJEC/Eduqas

Civil remedies — damages (general, special, aggravated, exemplary), injunctions, specific performance and rescission — plus criminal sentencing: the five purposes under CJA 2003 s.142, tariffs, aggravating and mitigating factors, custodial and community orders for AQA 3.1.1/3.1.2, OCR H418 Component 1 and WJEC/Eduqas Component 1.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Distinguish the types of damages — general, special, aggravated, exemplary and nominal — and apply them to claim scenarios (AQA 3.1.1; OCR H418 Component 1; WJEC/Eduqas Component 1)
  • Explain injunctions (prohibitory and mandatory; interim and final) and the contempt sanction, including the American Cyanamid test for interim injunctions
  • Explain when specific performance and rescission are available, including the bars to rescission (affirmation, lapse of time, restitutio in integrum, third-party rights)
  • State the five purposes of sentencing under the Criminal Justice Act 2003, s.142 and explain how judges weigh competing aims, including the welfare principle for under-18s (CYPA 1933, s.44)
  • Explain how the minimum term (tariff) is set for murder — the mandatory life sentence, Schedule 21 CJA 2003 starting points and whole life orders — and the role of the Sentencing Council
  • Apply statutory and guideline aggravating and mitigating factors, including the reduction for a guilty plea (Sentencing Act 2020, s.73) and aggravation for hostility (CJA 2003, ss.145-146)
  • Distinguish custodial, community, fine and discharge sentences — their thresholds, limits and relative effectiveness — and evaluate the aims of sentencing in problem and essay answers

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Damages: The primary common-law remedy: a sum of money awarded to the successful claimant, normally compensatory — restoring the claimant to the position as if the wrong had not happened (Livingstone v Rawyards Coal Co (1880)).
General damages: Non-pecuniary losses assessed by the court because they cannot be precisely calculated — pain, suffering and loss of amenity.
Special damages: Pecuniary losses provable and calculable up to the date of trial — lost earnings, medical expenses, damaged property.
Aggravated damages: A compensatory uplift awarded where the defendant's injurious, humiliating or high-handed conduct aggravated the claimant's loss (e.g. Thompson v Commissioner of Police of the Metropolis (1997)).
Exemplary (punitive) damages: Damages awarded to punish rather than compensate, confined by Rookes v Barnard (1964) to oppressive, arbitrary or unconstitutional action by government servants, and profit made from the wrong (AB v South West Water Services (1993)).
Nominal damages: A small symbolic sum where the claimant's right was infringed but no loss was suffered (e.g. trespass without damage).
Injunction: An equitable order requiring a party to do (mandatory) or stop (prohibitory) something; interim (until trial) or final; breach is a contempt of court.
Specific performance: An equitable order compelling performance of contractual obligations, granted only where damages are inadequate (Beswick v Beswick (1968)) and refused for personal-service or continuously supervised contracts (Co-operative Insurance Society v Argyll Stores (1998)).
Rescission: An equitable remedy setting a voidable contract aside from the beginning — available for misrepresentation, duress, undue influence or mistake — subject to bars: affirmation, lapse of time, impossibility of restitutio in integrum and third-party rights.
Purposes of sentencing (CJA 2003, s.142): For adults: the punishment of offenders (retribution), the reduction of crime (deterrence), the reform and rehabilitation of offenders, the protection of the public, and the making of reparation by offenders.
Tariff (minimum term): The minimum term a life-sentence prisoner must serve before the Parole Board may consider release; for murder, set by the judge from the Schedule 21 CJA 2003 starting points (15 years standard; 30 years or whole life order for the most serious).
Aggravating and mitigating factors: Factors increasing or reducing seriousness: statutory (CJA 2003, ss.143-146 — previous convictions, hostility, bail) and guideline factors; the reduction for a guilty plea is governed by the Sentencing Act 2020, s.73.

Civil remedies: damages (AQA 3.1.1; OCR H418 Component 1; WJEC/Eduqas Component 1)

Damages are the primary common-law remedy: a sum of money awarded to the successful claimant. The compensatory principle is restorative — put the claimant in the position he would have been in had the wrong not been committed (Livingstone v Rawyards Coal Co (1880)). The types matter for the exam because each answers a different loss:

  • General damages — non-pecuniary losses that cannot be precisely calculated and are assessed by the judge: pain, suffering and loss of amenity (PSLA), plus pre-trial non-pecuniary loss. Future losses are also assessed.
  • Special damages — pecuniary losses provable and calculable up to trial: lost earnings, medical expenses, cost of repairing damaged property.
  • Aggravated damages — a compensatory uplift where the defendant's injurious, humiliating or high-handed conduct increased the claimant's distress: Thompson v Commissioner of Police of the Metropolis (1997) (false imprisonment of a suspected thief; aggravated award for the humiliating strip search).
  • Exemplary (punitive) damages — awarded to punish, not compensate, and rare: only two categories survive Rookes v Barnard (1964) — (1) oppressive, arbitrary or unconstitutional action by government servants (confirmed in Broome v Cassell & Co (1972)); (2) profit made from the wrong (AB v South West Water Services (1993)).
  • Nominal damages — the claimant's right was infringed but no loss was suffered (e.g. trespass without damage); a small sum vindicating the right.
  • Contemptuous damages — liability is established but the court has no sympathy with the claimant: a token award, sometimes as little as one penny.

Other points: remoteness of loss follows the negligence rules (the Wagon Mound test); interim payments may be made before trial; structured settlements spread large awards; damages under the Human Rights Act 1998, s.8 are 'just and appropriate' rather than strictly compensatory.

Quick recall — the damages ladder
  • Compensatory: general (non-pecuniary, assessed) v special (pecuniary, proven to trial).
  • Aggravated: injurious/high-handed conduct (Thompson).
  • Exemplary: punish — Rookes v Barnard categories (government oppression; profit from wrong; AB v South West Water).
  • Nominal: right breached, no loss. Contemptuous: right breached, no sympathy.

Equitable remedies: injunctions, specific performance and rescission (AQA 3.1.1)

Equitable remedies are discretionary — the court applies the equitable maxims: he who seeks equity must come with clean hands; delay defeats equity (laches). They are flexible and forward-looking, unlike the fixed, backward-looking money award of damages.

Injunctions — orders requiring a party to act or refrain from acting:

  • Prohibitory (restrain the defendant from doing something) and mandatory (compel the defendant to undo something, e.g. remove an obstruction causing a nuisance).
  • Interim (interlocutory) — until trial; the test is American Cyanamid Co v Ethicon Ltd (1975): (1) is there a serious question to be tried, (2) does the balance of convenience favour granting the injunction, (3) preserve the status quo until trial.
  • Final (perpetual) — granted after trial where the claimant establishes the right.
  • Breach is a contempt of court — punishable by fine or imprisonment (Contempt of Court Act 1981); common uses: nuisance, harassment (Protection from Harassment Act 1997, s.3), domestic abuse (Family Law Act 1996, Part IV non-molestation orders), breach of confidence.

Specific performance — an equitable order compelling performance of contractual obligations. It is granted only where damages are inadequate: Beswick v Beswick (1968) (pension undertaking to a widow; order granted); Sky Petroleum v VIP Petroleum (1974) (rare fuel supply unavailable elsewhere). It is refused for contracts of personal service or requiring continuous supervision: Co-operative Insurance Society v Argyll Stores (1998); and against minors, or where the claimant has acted unconscionably (clean hands).

Rescission — sets a voidable contract aside from the beginning (ab initio); both parties are restored to their pre-contract positions. Grounds: misrepresentation (fraudulent, negligent or innocent), duress, undue influence, non-disclosure in contracts of the utmost good faith, and common mistake. Bars to rescission:

  • Affirmation — carrying on with the contract after discovering the vitiating factor.
  • Lapse of time — undue delay defeats equity: Leaf v International Galleries (1950) (misdescription of a painting discovered five years later).
  • Restitutio in integrum impossible — the goods or situation cannot be restored (substantial consumption or change).
  • Third-party rights — a bona fide third party has acquired an interest in the property.

Exam link: problem questions usually pair damages with an injunction or a contract remedy; state the remedy, its requirements and its limits, then argue which the claimant would get and why.

Aims of sentencing and the tariff (CJA 2003 s.142; AQA 3.1.2)

The Criminal Justice Act 2003, s.142(1) sets the purposes of sentencing adults: (a) the punishment of offenders (retribution); (b) the reduction of crime (deterrence — individual and general); (c) the reform and rehabilitation of offenders; (d) the protection of the public; and (e) the making of reparation by offenders (compensation orders; restorative justice).

  • Retribution — punishment proportionate to the offence and the offender's culpability; the basis of the tariff system and the 'just deserts' theory.
  • Deterrence — individual (discourage the offender) and general (discourage others); the evidence for general deterrence is weak — reoffending rates are high despite severe penalties.
  • Rehabilitation — changing the offender (drug and alcohol treatment, programmes, education); most effective in the community, undermined by prison.
  • Protection of the public — incapacitation (custody), plus restrictions (driving bans, restraining orders).
  • Reparation — compensation to the victim and apologies/restorative justice conferences.

For under-18s, s.142 does not apply — the welfare of the child is paramount (Children and Young Persons Act 1933, s.44), reflected in referral orders and youth rehabilitation orders.

The Sentencing Council (established by the Coroners and Justice Act 2009) issues guidelines the courts must follow unless contrary to the interests of justice (CJA 2003, s.125): categories of culpability and harm produce a range and a starting point, promoting consistency across courts.

The tariff for murder. Murder carries a mandatory life sentence (Murder (Abolition of Death Penalty) Act 1965). The judge sets the minimum term from the Schedule 21 CJA 2003 starting points: 15 years for standard adult murder; 30 years for particularly high seriousness (e.g. use of a firearm, murder of a police or prison officer in the course of duty); 25 years where a knife or other weapon was taken to the scene; and a whole life order (CJA 2003, s.269 with Sch.21) for the most exceptional cases — two or more murders, sexual or sadistic murder of a child, murder to advance a political, religious or ideological cause, or murder by a previously convicted murderer. Compatibility was upheld in Hutchinson v United Kingdom (2017). The Parole Board may release after the tariff; recall follows any breach of the licence.

Quick recall — the aims and the tariff
  • s.142: punishment (retribution); reduction of crime (deterrence); reform and rehabilitation; protection of the public; reparation.
  • Youths: welfare paramount (CYPA 1933, s.44).
  • Sentencing Council: guidelines must be followed (CJA 2003, s.125); culpability + harm.
  • Murder: mandatory life (1965 Act); Sch.21 starting points — 15/25/30 years; whole life order (Hutchinson v UK); Parole Board release; licence.

Aggravating and mitigating factors (CJA 2003 s.143; AQA 3.1.2)

Seriousness is assessed under the Criminal Justice Act 2003, s.143 by (1) the offender's culpability and (2) the harm caused, or risked and foreseeable; previous convictions are relevant (s.143(2)).

Statutory aggravating factors:

  • Offence racially or religiously aggravated — CJA 2003, s.145 (maximum penalties increased; guideline uplifts).
  • Aggravation by hostility to disability, sexual orientation or transgender identity — CJA 2003, s.146.
  • Offence committed while on bail — s.143(2).
  • Previous convictions, particularly similar ones.

Guideline aggravating factors (Sentencing Council): premeditation, use of a weapon, vulnerability of the victim, group action, abuse of trust or position, offence committed in the victim's home, impact on children, failure to respond to previous warnings, offence committed under the influence of alcohol or drugs.

Mitigating factors:

  • Guilty plea — the court must state the reduction (Sentencing Act 2020, s.73): the guideline allows up to one-third off for a plea at the first opportunity, sliding down towards one-tenth at the door of the court — rewards efficiency and remorse.
  • Assistance to the prosecution (informing on others) may reduce the sentence.
  • Age and youth; ill health or disability; mental illness or psychological disorder; genuine remorse; previous good character; caring responsibilities; provocation or circumstances beyond the offender's control.

Before sentence the court receives a pre-sentence report (probation), medical or psychiatric reports and victim personal statements; the defence delivers a plea in mitigation. Exam link: apply the factors to the facts in the order culpability, harm, aggravation, mitigation, and link each factor to the aims it serves — aggravation to retribution, a guilty plea and treatment requirements to rehabilitation.

Types of sentence — custodial, community, fines and discharges (AQA 3.1.2; OCR H418 Component 1)

Custodial sentences. The threshold: a court must not pass a custodial sentence unless the offence was so serious that neither a fine alone nor a community order can be justified (Sentencing Act 2020, s.230; formerly CJA 2003, s.152). Types:

  • Mandatory life sentence for murder (minimum term from Sch.21 CJA 2003 — above); discretionary life for other serious offences (manslaughter, rape).
  • Fixed-term imprisonment — the court sets the term up to the statutory maximum; the offender is released at the halfway point on licence, which runs to the end of the sentence; extended sentences for dangerous offenders add a licence period.
  • Suspended sentence — a custodial term of 6 months to 2 years may be suspended for up to 2 years (Sentencing Act 2020, s.189); breach of the requirements activates the custody unless it would be unjust.

Effectiveness: custody delivers retribution and incapacitation, but official figures show well over half of those serving short custodial sentences reoffend within a year — prison is poor at rehabilitation and expensive.

Community orders — one or more requirements (Sentencing Act 2020; formerly CJA 2003, s.177): unpaid work (40-300 hours), supervision, accredited programmes, curfew with electronic monitoring (up to 16 hours a day), exclusion from places, prohibited activities, drug rehabilitation requirement, alcohol treatment requirement, mental health treatment requirement, attendance centre (under 25s) and residence. Breach returns the offender to court for stricter requirements or custody. Effectiveness: lower reoffending and lower cost than custody for comparable offenders, but perceived as 'soft'.

Fines — the standard scale (levels 1-5: maxima from £200 to £5,000 in the magistrates' court; unlimited for either-way and indictable offences); means-tested against weekly income; enforcement by attachment of earnings, bailiffs and, ultimately, default.

Discharges — conditional (up to 3 years — Sentencing Act 2020, s.79; no punishment unless a further offence within the period, when both offences are resentenced) and absolute (no punishment — trivial cases or where the defendant is technically criminal but blameless). Other orders: compensation orders, restraining orders, driving disqualification, confiscation of assets.

Exam technique: AQA 25-mark essays ('Prison does not work' — evaluate) reward the aims tension (retribution and protection v rehabilitation), the statistics (short-sentence reoffending v community-order outcomes), and a judgement: custody is necessary for serious and dangerous offenders, but community orders with treatment requirements better serve rehabilitation for the rest. Scenario questions reward applying the custodial threshold, the factors (s.143), the guilty-plea reduction (Sentencing Act 2020, s.73) and the choice between custody, a suspended sentence and a community order.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: Holli was seriously injured when Kane drove carelessly through a red light. Kane has been convicted. (a) Advise Holli on the types of damages she may claim. (b) Kane has two previous convictions for driving while disqualified, committed this offence while on bail, and pleaded guilty only on the day of trial; he also suffers severe depression and cares for his elderly mother. Explain the sentence the judge may impose, including the aggravating and mitigating factors. [AQA 7162 Paper 1 style problem question, 25 marks]
Mark-scheme logic: The answer must hit: <strong>(a) Damages (AO1/AO2)</strong> — compensatory principle (Livingstone v Rawyards Coal Co); <strong>special damages</strong> — pecuniary losses provable to trial (lost earnings, medical expenses); <strong>general damages</strong> — pain, suffering and loss of amenity assessed by the judge; <strong>aggravated damages</strong> only if Kane's conduct was injurious or humiliating (unlikely for careless driving — note this); exemplary damages are unavailable — Rookes v Barnard categories not engaged. <strong>(b) Sentencing (AO1/AO2)</strong> — purposes under CJA 2003, s.142: punishment, reduction of crime (deterrence), reform and rehabilitation, protection of the public, reparation. <strong>Aggravating</strong> — two previous convictions for similar offences (s.143(2)); offence committed while on bail (s.143(2)); culpability (careless driving through a red light) and harm (serious injury) under s.143. <strong>Mitigating</strong> — guilty plea only at trial (reduction minimal under Sentencing Act 2020, s.73 — up to one-third at first opportunity, less here); severe depression (mental illness); caring responsibilities. <strong>Sentence</strong> — apply the custodial threshold (Sentencing Act 2020, s.230): the offending is serious but the strong mitigation may justify a suspended sentence or a community order with requirements (unpaid work, supervision, mental health treatment); custody with extended licence if the risk of harm justifies it. <strong>AO3</strong> — weigh the aims: previous convictions and bail point to protection and retribution; depression and care responsibilities point to rehabilitation; conclude with a reasoned, definite sentence recommendation. A* answers apply each statutory section to the facts in one sentence and state the likely outcome, not 'it depends'.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Explain the different types of damages available in the civil courts. Refer to Rookes v Barnard. [AQA Paper 1, 10 marks]
Answer: AO1: Damages are the primary common-law remedy, normally compensatory — restoring the claimant to the position as if the wrong had not happened (Livingstone v Rawyards Coal Co (1880)). Types: <strong>general damages</strong> — non-pecuniary, assessed by the court (pain, suffering, loss of amenity); <strong>special damages</strong> — pecuniary losses provable to trial (lost earnings, medical expenses); <strong>aggravated damages</strong> — an uplift for injurious, humiliating or high-handed conduct (Thompson v Commissioner of Police of the Metropolis (1997)); <strong>exemplary (punitive) damages</strong> — awarded to punish, not compensate, confined by <strong>Rookes v Barnard (1964)</strong> to two categories: oppressive, arbitrary or unconstitutional action by government servants (confirmed in Broome v Cassell & Co (1972)) and profit made from the wrong (AB v South West Water Services (1993)); <strong>nominal damages</strong> — the right infringed but no loss; <strong>contemptuous damages</strong> — liability established but no sympathy. AO2/mini-conclusion: each type answers a different loss; exemplary damages are rare because civil courts do not generally punish.
Explain when the courts will grant specific performance and rescission, and state the bars to rescission. [AQA Paper 1, 10 marks]
Answer: AO1: <strong>Specific performance</strong> — an equitable order compelling performance of contractual obligations, granted only where damages are inadequate: Beswick v Beswick (1968); Sky Petroleum v VIP Petroleum (1974); refused for personal-service or continuously supervised contracts (Co-operative Insurance Society v Argyll Stores (1998)) and where the claimant has unclean hands. <strong>Rescission</strong> — sets a voidable contract aside ab initio, restoring both parties; grounds: misrepresentation, duress, undue influence, mistake, non-disclosure in utmost-good-faith contracts. AO2: <strong>Bars to rescission</strong>: affirmation (carrying on after discovering the vitiating factor); lapse of time (Leaf v International Galleries (1950)); restitutio in integrum impossible (substantial change or consumption); third-party rights acquired in good faith. AO3/mini-conclusion: both are discretionary equitable remedies — flexible and forward-looking, contrasted with the automatic common-law right to damages.
A factory's fumes drift into Priya's garden and damage her plants; the factory refuses to change its filters. Advise Priya on the remedies available, including an injunction. [OCR H418 Component 1 style scenario, 20 marks]
Answer: AO1: Remedies for nuisance include damages and injunctions; an injunction is an equitable order — prohibitory (stop the fumes) or mandatory (require new filters); interim (until trial) or final (perpetual). AO2: <strong>Damages</strong> — compensatory: special damages for the destroyed plants (provable to trial) and general damages for loss of enjoyment of the land. <strong>Interim injunction</strong> — American Cyanamid Co v Ethicon Ltd (1975): (1) a serious question to be tried — nuisance is arguable on the facts; (2) the balance of convenience — compare the harm to Priya's garden with the cost of stopping production; (3) preserve the status quo until trial. <strong>Final injunction</strong> — if Priya establishes the nuisance at trial, a prohibitory injunction restraining the fumes is likely; the court may award damages in lieu where an injunction would be oppressive. <strong>Breach</strong> of any injunction is a contempt of court — fine or imprisonment. AO3/evaluation: damages are inadequate where the nuisance is continuing — an injunction is the apt remedy; but equity is discretionary, and delay or clean-hands problems could defeat it; conclude that Priya should seek an interim injunction immediately and damages for past loss.
'Prison does not work.' Evaluate this statement with reference to the aims of sentencing and to community orders. [AQA Paper 1, 25 marks]
Answer: AO1: Aims under CJA 2003, s.142 — punishment (retribution), reduction of crime (deterrence), reform and rehabilitation, protection of the public, reparation. Custodial sentences: mandatory life for murder; fixed-term custody with release at the halfway point; suspended sentences (Sentencing Act 2020, s.189). Community orders: unpaid work (40-300 hours), supervision, curfew with electronic monitoring, drug and alcohol treatment requirements (Sentencing Act 2020). AO3 (for the statement): official figures show well over half of those serving short custodial sentences reoffend within a year — prison fails at rehabilitation; general deterrence evidence is weak; prison is expensive and overcrowded; release at the halfway point undercuts protection and retribution. AO3 (against): custody incapacitates dangerous offenders and delivers retribution proportionate to serious crime; the mandatory life tariff for murder (Schedule 21 CJA 2003) protects the public; community orders can be breached and are perceived as 'soft', so they may not denounce serious offending; a suspended sentence is a middle path. <strong>Judgement</strong>: the statement is too broad — prison 'works' for retribution, incapacitation and the most serious offenders, but fails rehabilitation for short sentences; the better reform is targeted community orders with treatment requirements for lower-level offenders and custody reserved for serious and dangerous cases.
Dev, 19, of previous good character, pleads guilty at the first opportunity to a single assault occasioning actual bodily harm on an emergency worker, committed while drunk and distressed after a family bereavement. Advise the court on the likely sentence, applying the aggravating and mitigating factors. [AQA 7162 Paper 1 style problem question, 25 marks]
Answer: AO1: Purposes of sentencing (CJA 2003, s.142): punishment, reduction of crime, reform and rehabilitation, protection of the public, reparation; seriousness assessed by culpability and harm (CJA 2003, s.143). AO2: <strong>Aggravating</strong> — the victim was an emergency worker (a guideline aggravating factor and a separate offence under the Assaults on Emergency Workers (Offences) Act 2018); committed under the influence of alcohol; harm caused (ABH). <strong>Mitigating</strong> — guilty plea at the first opportunity (Sentencing Act 2020, s.73 — up to one-third off); previous good character; age and youth (19 — the court may treat young adults with greater indulgence); distress after a bereavement (circumstances beyond his control). <strong>Sentence</strong> — the custodial threshold (Sentencing Act 2020, s.230): an ABH on an emergency worker is serious, but with one-third reduction and strong mitigation the threshold may arguably be met without custody — likely outcomes: a community order with an alcohol treatment requirement and unpaid work, or a short suspended sentence; a fine alone is inappropriate for ABH. <strong>AO3/conclusion</strong> — the aims in tension: retribution and protection (emergency worker) v rehabilitation (youth, remorse, bereavement); a reasoned recommendation with the factors applied, not 'it depends'.
Explain how judges set the minimum term for murder and when a whole life order may be imposed. [AQA Paper 1, 10 marks]
Answer: AO1: Murder carries a <strong>mandatory life sentence</strong> (Murder (Abolition of Death Penalty) Act 1965); the judge sets the <strong>minimum term (tariff)</strong> from the Schedule 21 CJA 2003 starting points. AO2: <strong>15 years</strong> — standard adult murder; <strong>30 years</strong> — particularly high seriousness (use of a firearm; murder of a police or prison officer in the course of duty); <strong>25 years</strong> — where a knife or other weapon was taken to the scene; <strong>whole life order</strong> (CJA 2003, s.269 with Sch.21) — the most exceptional cases: two or more murders, sexual or sadistic murder of a child, murder to advance a political, religious or ideological cause, murder by a previously convicted murderer. The Parole Board may release after the tariff; recall follows breach of the licence; <em>Hutchinson v United Kingdom</em> (2017) upheld the compatibility of whole life orders. AO3/mini-conclusion: the tariff balances retribution (proportionality) with the mandatory nature of the sentence, and the Parole Board adds a risk-based protection layer.
