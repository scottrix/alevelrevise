Non-Fatal Offences Against the Person

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA, OCR, WJEC/Eduqas

The non-fatal offences ladder: assault, battery, s.47 ABH, s.20 wounding/GBH and s.18 GBH with intent under the OAPA 1861 — actus reus, mens rea, consent and Law Commission reform for AQA 3.2.4, OCR 1.2.3 and WJEC/Eduqas Component 1.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define and apply the actus reus and mens rea of assault and battery, citing the Criminal Justice Act 1988, s.39 (AQA 3.2.4; OCR 1.2.3; WJEC/Eduqas Component 1)
  • Set out the elements of s.47 OAPA 1861 (ABH) and apply the R v Roberts and R v Savage; R v Parmenter rules on occasioning and mens rea
  • Distinguish a wound from other injury (R v Eisenhower) and define grievous bodily harm for s.20 (DPP v Smith; R v Saunders; R v Bollom)
  • Apply the s.20 mens rea (R v Mowatt; R v Savage) and contrast it with the specific intent required for s.18 (R v Mohan; R v Woollin)
  • Assess the availability and limits of consent as a defence to non-fatal offences (R v Brown; R v Wilson; R v Barnes; A-G's Reference (No 6 of 1980))
  • Evaluate the OAPA 1861 and the Law Commission (2015) reform recommendations, including the s.47/s.20 sentencing anomaly
  • Deploy offence ladder and case law accurately in 25-mark problem questions (AQA) and scenario questions (OCR/WJEC/Eduqas)

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Assault: A common-law offence (prosecuted summarily under CJA 1988, s.39): any act, word or gesture causing the victim to apprehend the immediate application of unlawful force.
Battery: The application of unlawful force to another, however slight; no apprehension is required, and contact generally acceptable in everyday conduct (Collins v Wilcock) is excluded.
Actual bodily harm (ABH): Harm calculated to interfere with the victim's health or comfort (R v Miller), including recognised psychiatric injury but not mere emotion (R v Chan-Fook); s.47 OAPA 1861, max 5 years.
Occasioning: The causation word in s.47: the defendant's assault or battery must factually cause the harm; no foreseeability of the harm is needed (R v Roberts).
Wound: A break in the continuity of both layers of the skin (R v Eisenhower); internal bleeding, bruises and fractured bones are not wounds.
Grievous bodily harm (GBH): Really serious harm (DPP v Smith; R v Saunders), which can be the aggregation of injuries assessed against the victim's age and health (R v Bollom); includes serious psychiatric illness (R v Ireland; R v Burstow).
Maliciously: In s.20 OAPA 1861: intention or subjective recklessness as to some physical harm, not as to GBH itself (R v Mowatt; R v Savage; R v Parmenter).
Inflict: The s.20 causation word: held not to require a technical assault — inflicting is the same as causing (R v Ireland; R v Burstow).
Direct intention: The defendant's aim, purpose or decision to bring about the prohibited consequence (R v Mohan).
Oblique intention: Where the consequence is a virtual certainty of the defendant's act and the defendant appreciated that virtual certainty, the jury may find intention (R v Woollin).
Subjective recklessness: The defendant foresees a risk of harm and unreasonably goes on to take it (Cunningham approach, adopted for OAPA offences by R v Savage; R v Parmenter).
Psychiatric injury: A recognised clinical condition (e.g. depression, anxiety states, PTSD) which can constitute ABH (s.47) or GBH (s.20/s.18); mere emotions such as fear, panic or stress do not qualify (R v Chan-Fook; R v Ireland).

📌 Key Points

Offence ladder by maximum sentence: assault/battery (6 months, CJA 1988 s.39) < s.47 ABH (5 years) = s.20 wounding/GBH (5 years) < s.18 GBH with intent (life imprisonment)
Mens rea never rises above the base offence of assault/battery except for s.18, which requires specific intent
R v Ireland; R v Burstow: words (even silence) can be an assault, and psychiatric injury can be ABH and GBH
Wound = both layers of skin broken (R v Eisenhower); GBH = really serious harm (DPP v Smith; R v Saunders)
R v Savage; R v Parmenter fixed the mens rea of s.47 and s.20: recklessness as to some harm suffices; foreseeing GBH is never required
R v Woollin: oblique intention requires virtual certainty plus defendant's appreciation — jury may find intention
Law Commission report 361 (2015) recommended replacing the OAPA 1861 with a modern statute graded by culpability — use for AO3

Assault and battery — the common-law base (AQA 3.2.4; OCR 1.2.3; WJEC/Eduqas Component 1)

Assault is a common-law offence, prosecuted summarily under Criminal Justice Act 1988, s.39 (maximum 6 months' imprisonment and/or a fine): any act, word or gesture by which the defendant intentionally or recklessly causes the victim to apprehend the immediate application of unlawful force. The actus reus needs no contact at all — silent telephone calls causing fear can be an assault: R v Ireland; R v Burstow [1998]. Conditional threats still count: R v Martin (1881) — defendant barred theatre exits and told the crowd to hasten or be hurt. Immediacy is applied flexibly: R v Constanza [1997] — letters and a resentful campaign gave the victim well-founded fear that violence might be used at some time not excluding the immediate future. Words can negate apprehension where they make the threat conditional on a lawful act: Tuberville v Savage (1669). Mens rea is intention or subjective recklessness: in R v Lamb [1967] the victim was shot with a revolver the defendant did not know would fire — no assault (no apprehension) and no unlawful act manslaughter because no unlawful act existed.

Battery is the application of unlawful force to another, however slight (Faulkner v Talbot (1981)); touching a skirt hem without more is too trivial (R v Thomas (1985)). Force can be indirect: pushing a bar door into a fleeing crowd (R v Martin (1881)); acid spraying from a tampered petrol nozzle (DPP v K [1990]); punching a mother causing her to drop her baby (R v Haystead (2000)). Everyday contact — such as generally guiding a person rather than seizing them — is not unlawful: Collins v Wilcock [1984]. Creating a danger then failing to warn can found liability by omission: R v Santana-Bermudez [2003] — defendant told a searching officer there was nothing in his pockets; she was stabbed by a needle.

Exam traps: assault and battery
  • Apprehension, not fear: the victim need only be aware force is imminent, not frightened.
  • Battery requires no apprehension and no direct contact; assault requires no contact and may be by words or silence.
  • Charge practice: where there is injury with a lower mens rea, the CPS charges s.39 assault/battery or s.47 ABH rather than s.20 (CPS charging standards) — use for AO3.
  • Mens rea of both offences: intention or subjective recklessness — see R v Parmenter [1991] for the application.

ABH — s.47 OAPA 1861

s.47 OAPA 1861 (maximum 5 years): an assault or battery occasioning actual bodily harm. Actus reus: a complete assault or battery plus harm — injury calculated to interfere with the health or comfort of the victim (R v Miller [1954]); it need not be permanent, and scratches, bruising and transient injuries suffice. Psychiatric injury counts if it is a recognised clinical condition beyond mere emotions: R v Chan-Fook [1994] and R v Ireland; R v Burstow [1998] (depressive illness and anxiety from stalking phone calls).

Occasioning requires only factual causation between the assault/battery and the harm — the harm need not be reasonably foreseeable: R v Roberts [1971] — the victim jumped from a moving car to escape the defendant's sexual advances; her injuries were still ABH. Mens rea is only the mens rea of the base assault or battery: R v Savage; R v Parmenter [1992] — the House of Lords held that s.47 requires no mens rea as to the harm itself; recklessness as to some unlawful physical contact suffices (DPP v Spratt [1990] — pellet-gun spraying near passers-by).

Why s.47 matters for AO3
  • Anomaly: s.47 and s.20 carry the same maximum sentence (5 years), yet s.47 requires only the mens rea of a battery while s.20 requires recklessness as to some harm — the Law Commission (2015) treats this as a principal defect of the OAPA 1861.
  • Causation gap: because occasioning requires no foreseeability, an unforeseeable flight reaction can elevate a battery to a 5-year offence.
  • Consent interplay: for serious consensual activity, s.47 charges are used where s.20 would fail for lack of injury — see R v Wilson [1996] (branding initials; consent available where comparable to tattooing).

s.20 and s.18 OAPA 1861 — wounding and grievous bodily harm

s.20 OAPA 1861 (maximum 5 years): unlawfully and maliciously wounding or inflicting grievous bodily harm. A wound is a break in the continuity of both layers of the skin: R v Eisenhower [1984] — pellet punctures with internal bleeding were not a wound. GBH means really serious harm (DPP v Smith [1961]; juries today are directed simply with really serious harm — R v Saunders (1985)). Harm can be cumulative: a series of minor injuries aggregated, assessed in light of the victim's age and health (R v Bollom [2004]; R v Brown and Stratton [2008]). Serious psychiatric illness can be GBH: R v Ireland; R v Burstow [1998] — inflict is satisfied without a technical assault, and for s.20 inflict means cause. Disease transmission can also found s.20 liability where the victim did not knowingly consent: R v Dica [2004] (HIV).

Mens rea of s.20: maliciously means intention or subjective recklessness as to some physical harm — not as to GBH: R v Mowatt [1968]; confirmed in R v Savage; R v Parmenter [1992]. The defendant who foresees only a slight injury is still guilty of s.20 if really serious harm results.

s.18 OAPA 1861 (maximum life): unlawfully and maliciously by any means wounding or causing GBH with intent to do GBH, or with intent to resist or prevent lawful apprehension or detention. The mens rea is specific intent: direct intention (R v Mohan [1976]) or oblique intention — where GBH is a virtual certainty of the act and the defendant appreciated that — the jury may find intention (R v Woollin [1999]). Subjective recklessness is never enough for s.18: the key distinction from s.20. Note the drafting width: s.18 says by any means (so omissions can suffice), while s.20 says inflict.

Consent as a defence to non-fatal offences (AQA 3.2.4; OCR 1.2.3)

Consent is no answer where the conduct causes actual bodily harm or worse, except in recognised categories: properly conducted games and sports, horseplay, surgery, tattooing, ear-piercing, and lawful dangerous displays. The baseline rule: R v Brown [1994] — consensual sadomasochistic activities causing injuries were not protected; consent is unavailable for s.47/s.20 assaults beyond the exceptions. Public policy condemns private fights: A-G's Reference (No 6 of 1980) [1981] — a planned fist fight is unlawful, though incidental injury in a spontaneous scuffle is treated differently (R v Barnes [2004] — sporting consent covers conduct within the rules and what a reasonable player would expect, but not criminal violence off the ball). R v Wilson [1996] allowed a consent defence for branding initials on a wife — activity analogous to tattooing, showing the court's willingness to expand exceptions; R v Richardson and Irwin [1999] allowed consent to dangerous horseplay (dangling a student from a balcony) and took account of what sober participants would have appreciated. Accidental, truly unforeseeable harm during consensual activity may leave no unlawful act: R v Slingsby [1995]; trivial harm with consent is outside the OAPA altogether: R v Donovan [1934].

Using consent for marks
  • Always test consent after establishing the offence — examiners reward order.
  • Link to law reform: the Law Commission (2015) recommended clarifying which consensual activities should remain criminal.
  • Contrast criminal consent limits with civil volenti (see the negligence topic) — knowledge must be real and free in tort too.

The offence ladder, law reform and exam technique

The non-fatal offences form a ladder matched to culpability and harm: battery/assault (6 months) to s.47/s.20 (5 years) to s.18 (life). The ladder has long been criticised: language — occasioning, maliciously, inflict and grievous are archaic and inconsistently construed; structure — s.47 carries the same maximum as s.20 despite a lower mens rea; wounding — a technical skin rule produces odd results (R v Eisenhower: internal bleeding worse than a cut is not a wound). The Law Commission report 361 (2015), Reform of Offences Against the Person Act 1861, recommended a new statute with offences of intentional serious injury, reckless serious injury and intentional/reckless injury, abolishing the distinctions between wounding and other harm; successive governments have not enacted it — a strong AO3 point on law-making inaction. CPS charging standards mitigate some inconsistency by charging the offence that matches culpability.

Exam technique. For each suspect act: (1) identify the possible offence from the ladder; (2) work upwards — assault/battery, then s.47, then s.20, then s.18; (3) define AR and MR with one authority per element; (4) apply to facts, including causation; (5) consider consent; (6) conclude on the most likely charge. In evaluation paragraphs, deploy the s.47/s.20 anomaly, the Eisenhower wound rule, Burstow's psychologisation of harm, and the Law Commission recommendations.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: Sally repeatedly telephones Mike at night in silence until he suffers a recognised psychiatric illness and needs three months off work. At a party later that year, Karl shoves Joe, who stumbles into a glass table that shatters, deeply cutting his arm and severing a nerve. Discuss the criminal liability of Sally and Karl for offences against the person. (AQA 7162 Paper 1 style problem question, 25 marks)
Mark-scheme logic: AO1 — state the law: assault/battery under CJA 1988 s.39 (Ireland; Collins v Wilcock); s.47 ABH (Miller; Chan-Fook; Roberts; Savage; Parmenter); s.20 wounding/GBH (Eisenhower; DPP v Smith; Saunders; Mowatt; Burstow); s.18 GBH with intent (Mohan; Woollin). AO2 — apply: SALLY: silent calls = assault (R v Ireland; R v Burstow); repeated campaign gives well-founded fear of immediate violence (Constanza). Her depressive illness is a recognised condition = ABH (Chan-Fook; Ireland) and could be GBH if really serious; mens rea: intention or recklessness to cause apprehension (Parmenter); occasioning satisfied — no foreseeability of the harm needed (Roberts). Likely s.47 ABH; s.20 possible if illness is really serious (Burstow). KARL: shove = battery (Faulkner v Talbot); the battery factually occasioned Joe's injuries (but for the shove he would not have fallen). Shattered-table cut is a wound (both skin layers broken — Eisenhower) and deep cut with nerve damage is really serious harm (DPP v Smith; Saunders). Mens rea: recklessness as to some force/contact (Parmenter; DPP v Spratt) makes out s.20 (Mowatt — no need to foresee GBH); whether s.18 applies depends on oblique intent — severing a nerve would have to be a virtual certainty Karl appreciated (Woollin); on these facts s.20 is the better conclusion, with s.18 as an alternative considered and rejected. Consent: none arises (no consensual activity). AO3 — evaluation: s.47 and s.20 share a 5-year maximum though Karl's s.20 mens rea is arguably no worse than Sally's s.47 recklessness; the wound/GBH split (Eisenhower) is arbitrary; Law Commission 361 (2015) would grade by culpability and end the anomaly. Conclude: Sally — s.47 ABH (s.20 alternative); Karl — s.20 (s.18 not made out).

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Nadia, a police officer, seizes Nadine by the wrist to escort her from a shop; when Nadine resists, Nadia twists the arm. Separately, Owen blocks a doorway and tells a frightened customer: Move, or I will move you — but only if you refuse to leave lawfully. Discuss the liability of Nadia and Owen for assault and battery. (10 marks)
Answer: AO1: battery is the application of unlawful force (Faulkner v Talbot); assault is causing apprehension of immediate unlawful force (CJA 1988 s.39); everyday contact is excluded (Collins v Wilcock); words can negate a threat (Tuberville v Savage); mens rea is intention or subjective recklessness (Parmenter). AO2: Nadia — generally escorting a person without seizing them is not battery (Collins v Wilcock), but twisting the arm on resistance exceeds the general liberty to detain and is a battery; no s.47 injury stated. Owen — the threat is conditional on a lawful act: refusing to leave lawfully. Like Tuberville v Savage, the words may negate the apprehension of unlawful force, so no assault; if the condition is idle or the threat is of unlawful force, Martin (1881) (conditional threats) makes it an assault. AO3: note how quickly lawful contact becomes battery, and the fine line between conditional assault and words that destroy the threat. Conclude: Nadia guilty of battery; Owen not guilty of assault if the condition negates apprehension.
The mens rea of s.47 OAPA 1861 is too low for an offence carrying five years' imprisonment. Discuss. (15 marks)
Answer: AO1: s.47 requires only the mens rea of an assault or battery — intention or subjective recklessness as to some physical contact (R v Savage; R v Parmenter); occasioning requires only factual causation with no foreseeability of the harm (R v Roberts); recklessness as to trivial contact suffices (DPP v Spratt). AO2: a defendant reckless only as to a shove is liable for five years where the victim reacts unexpectedly (Roberts facts). AO3 — arguments for: the rule defies the harm-culpability match; the Law Commission (2015) recommended at least recklessness as to some injury for the middle offences; foreseeability of harm would align s.47 with s.20. Arguments against: the victim's reaction is often the essence of the offence (Roberts was decided to protect victims who act to escape); Parliament has repeatedly re-enacted the structure; CPS charging standards channel low-culpability cases to lesser charges. Conclusion: the mens rea is too low relative to the maximum, but judicial tightening (requiring foreseeability of some injury) or statutory reform is the better remedy than juries ignoring binding authority.
Dana throws a beer glass at Perry after a quarrel. It slips from her hand, shatters and a splinter cuts Perry's wrist deeply, requiring eight stitches. Discuss Dana's liability. (10 marks)
Answer: AO1: s.20 requires unlawfully and maliciously wounding or inflicting GBH (R v Savage); maliciously = intention or subjective recklessness as to some physical harm, not GBH (R v Mowatt; Savage; Parmenter); a wound is a break in both skin layers (R v Eisenhower); GBH is really serious harm (DPP v Smith; R v Saunders). s.18 additionally requires specific intent (Mohan; Woollin). AO2: the deep cut requiring stitches is a wound and arguably really serious harm; throwing the glass shows at least subjective recklessness as to some harm — s.20 made out; there is no evidence Dana intended GBH, and the slip shows GBH was not a virtual certainty she appreciated, so s.18 (Woollin) is not made out. AO3: contrast with R v Savage itself, where the defendant did not foresee the wounding yet was liable under s.20 for recklessness as to some harm; note the severity of a 5-year offence for a foreseeably minor act. Conclude: s.20 wounding/inflicting GBH; s.18 rejected.
Tom and Ravi, both adult footballers, consent to a hard but legal challenge in a five-a-side match; Tom's tackle is outside the rules and breaks Ravi's leg. Would Tom's liability differ if the injury occurred in consensual, private fist-fighting? (15 marks)
Answer: AO1: consent is a defence only within recognised categories — properly conducted sports, horseplay, surgery, tattooing; it cannot excuse s.47/s.20 injury (R v Brown); public policy forbids private fights (A-G's Reference (No 6 of 1980)); sporting consent covers what the rules permit and what a reasonable player would expect (R v Barnes); incidental injury in spontaneous scuffles is treated less severely than planned violence. AO2: the football tackle — if within or at the margin of the rules and what players foresee, Barnes keeps it out of criminal law despite the broken leg; outside the rules with force beyond what is foreseeable, criminal liability (s.20) follows. The private fist fight — No 6 makes it unlawful per se; injury elevates it to s.47/s.20 regardless of consent. AO3: Barnes balances sport's social value against consent limits; Brown prioritises dignity and public policy over autonomy; the Law Commission (2015) favoured clarifying the boundary. Conclude: liability differs — consent protects within-sport injury but never private fighting causing harm.
The Offences Against the Person Act 1861 is a serious defect in the criminal law. Discuss with reference to law reform. (25 marks, AQA Paper 1 evaluation style)
Answer: AO1: the OAPA 1861 supplies s.47, s.20 and s.18; key authorities: Savage; Parmenter (mens rea), Eisenhower (wound), Smith/Saunders/Bollom (GBH), Ireland; Burstow (psychiatric harm), Woollin (intent); Law Commission report 361 (2015) proposed a modern statute; CPS charging standards manage the gaps. AO2: evaluate each defect with authority — (i) archaic language: maliciously, occasioning, inflict construed inconsistently (Mowatt vs Smith; Burstow reading inflict as cause); (ii) the s.47/s.20 anomaly: equal maxima, unequal mens rea; (iii) the wound rule: internal bleeding worse than a cut is not a wound (Eisenhower); (iv) psychiatric harm forced into bodily language (Chan-Fook; Ireland). AO3: for reform — coherence, grading by culpability, accessibility; against — Parliament's inaction shows working practice is tolerable; CPS charging standards and the Sentencing Council mitigate; abolition risks unsettling settled interpretations (Burstow). Conclusion: the defects are real and the Law Commission's 2015 scheme is the better approach, but incremental judicial management explains legislative inertia.
