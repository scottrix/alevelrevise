Parliamentary Law Making and Delegated Legislation

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA, OCR, WJEC/Eduqas

The legislative process from Green Paper to Royal Assent, influences on Parliament, the types of delegated legislation (statutory instruments, byelaws, Orders in Council) and their parliamentary and judicial control — ultra vires, Aylesbury Mushrooms, Fire Brigades Union — for AQA 3.1.2/3.1.3, OCR H418 Component 3 and WJEC/Eduqas Component 1.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Trace the full legislative process — Green and White Papers, bill types (public, private, hybrid, private members'), all stages in both Houses, ping-pong and Royal Assent — citing the Parliament Acts 1911 and 1949 (AQA 3.1.2; OCR H418 Component 3, Section A; WJEC/Eduqas Component 1)
  • Explain the influences on parliamentary law making, including pressure groups (Snowdrop campaign), the media, public opinion and the Law Commission under the Law Commissions Act 1965
  • Distinguish the three types of delegated legislation — statutory instruments, byelaws and Orders in Council — with examples, and explain why Parliament delegates (AQA 3.1.3)
  • Apply parliamentary controls: enabling act limits, affirmative and negative resolutions (Statutory Instruments Act 1946), the Joint Select Committee on Statutory Instruments and Henry VIII clauses
  • Apply judicial control by ultra vires, substantive (A-G v Fulham Corporation; Strickland v Hayes BC; Cure and Deeley) and procedural (Aylesbury Mushrooms), plus the brake on prerogative power (Laker Airways; Fire Brigades Union; Ahmed v HM Treasury)
  • Evaluate delegated legislation against democratic deficit, sub-delegation, publicity and flexibility, using accurate case law in problem questions and 25-mark essays

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Green Paper: A government consultation document setting out provisional policy ideas and inviting comment before legislation is drafted.
White Paper: A firmer government statement of intended policy, published after consultation, on which proposed legislation is based.
Public bill: A bill introduced by the government (or a backbencher) affecting the whole country and the general public; most Acts of Parliament are public bills.
Private members' bill: A public bill introduced by a backbench MP rather than a minister — via the ballot, the Ten Minute Rule or presentation; few pass (e.g. Abortion Act 1967; Murder (Abolition of Death Penalty) Act 1965).
Hybrid bill: A public bill that also affects particular private interests (e.g. Crossrail Act 2008), giving affected parties a right to be heard — treated like a private bill procedure.
Royal Assent: The monarch's formal approval making a bill an Act; a constitutional convention since the Royal Assent Act 1967 — announced by the presiding officers of each House and never refused in modern times.
Salisbury Convention: The convention that the House of Lords does not block or seriously delay government bills fulfilling manifesto commitments.
Statutory instrument (SI): Rules, orders or regulations made by government ministers under powers in an enabling Act (Statutory Instruments Act 1946); over 3,000 are made each year.
Byelaw: Delegated legislation made by local authorities (Local Government Act 1972) or public bodies (e.g. water companies under the Water Industry Act 1991, rail operators) for their own area or undertaking — confirmed centrally before taking effect.
Order in Council: Delegated legislation made by the Privy Council, used for transferring ministerial functions, bringing Acts into force and emergency powers under the Civil Contingencies Act 2004.
Henry VIII clause: An enabling power allowing ministers to amend or repeal provisions of primary legislation by statutory instrument (e.g. Civil Contingencies Act 2004; EU (Withdrawal) Act 2018) — criticised as shifting law-making away from Parliament.
Ultra vires: Beyond the powers — the judicial doctrine that delegated legislation (or action under prerogative power) is void if it exceeds the enabling Act (substantively or procedurally) or defeats the will of Parliament.

📌 Key Points

Bill stages: First Reading (formal) → Second Reading (debate, vote) → Committee (Public Bill Committee, line by line) → Report → Third Reading → Lords → ping-pong → Royal Assent.
Parliament Acts 1911 and 1949 allow a bill to pass without Lords consent after delay — used for the War Crimes Act 1991 and Hunting Act 2004.
Over 3,000 statutory instruments a year against roughly 50–60 public Acts — delegation saves parliamentary time.
Affirmative resolution: SI needs active approval; negative resolution: annulled within 40 days unless objected to (Statutory Instruments Act 1946).
JCSI scrutinises SIs on technical grounds (e.g. charging money, retrospective effect, doubtful legality).
Ultra vires has two limbs: substantive (beyond the enabling act — Fulham Corporation) and procedural (required consultation skipped — Aylesbury Mushrooms).
Courts also brake prerogative power used to defeat statute: Laker Airways; Fire Brigades Union; Ahmed v HM Treasury.

From policy to statute — the legislative process (AQA 3.1.2; OCR H418 Component 3, Section A; WJEC/Eduqas Component 1)

Most major reform begins with consultation: a Green Paper invites views on provisional ideas, then a White Paper states the government's intended policy. Bills are then drafted and introduced as public bills (affecting the country), private bills (promoted by local authorities or companies for particular powers), hybrid bills (public bills touching particular private interests, e.g. Crossrail Act 2008) or private members' bills — backbench bills entered via the ballot, the Ten Minute Rule or presentation. Few private members' bills succeed (Abortion Act 1967; Murder (Abolition of Death Penalty) Act 1965 are celebrated exceptions) because the ballot allocates only limited parliamentary time and government business dominates the timetable.

The stages in each House:

  1. First Reading — formal introduction; title read, no debate.
  2. Second Reading — main debate on the bill's principles; MPs vote.
  3. Committee stage — a Public Bill Committee examines the bill line by line and amendments are tabled; constitutional or urgent bills may be taken by the whole House.
  4. Report stage — the whole House considers committee amendments and further changes.
  5. Third Reading — final debate and vote; no substantive amendments.

The bill then repeats the same stages in the House of Lords, where peers act as a revising chamber — appointed life peers, 92 hereditary peers and the Lords Spiritual give independent expertise from law, medicine, business and the civil service, and party whipping is weaker than in the Commons; the Salisbury Convention stops peers wrecking manifesto bills, and government defeats are frequent but rarely fatal. Disagreement produces ping-pong — messages passing between the Houses — until one side gives way or the government uses the Parliament Acts 1911 and 1949 to pass the bill without Lords consent after a delay (War Crimes Act 1991; European Parliamentary Elections Act 1999; Hunting Act 2004). Royal Assent follows by convention (Royal Assent Act 1967) and is never refused in modern practice. Commencement is on assent, on a fixed date, or by ministerial commencement order — giving ministers practical control over when the law takes effect.

Influences on parliamentary law making (AQA 3.1.2; OCR H418 Component 3, Section A)

Government policy is shaped between elections by several pressures:

  • Pressure groups — insider groups work closely with ministers (e.g. trade associations consulted on drafts); outsider groups campaign publicly. The Snowdrop campaign after the Dunblane massacre produced the Firearms (Amendment) Act 1997 — a model of single-issue influence.
  • The media — newspapers and broadcasts set agendas, expose problems and mobilise opinion; ministers respond to sustained coverage, though media panics can drive rushed law (Dangerous Dogs Act 1991).
  • Public opinion — especially where high-profile events shift attitudes (hunting, same-sex marriage, knife crime); e-petitions and referendums (the 2016 Brexit referendum) also steer the legislative programme.
  • Law reform bodies — the Law Commission (Law Commissions Act 1965) keeps the law under review and publishes reports with draft bills; reforms such as the consolidation found in the Consumer Rights Act 2015 and the Land Registration Act 2002 show its influence, though not every report is enacted (its non-fatal offences reform proposals remain unimplemented).
  • European and international obligations — retained EU law (EU (Withdrawal) Act 2018) and treaties drive change; the Human Rights Act 1998 and the Joint Committee on Human Rights shape drafts at committee stage.

Professional and trade bodies (the Law Society, Bar Council, CBI) lobby on drafts they will have to work with, giving practical drafting feedback. Evaluation: consultation and law reform bodies give expertise and legitimacy, but government majorities dominate the process, private members' bills rarely pass, and media panics can produce rushed, poorly scrutinised legislation — a recurring A* evaluative point linking law making to the separation of powers: law should be made by the elected Commons after proper scrutiny, not by ministers acting alone or by panicked headlines.

Delegated legislation — the types (AQA 3.1.3; OCR H418 Component 3, Section A; WJEC/Eduqas Component 1)

Parliament delegates law-making power through an enabling Act (the parent statute), which specifies the person who may make the rules, their purpose and their area — e.g. enabling powers under education or social security legislation. Three main types:

  • Statutory instruments — rules, orders and regulations made by government ministers (Statutory Instruments Act 1946); over 3,000 a year, covering everything from benefits uprating and national minimum wage rates to road traffic regulations, and including codes of practice such as the PACE 1984 codes — SIs touch almost every area of daily life, which is why their control matters. Flexible, detailed and fast, but subject to tight control (below).
  • Byelaws — made by local authorities (Local Government Act 1972) and public bodies for their own area: water companies (Water Industry Act 1991), National Parks, and rail operators. Byelaws must be confirmed centrally before taking effect, and a defendant can challenge their validity even in criminal proceedings (Boddington v British Transport Police, 1998).
  • Orders in Council — made by the Privy Council, used for transferring functions between ministers, bringing Acts into force, and emergency powers under the Civil Contingencies Act 2004; early general elections under the Fixed-term Parliaments Act 2011 were also triggered by Order in Council (the Dissolution and Calling of Parliament Act 2022 has since restored the prerogative).

Why delegate? Parliament lacks the time and technical expertise for thousands of detailed rules; delegation is flexible (fast response to emergencies, e.g. Civil Contingencies Act 2004), local (byelaws reflect local knowledge), expert (ministers and departments know their fields) and easily amended and updated. The risks are the democratic deficit, sub-delegation (power passed further down to civil servants) and less publicity — the core A* tension revisited in the evaluation.

Controlling delegated legislation — parliamentary and judicial (AQA 3.1.3; OCR H418 Component 3, Section A)

Parliamentary control operates at the point of delegation and afterwards:

  • The enabling Act sets the limits: who may make the rules, for what purpose and over what area — the anchor for judicial review.
  • Affirmative resolution — the SI needs active approval by both Houses (e.g. PACE 1984 codes of practice); negative resolution — it takes effect unless annulled within 40 days (Statutory Instruments Act 1946). Most SIs face no debate at all, so these two resolution procedures are the only formal check for the vast majority.
  • The Joint Select Committee on Statutory Instruments (JCSI) scrutinises every SI on technical grounds: charging money on the Consolidated Fund, retrospective effect, unusual or unexpected use of powers, doubtful legality or unjustifiable interference with rights.
  • Hansard debates, votes in the Lords (Secondary Legislation Scrutiny Committee) and sunset clauses (powers expiring unless renewed) provide further control.

Judicial control — the doctrine of ultra vires, enforced through judicial review (a quashing order, with standing and promptness requirements):

  • Substantive ultra vires: the rules go beyond the power given. A-G v Fulham Corporation (1921) — power to provide wash-houses did not extend to charging for their use; Strickland v Hayes Borough Council (1896) — a byelaw banning obscene singing went beyond maintaining public order; Customs and Excise Commissioners v Cure and Deeley (1962) — no power to impose a tax charge, still less retrospectively.
  • Procedural ultra vires: required procedures were skipped. Agricultural Training Board v Aylesbury Mushrooms (1972) — consultation with the organisation representing the industry was required, but mushroom growers were excluded, so the instrument was void.
  • Brake on prerogative power: Laker Airways v Department of Trade (1977) — prerogative cannot be used to defeat a statutory right; R v Secretary of State, ex p Fire Brigades Union (1995) — using the prerogative to frustrate a statute's criminal injuries compensation scheme was unlawful; Ahmed v HM Treasury (2010) — the Supreme Court quashed terrorist asset-freezing Orders in Council as beyond prerogative power, and Parliament passed the Terrorist Asset-Freezing etc. Act 2010 within weeks — a textbook demonstration of parliamentary sovereignty restoring control.

Evaluation and exam technique — democracy v flexibility (AQA 3.1.3; OCR H418 Component 3, Section A)

AdvantagesDisadvantages
Saves parliamentary time for major policy; Parliament cannot legislate in detail for 3,000+ instruments a yearDemocratic deficit — rules made by unelected ministers and civil servants, often without debate
Flexibility and speed — emergency powers, quick amendment (Civil Contingencies Act 2004)Sub-delegation — power passed further down the chain, diluting accountability
Local knowledge — byelaws fit local conditions; departmental expertiseLack of publicity — most SIs are never debated or read by the public
Detail and technical precision impossible in primary legislationHenry VIII clauses allow amendment of primary legislation — sovereignty risk; overlap and confusion between instruments
Quick recall — the case and statute bank
  • Process: Green/White Papers; Parliament Acts 1911 and 1949 (Hunting Act 2004); Royal Assent Act 1967; Salisbury Convention.
  • Types: Statutory Instruments Act 1946; Local Government Act 1972 (byelaws); Water Industry Act 1991; Civil Contingencies Act 2004 (Orders in Council); Boddington v British Transport Police.
  • Control: JCSI technical grounds; A-G v Fulham Corporation; Strickland v Hayes Borough Council; Cure and Deeley; Aylesbury Mushrooms; Laker Airways; Fire Brigades Union; Ahmed v HM Treasury.
  • Influences: Law Commissions Act 1965; Snowdrop campaign (Firearms (Amendment) Act 1997).

Exam technique. Problem questions (OCR/WJEC scenario style) usually test ultra vires: identify the enabling act, check the words and area of the power, check procedural requirements (consultation — Aylesbury Mushrooms), and remember byelaws can be challenged even in criminal proceedings (Boddington). Essay questions (AQA 25-mark evaluate) reward the democracy v flexibility tension, strong case authority (Fulham; Aylesbury; FBU; Ahmed) and a link to parliamentary sovereignty and law reform. The strongest conclusions weigh both sides: controls are extensive in theory but thin in practice — most SIs pass undebated — yet delegation is necessary for volume, urgency and expertise, and must remain anchored to Parliament by enabling acts and ultra vires review rather than abolished.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: The Motor Scooters Act 2023 s.3 enables the Secretary of State to make regulations 'for the safe operation of motor scooters on roads'. The Secretary of State makes the Motor Scooters Regulations 2024 banning riding on pavements and requiring crash helmets, without consulting manufacturers. Separately, Brightsea Council makes a byelaw banning all two-wheeled vehicles from the seafront. Advise the manufacturers and Brightsea riders on the validity of the regulations and the byelaw. [OCR H418 Component 3 style scenario; AQA Paper 1 style — 25 marks]
Mark-scheme logic: AO1 — state the law: delegated legislation must stay within the enabling act (ultra vires): substantive limb — beyond the words or purpose of the power (A-G v Fulham Corporation; Strickland v Hayes BC; Cure and Deeley); procedural limb — required procedures skipped (Agricultural Training Board v Aylesbury Mushrooms — consultation with representative organisations mandatory); byelaws made under LGA 1972 must be confirmed centrally and can be challenged in criminal proceedings (Boddington v British Transport Police); parliamentary control via JCSI, affirmative/negative resolutions; courts police the boundary between delegated and prerogative power (Laker Airways; Fire Brigades Union). AO2 — apply: (1) Regulations: the enabling power covers 'safe operation on roads'; the pavement ban arguably exceeds 'on roads' — substantive ultra vires in the Fulham/Strickland tradition (wider purpose than conferred); the helmet requirement is defensible as safety regulation. No consultation with manufacturers: if the Act requires consultation with representative industry bodies, Aylesbury Mushrooms makes the Regulations void for procedural ultra vires — the parallel is exact (growers excluded from Agricultural Training Board consultation). (2) Byelaw: Brightsea Council has power under LGA 1972 to make byelaws for good rule and government of its district; a blanket ban on all two-wheeled vehicles from the seafront may go beyond maintaining public order — Strickland v Hayes BC (obscene singing byelaw beyond powers) suggests over-broad byelaws are void; if not confirmed centrally it never takes effect; riders can challenge validity even if prosecuted in criminal proceedings (Boddington). AO3 — evaluate: ultra vires review gives teeth to parliamentary limits but operates only after challenges are brought; Aylesbury-type consultation protects affected interests; the democracy v flexibility tension means ministers may overreach (pavement ban) while genuine safety regulation (helmets) shows delegation working well; conclusion — both instruments are vulnerable, the regulations for substantive overreach and skipped consultation (Aylesbury; Fulham), the byelaw for disproportionate breadth (Strickland; Boddington).

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Describe the stages a public bill passes through before becoming an Act of Parliament. [AQA Paper 1, 10 marks]
Answer: Pre-parliamentary: Green Paper (consultation on ideas) and White Paper (statement of intended policy); the bill is drafted. Stages in each House: First Reading — formal introduction, no debate; Second Reading — debate on principles and a vote; Committee stage — Public Bill Committee examines line by line, amendments tabled (constitutional bills may be taken by the whole House); Report stage — the House considers committee amendments; Third Reading — final debate and vote. The bill then repeats the stages in the House of Lords, with the Salisbury Convention protecting manifesto bills; disagreements produce ping-pong until resolved or the Parliament Acts 1911 and 1949 are used (War Crimes Act 1991; Hunting Act 2004). Royal Assent (Royal Assent Act 1967) makes the bill an Act; commencement follows on assent, a fixed date or by commencement order.
Explain the differences between statutory instruments, byelaws and Orders in Council, giving an example of each. [OCR H418 Component 3, 10 marks]
Answer: Statutory instruments: rules, orders and regulations made by ministers under enabling acts (Statutory Instruments Act 1946) — over 3,000 a year; example: regulations uprating benefits or road traffic rules. Byelaws: made by local authorities (Local Government Act 1972) and public bodies for their own area or undertaking, confirmed centrally before taking effect; example: water company byelaws (Water Industry Act 1991) or rail byelaws (Boddington v British Transport Police). Orders in Council: made by the Privy Council — used for transferring ministerial functions, bringing Acts into force and emergency powers under the Civil Contingencies Act 2004. Differences: maker (minister v local/public body v Privy Council), scope (national detail v local area v specific transfers/emergencies) and confirmation procedure (parliamentary resolutions and JCSI scrutiny for SIs; central confirmation for byelaws).
Explain how Parliament controls delegated legislation, and evaluate whether the controls are effective. [WJEC/Eduqas Component 1, 15 marks]
Answer: Control: the enabling act fixes the maker, purpose and area of the power; affirmative resolution requires active approval (e.g. PACE 1984 codes of practice) while negative resolution allows annulment within 40 days (Statutory Instruments Act 1946); the Joint Select Committee on Statutory Instruments scrutinises technical defects (charging money, retrospective effect, doubtful legality, unusual use of powers); debates, the Lords' Secondary Legislation Scrutiny Committee and sunset clauses add further checks. Evaluation: effective — JCSI catches technical defects in every instrument, affirmative procedure forces debate on sensitive rules, enabling acts anchor judicial review. Ineffective — most SIs are never debated (negative resolution rarely triggered), the democratic deficit persists because ministers and officials (unelected) make the rules, sub-delegation dilutes accountability, and Henry VIII clauses let ministers amend primary legislation. Judged overall: parliamentary control is thorough in form but thin in practice; courts supply the real teeth through ultra vires (Fulham; Aylesbury; FBU; Ahmed).
Explain how the courts control delegated legislation. Refer to substantive and procedural ultra vires. [OCR H418 Component 3, 10 marks]
Answer: Doctrine: ultra vires — delegated legislation is void if beyond the powers conferred. Substantive limb: the rules exceed the words or purpose of the enabling act — A-G v Fulham Corporation (1921, charging for wash-houses beyond the power), Strickland v Hayes Borough Council (1896, byelaw banning obscene singing beyond maintaining public order), Customs and Excise Commissioners v Cure and Deeley (1962, no power to tax, still less retrospectively). Procedural limb: required procedures skipped — Agricultural Training Board v Aylesbury Mushrooms (1972, mandatory consultation with industry representatives; mushroom growers excluded, so the instrument was void). Courts also police the boundary between delegated and prerogative power: Laker Airways v Department of Trade (1977, prerogative cannot defeat a statutory right), R v Secretary of State, ex p Fire Brigades Union (1995), Ahmed v HM Treasury (2010, asset-freezing Orders in Council quashed). Byelaws can be challenged in criminal proceedings (Boddington v British Transport Police, 1998).
Evaluate the argument that delegated legislation is a necessary but dangerous form of law-making. [AQA Paper 1, 25 marks]
Answer: AO1: types — statutory instruments (Statutory Instruments Act 1946; over 3,000/year), byelaws (LGA 1972; Water Industry Act 1991; Boddington), Orders in Council (Civil Contingencies Act 2004); control — enabling acts, affirmative/negative resolutions, JCSI technical scrutiny, ultra vires (substantive: Fulham, Strickland, Cure and Deeley; procedural: Aylesbury Mushrooms; prerogative brake: Laker Airways, FBU, Ahmed). AO2/analysis: necessity — Parliament cannot produce 3,000 detailed instruments a year; delegation is fast and flexible (emergency powers), expert and local (byelaws), easily amended. Danger — democratic deficit (unelected ministers and civil servants; most SIs undebated), sub-delegation, poor publicity, Henry VIII clauses amending primary legislation (EU (Withdrawal) Act 2018), overlap and confusion. AO3/evaluation: strong case authority both ways — Ahmed and FBU show courts and Parliament can and do reclaim control, suggesting the system self-corrects; Aylesbury shows affected interests protected; but challenges depend on litigants, most instruments pass unnoticed, and speed in emergencies (Civil Contingencies Act 2004) risks overreach. Conclusion: necessary because primary legislation cannot cope with volume and urgency; dangerous where scrutiny is thin; the balance holds only because enabling acts and ultra vires review keep delegation anchored to Parliament — a sovereignty-based judgement, not an abolitionist one.
