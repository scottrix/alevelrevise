The English Legal System

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA, OCR, WJEC/Eduqas

Court structure and appeal routes for both civil and criminal law, the burden and standard of proof, the judiciary, magistrates, juries, ADR, tribunals and access to justice under LASPO 2012 — AQA 3.1.1, OCR H418 Component 1 and WJEC/Eduqas Component 1.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Describe the hierarchy of the civil courts (County Court, High Court, Court of Appeal, Supreme Court) and apply the track system (small claims, fast, intermediate, multi) to a claim scenario (AQA 3.1.1; OCR H418 Component 1; WJEC/Eduqas Component 1)
  • Set out the criminal court structure, including the summary/either-way/indictable classification and the magistrates' plea-before-venue and mode-of-trial procedures
  • Trace appeal routes in both systems, including case stated, second appeals (Access to Justice Act 1999 s.55), leapfrog appeals (Administration of Justice Act 1969) and appeals to the Supreme Court
  • Distinguish civil from criminal law using purpose, parties, terminology, remedies and the standards of proof (Woolmington v DPP; Miller v Minister of Pensions)
  • Explain the role, appointment and independence of the judiciary, citing the Constitutional Reform Act 2005, Pinochet (No 2) and Miller (No 2)
  • Apply the rules on jury qualification, selection, disqualification and majority verdicts (Juries Act 1974 s.17; Bushel's Case; R v Ponting), and evaluate jury equity
  • Compare court and non-court dispute resolution (negotiation, mediation, conciliation, arbitration, tribunals, ombudsmen) and assess access to justice after LASPO 2012

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Small claims track: The County Court track for most claims under £10,000 (£1,000 for personal injury and housing disrepair): heard informally, often by district judge, no legal costs recoverable — designed to be used without a lawyer.
Intermediate track: Track introduced in October 2023 for most civil claims of £25,000–£100,000 (excluding, e.g., clinical negligence), with fixed recoverable costs to control litigation expense.
Multi-track: The track for complex claims or those over the intermediate/fast track limits, giving the judge case-management discretion and allowing full costs rules.
Divisional court: A High Court panel (King's Bench, Chancery or Family Division) hearing appeals by case stated from magistrates' courts and certain tribunals, plus judicial review of administrative action (KBD).
Leapfrog appeal: A direct appeal from the High Court to the Supreme Court under the Administration of Justice Act 1969, available only where all parties consent and a point of law of general public importance is certified.
Summary offence: The least serious category of criminal offence, triable only in the magistrates' court; magistrates may imprison for up to 6 months for a single offence (12 months in total for two or more either-way offences tried summarily).
Either-way offence: A mid-level offence (e.g. theft, ABH) triable in either court; the defendant's plea before venue and the magistrates' mode-of-trial decision determine the forum.
Burden of proof: Which party must prove the case: in criminal law the prosecution (the 'golden thread' — Woolmington v DPP), in civil law the claimant.
Standard of proof: How convincing the proof must be: beyond reasonable doubt in criminal cases; on the balance of probabilities in civil cases (Miller v Minister of Pensions).
Judicial independence: The principle that judges decide cases free from pressure by government, parties or colleagues — protected by security of tenure (Act of Settlement 1701), paid salaries, the JAC appointment process and, in extreme cases, recusal (Pinochet (No 2); Dimes v Grand Junction Canal).
Jury equity: The jury's power to acquit according to conscience even against the evidence or the judge's direction (Bushel's Case 1670; R v Ponting 1985); there is no appeal against an acquittal.
Alternative dispute resolution (ADR): Methods of settling disputes without litigation: negotiation, mediation, conciliation, arbitration (Arbitration Act 1996) and ombudsman schemes.

📌 Key Points

Civil hierarchy: County Court → High Court (KBD, Chancery, Family) → Court of Appeal (Civil Division) → Supreme Court.
Criminal hierarchy: Magistrates' Court → Crown Court → Court of Appeal (Criminal Division) → Supreme Court.
Criminal standard: beyond reasonable doubt (Woolmington v DPP). Civil standard: balance of probabilities (Miller v Minister of Pensions).
Magistrates deal with roughly 95% of criminal cases; juries of 12 try indictable offences in the Crown Court.
Majority verdicts: 10:1, or 9:1 if the panel has been reduced, after at least 2 hours (Juries Act 1974 s.17).
CCRC (Criminal Appeal Act 1995) refers suspected miscarriages of justice back to the Court of Appeal.
LASPO 2012 cut civil legal aid; funding now relies heavily on CFAs, ombudsmen and free advice services.

Civil courts and the track system (AQA 3.1.1; OCR H418 Component 1; WJEC/Eduqas Component 1)

Civil claims start in the County Court (most contract and tort claims up to £100,000; personal injury claims up to £50,000) or in the High Court, which has three divisions: King's Bench Division (contract, tort, judicial review), Chancery Division (land, trusts, probate, insolvency) and Family Division. Each division has a divisional court hearing specialist appeals — notably the KBD divisional court for case stated and judicial review. County Court business is distributed across hearing centres, and judgment can be enforced by writ of control, attachment of earnings or third-party debt orders.

Case management follows the track system under the Civil Procedure Rules 1998, driven by the Woolf reforms (Access to Justice Act 1999) and their aims of cost-proportionality, speed and equality of arms:

  • Small claims track — most claims under £10,000 (£1,000 for personal injury and housing disrepair); informal hearing, usually a district judge, no legal costs recoverable — deliberately usable without a lawyer.
  • Fast track — claims of £10,000–£25,000, fixed trial window, limited recoverable costs.
  • Intermediate track — £25,000–£100,000 for most claims, with fixed recoverable costs (from October 2023) to rein in expense.
  • Multi-track — complex or high-value claims; judge-led case management and full costs rules.

Appeals: under CPR Part 52 an appeal normally goes to the next tier of judge, so County Court cases can move up to a circuit judge, High Court judge or the High Court itself. The Court of Appeal (Civil Division) is the next stop, but a second appeal needs permission because it raises an important point of principle or practice, or there is a compelling reason (Access to Justice Act 1999 s.55). From the High Court a leapfrog appeal may go straight to the Supreme Court with the consent of all parties and a certified point of law of general public importance (Administration of Justice Act 1969). The Supreme Court, created by the Constitutional Reform Act 2005 and sitting since October 2009, is the final civil (and criminal) appeal court for the UK.

Criminal courts and appeal routes (AQA 3.1.1; OCR H418 Component 1)

Offences are classified as summary (triable only by magistrates, e.g. most driving offences and common assault), either-way (e.g. theft, ABH) or indictable only (e.g. murder, rape — Crown Court only). For either-way offences the defendant enters a plea before venue; if he pleads not guilty, magistrates decide mode of trial — if they are acceptable and the case suitable, it can stay summary (Magistrates' Courts Act 1980 s.19), otherwise it is sent to the Crown Court. Defendants can elect Crown Court trial (s.20) — a strategic choice, since Crown Court acquittal rates are higher but sentencing powers are greater.

Magistrates deal with about 95% of criminal cases and may imprison for up to 6 months for one offence (12 months in total across two or more either-way offences) and impose unlimited fines for adults (since 2015). Defendants aged 10–17 are tried in the youth court, with restrictions on publicity and no jury. The Crown Court tries the most serious cases before a judge and jury of 12, and sentences either-way cases sent up from the magistrates' court.

Appeals:

  • Defendant: from the magistrates' court to the Crown Court against conviction or sentence (Magistrates' Courts Act 1980 s.108 — full rehearing), or by case stated to the High Court on a point of law (s.111); from the Crown Court to the Court of Appeal (Criminal Division) against conviction or sentence where the conviction is unsafe (Criminal Appeal Act 1968).
  • Prosecution: no appeal against an acquittal, but the Attorney-General can refer a point of law after an acquittal (Criminal Justice Act 1972 s.36) or seek leave to challenge an unduly lenient sentence (Criminal Justice Act 1988 s.36) — the sentence may then be increased (A-G's References (Nos 4 and 5 of 2007)).
  • Further appeals: with leave, to the Supreme Court on a point of law of general public importance.

The Criminal Cases Review Commission (Criminal Appeal Act 1995) investigates possible miscarriages of justice and can refer convictions back to the Court of Appeal; the double jeopardy bar was relaxed by the Criminal Justice Act 2003 for serious offences where compelling new evidence emerges (R v Dunlop; R v Dobson — the Stephen Lawrence killers).

Civil law v criminal law — the key distinctions (AQA 3.1.1; OCR H418 Component 1)

Civil lawCriminal law
PurposeCompensate the claimant and restore rightsRegulate behaviour, punish and deter
PartiesClaimant v defendant (both private parties)Crown (via the CPS) v defendant
Burden of proofClaimantProsecution — the golden thread (Woolmington v DPP)
Standard of proofBalance of probabilities (Miller v Minister of Pensions)Beyond reasonable doubt
OutcomeLiable — damages, injunctions, specific performance, possession ordersGuilty — imprisonment, fines, community orders, discharge
CourtsCounty Court, High Court, Court of Appeal (Civil Division)Magistrates' Court, Crown Court, Court of Appeal (Criminal Division)
FundingLargely private (CFAs, insurance); little legal aidPublicly funded defence and prosecution
PublicitySettlements common, courts less publicisedHighly publicised; name-and-shame function

The same act can generate both proceedings: an assault may lead to a criminal prosecution for battery and a civil claim for damages — different parties, standards and outcomes. Terminology also differs (guilty v liable; prosecutor v claimant), and a criminal conviction can support a civil claim in practice. The gap between the standards of proof matters: O J Simpson was acquitted of murder beyond reasonable doubt but found liable for the deaths on the balance of probabilities. The heavier criminal standard protects liberty; the lighter civil standard reflects that only money or property is at stake.

Legal personnel — judiciary, magistrates and juries (AQA 3.1.1; OCR H418 Component 1; WJEC/Eduqas Component 1)

Judiciary. The judicial hierarchy runs from district judges and recorders through circuit judges, High Court judges (puisne judges), Lords Justices of Appeal (Court of Appeal) to the Justices of the Supreme Court. Judges are appointed on merit by the Judicial Appointments Commission, created by the Constitutional Reform Act 2005 to make the process open and independent of government. Judicial independence is protected by security of tenure (Act of Settlement 1701), protected salaries, and convention: ministers cannot criticise judges' decisions. The Supreme Court's ruling that prorogation was unlawful (Miller (No 2), 2019) demonstrated independence in practice; where bias arises, judges recuse themselves (Pinochet (No 2) — automatic disqualification for apparent bias; Dimes v Grand Junction Canal — a decision was void because the judge held shares in the litigant canal company).

Lay magistrates. Unpaid, part-time volunteers appointed by local advisory committees, sitting in panels of three and advised by a legally qualified adviser; they need no legal qualification but must show six key attributes (good character, understanding and communication, social awareness, maturity and sound temperament, sound judgement, commitment and reliability) and commit to training. They try ~95% of criminal cases, decide bail and remand, issue warrants, and hear some family and civil matters — local justice delivered cheaply, though consistency across benches is a standing criticism.

Juries. A jury of 12 tries indictable offences at the Crown Court. Eligibility under the Juries Act 1974: aged 18–74, registered on the electoral roll, ordinarily resident in the UK for at least 5 years since age 13. Disqualification covers those on bail and those with relevant convictions or community orders. Selection is random from the electoral roll via the Jury Central Summoning Bureau; vetting is exceptional (national security, A-G's guidelines). Key rules:

  • Jurors decide according to conscience and cannot be punished for their verdict (Bushel's Case, 1670) — the foundation of jury equity (R v Ponting, 1985 — acquittal despite the judge's direction); there is no appeal against an acquittal.
  • The judge sums up the case; the verdict should be unanimous, but a majority of 10:1 (or 9:1 if the panel has been reduced) may be accepted after at least 2 hours' deliberation (Juries Act 1974 s.17) — recommended by the Runciman Royal Commission (1993) to defeat jury nobbling.

Resolving disputes outside court and access to justice (AQA 3.1.1; OCR H418 Component 1)

ADR avoids court entirely: negotiation (direct or through solicitors — cheapest, preserves relationships, but may fail), mediation (a neutral third party facilitates agreement but does not impose it; common in family and neighbour disputes), conciliation (like mediation, but the conciliator suggests terms — ACAS in employment disputes), arbitration (a binding decision by an arbitrator under the Arbitration Act 1996; private, flexible, but costs can rival litigation) and ombudsman schemes (e.g. Parliamentary and Health Service Ombudsman, Legal Ombudsman, Financial Ombudsman Service — free to users, investigate complaints and can award compensation).

Tribunals (Tribunals, Courts and Enforcement Act 2007) hear disputes between individuals and the state in a two-tier system — e.g. First-tier Tribunal (Social Entitlement Chamber) for benefits, employment tribunals for discrimination and dismissal, with appeals to the Upper Tribunal and beyond. They are cheaper, faster and more specialist than courts, with rights of audience open to non-lawyers, but can still be complex and adversarial.

Access to justice depends on both dispute resolution and funding. The Legal Aid, Sentencing and Punishment of Offenders Act 2012 (LASPO) cut civil legal aid sharply (family, welfare and housing matters largely removed), pushing litigants towards conditional fee agreements (no-win, no-fee, developed after the Access to Justice Act 1999), pro bono work and free advice services (Citizens Advice, law centres). Courts and government now actively encourage ADR before litigation. Evaluation points: ADR is quicker, cheaper, private and less adversarial, but lacks binding precedent, may under-protect weaker parties, and public funding gaps risk justice only for those who can pay — a live constitutional concern (R (Public Law Project) v Lord Chancellor, 2016 — the residence test for legal aid was unlawful). On the other hand, small claims and ombudsman schemes give lay users genuine routes to redress without lawyers, and scarce public funds are arguably best targeted at criminal defence and urgent family cases.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: Priya is suing her former landlord in the County Court for £35,000 in damages for disrepair and personal injury. Separately, her neighbour Theo is charged with unlawful wounding (either-way) after an argument in the street. (a) Explain which track Priya's claim is likely to be allocated and how it could be appealed. (b) Explain how Theo's case will proceed through the criminal courts, including appeal routes, and how the standard of proof differs between his case and Priya's. [OCR H418 Component 1 style scenario; AQA Paper 1 style — 25 marks]
Mark-scheme logic: AO1 — state the law: (a) civil hierarchy — County Court, High Court divisions, Court of Appeal (Civil Division), Supreme Court; track system under CPR 1998: small claims (under £10,000; £1,000 for PI), fast track (£10,000–£25,000), intermediate track (£25,000–£100,000, fixed recoverable costs from 2023), multi-track (complex/high value); appeal routes: next level of judge/High Court, second appeal test (Access to Justice Act 1999 s.55 — important point of principle/practice or compelling reason), leapfrog (Administration of Justice Act 1969). (b) criminal classification — summary, either-way, indictable only; plea before venue and mode of trial; magistrates' powers (6 months per offence; 12 months total); Crown Court for trial before judge and jury; appeals — defendant: s.108 MCA 1980 to Crown Court, case stated s.111 to High Court, Crown Court to CoA Crim Div if unsafe (Criminal Appeal Act 1968); prosecution: no appeal against acquittal, A-G's reference on point of law (CJA 1972 s.36), unduly lenient sentences (CJA 1988 s.36); CCRC (Criminal Appeal Act 1995); standards of proof — beyond reasonable doubt (Woolmington v DPP) for the prosecution, balance of probabilities (Miller v Minister of Pensions) for Priya as claimant. AO2 — apply: Priya's claim is £35,000 including personal injury above £1,000, so small claims is excluded; £35,000 falls in the intermediate track (fixed recoverable costs), appealed to a High Court judge then CoA (Civil Division) only with permission under the s.55 second-appeal test; leapfrog only if a certified point of law arises with consent. Theo's wounding is either-way: he pleads before venue; if he pleads not guilty, magistrates decide mode of trial — likely acceptable and suitable, but given injury severity may send to Crown Court; appeal by defendant via s.108 to Crown Court or case stated to High Court (s.111), or from Crown Court to CoA Crim Div if conviction unsafe. The prosecution must prove guilt beyond reasonable doubt (Woolmington); Priya must prove her claim only on the balance of probabilities (Miller) — Theo's burden is heavier because liberty is at stake. AO3 — evaluate: tracks prioritise proportionality (Woolf aims — cost, speed, fairness); fixed recoverable costs control expense but may squeeze representation; the criminal/civil proof gap prevents inconsistent punishment but can produce apparently conflicting verdicts (O J Simpson); CCRC and relaxed double jeopardy (CJA 2003; R v Dunlop; R v Dobson) balance finality with correcting miscarriages of justice.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Distinguish civil law from criminal law. Refer to purpose, parties, standard of proof and outcomes. [AQA Paper 1, 10 marks]
Answer: Purpose: civil law compensates claimants and vindicates private rights; criminal law punishes and deters behaviour the state condemns. Parties: a claimant sues a defendant privately; the Crown prosecutes a defendant through the CPS. Burden of proof: claimant in civil; prosecution in criminal — the golden thread (Woolmington v DPP). Standard: balance of probabilities (Miller v Minister of Pensions) against beyond reasonable doubt. Outcomes: liable — damages, injunctions, specific performance; guilty — imprisonment, fines, community orders, discharge. Courts differ (County/High Court and CoA Civil Division v magistrates'/Crown Court and CoA Criminal Division). One act can trigger both systems (assault → prosecution for battery plus damages), illustrating that the systems are parallel, not exclusive.
Describe the appeal routes from a magistrates' court and from the Crown Court. [OCR H418 Component 1, 10 marks]
Answer: From the magistrates' court: defendant may appeal against conviction or sentence to the Crown Court (Magistrates' Courts Act 1980 s.108) for a full rehearing, or appeal by case stated to the High Court (KBD divisional court) on a point of law (s.111); the prosecution cannot appeal an acquittal but may refer a point of law (CJA 1972 s.36) or challenge an unduly lenient sentence (CJA 1988 s.36). From the Crown Court: defendant appeals to the Court of Appeal (Criminal Division) against conviction or sentence where unsafe (Criminal Appeal Act 1968); CoA may then allow appeal to the Supreme Court with leave on a point of law of general public importance. The CCRC (Criminal Appeal Act 1995) can refer alleged miscarriages back to CoA, and the CJA 2003 permits retrial for serious offences on compelling new evidence (R v Dunlop; R v Dobson).
Explain how juries are qualified, selected and controlled, and evaluate the idea of jury equity. [WJEC/Eduqas Component 1, 15 marks]
Answer: Qualification (Juries Act 1974): aged 18–74, on the electoral roll, ordinarily resident 5+ years since 13; disqualified if on bail or with relevant convictions/community orders. Selection: random from the electoral roll by the Jury Central Summoning Bureau; vetting only exceptionally (national security). Control: judge directs on law; majority verdicts of 10:1 or 9:1 after 2 hours (s.17) guard against nobbling (Runciman Report 1993). Jury equity: Bushel's Case (1670) — jurors answer to conscience, not punishment; R v Ponting (1985) — acquittal contrary to the judge's direction; no appeal lies against an acquittal. Evaluation: equity keeps law accountable to community conscience and legitimises verdicts, but undermines legal certainty, risks prejudice/emotional decision-making and can produce perverse outcomes; random selection is fair and representative in principle, though excusals and ethnic minority under-representation are persistent criticisms.
Theo is dismissed and claims unfair dismissal. His employer wants arbitration; Theo wants to go to an employment tribunal. Explain arbitration and tribunals, then evaluate which suits Theo better. [OCR H418 Component 1, 20 marks]
Answer: Arbitration: private, binding adjudication under the Arbitration Act 1996 — parties choose the arbitrator, procedure and timescale; the award is enforceable like a judgment; often used where contracts contain arbitration clauses. Tribunals (Tribunals, Courts and Enforcement Act 2007): state-linked disputes heard in a two-tier system (First-tier; Upper Tribunal on appeal); employment tribunals are specialist, cheaper than court, allow non-lawyer representation, and enforce employment rights (ACAS conciliation first). Application: Theo gains publicity, precedent and specialist employment law at a tribunal; enforceable statutory remedies; limited costs risk. Arbitration is faster and private but the employer may choose the arbitrator, there is no precedent, and statutory unfair-dismissal rights may be hard to enforce in a private forum. Evaluation: for an individual against a stronger institution, a tribunal usually better protects access to justice — cheaper, structured, public — though arbitration may suit commercial parties valuing confidentiality and speed.
Evaluate the extent to which LASPO 2012 and cuts to civil legal aid have undermined access to justice. [AQA Paper 1, 25 marks]
Answer: AO1: LASPO 2012 removed legal aid for most family, welfare-benefit, housing and debt matters; Access to Justice Act 1999 replaced civil legal aid with CFAs (no-win no-fee) and controlled conditional costs; funding of representation is part of access to justice (AQA 3.1.1). AO2/analysis: after LASPO, many litigants self-represent ('litigants in person'), family mediation referrals fell initially, advice agencies (law centres, Citizens Advice) faced funding pressure; conditional fees shifted risk to claimants and success fees were restricted, so losing claimants may pay nothing but winning claimants bear unrecovered costs. AO3/evaluation: yes — justice now depends heavily on means; the Supreme Court has struck at restrictions (R (Public Law Project) v Lord Chancellor — residence test unlawful), showing constitutional limits on excluding the poor. However — courts now promote ADR (mediation, ombudsmen) which is genuinely cheaper and faster, small claims allow lay access without lawyers, and public funds are finite; prioritising scarce legal aid on criminal defence and urgent family cases is defensible. Balanced judgement: access is diminished for medium-value private disputes, partially compensated by ADR and no-win-no-fee, but the rule of law requires a floor of funded advice.
