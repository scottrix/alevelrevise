Criminal Law: Actus Reus and Mens Rea

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA, OCR, WJEC/Eduqas

Criminal liability building blocks — actus reus (conduct, omissions, state of affairs), factual and legal causation (R v Pagett, R v Kimsey, R v Cheshire), intention (R v Woollin), recklessness (R v Cunningham, R v G) and strict liability (Gammon) — for AQA 3.2.1, OCR H418 Component 3 and WJEC/Eduqas Components 2-3 (criminal law option).

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Identify and apply the actus reus of an offence, distinguishing conduct, state of affairs and consequence crimes, and the six duty situations where an omission satisfies the actus reus (R v Pittwood; R v Stone & Dobinson; R v Miller) (AQA 3.2.1; OCR H418 Component 3)
  • Apply the two-stage causation test — factual (but-for; R v White) and legal (operating and substantial; R v Pagett; R v Kimsey; R v Cheshire) — including the thin skull rule (R v Blaue) and the victim's own act (R v Kennedy)
  • State the rules of intention — direct (R v Mohan) and oblique (R v Woollin virtual-certainty direction) — and apply them to problem scenarios with the R v Matthews & Alleyne evidential gloss
  • Distinguish subjective recklessness (R v Cunningham; R v G and another) from negligence, and explain the limited role of negligence as mens rea (R v Adomako)
  • Explain the doctrine of transferred malice (R v Latimer; R v Gnango) and its limits (R v Pembliton)
  • Define strict liability, apply the Gammon presumptions, contrast absolute liability (R v Larsonneur) and evaluate the arguments for and against offences of strict liability
  • Deploy the elements, two-stage causation and mens rea ladder accurately in 25-mark AQA problem questions and 20-mark OCR/WJEC scenario questions

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Actus reus: The external, guilty element of a crime: the prohibited act or omission, any prohibited result, and the surrounding circumstances of the offence.
Conduct crime: An offence completed by the doing of the act itself, with no prohibited result required (e.g. assault, dangerous driving under the Road Traffic Act 1988).
State of affairs crime: An offence where liability attaches to being in a prohibited situation, even where D acts involuntarily (R v Larsonneur; Winzar v Chief Constable of Kent).
Omission: A failure to act; generally not criminal, but satisfies the actus reus where a statutory, contractual, relational, assumed-care, danger-created or public-office duty exists (R v Pittwood; R v Gibbins & Proctor; R v Miller; R v Dytham).
Factual causation: The 'but for' test: the result would not have occurred but for D's act; D must also be a substantial and operating cause of the result (R v White; R v Pagett).
Legal causation: D's contribution must be more than minimal ('more than a slight or trifling link' — R v Kimsey) and must remain an operating cause unless a novus actus interveniens breaks the chain (R v Cheshire).
Thin skull rule: D must take the victim as found: pre-existing physical or psychological fragility does not break the chain of causation, even if unforeseeable (R v Blaue; R v Hayward).
Direct intention: The consequence is D's aim or purpose — 'a decision to bring about... the prohibited consequence, no matter whether the accused desired it' (R v Mohan).
Oblique (indirect) intention: Where death or serious injury was a virtual certainty as a result of D's act and D appreciated that, the jury may find intention (R v Woollin; R v Matthews & Alleyne).
Recklessness: D consciously takes an unjustifiable risk, knowing the risk exists or will exist — the subjective Cunningham test restored across the criminal law by R v G and another (2003).
Transferred malice: Intention or recklessness towards one victim transfers to the actual victim of the same kind of offence (R v Latimer; R v Gnango); different kinds of malice do not transfer (R v Pembliton).
Strict liability: An offence requiring no proof of mens rea for at least one element, found only where the Gammon presumptions are displaced; absolute liability (R v Larsonneur) admits no defence of any kind.

Actus reus — conduct, state of affairs and omissions (AQA 3.2.1; OCR H418 Component 3; WJEC/Eduqas Components 2-3)

The actus reus is the external, guilty element of an offence: the prohibited act, any prohibited result and the surrounding circumstances. The shape it takes dictates what the prosecution must prove.

  • Conduct crimes — the act itself completes the offence, no result needed: assault, dangerous driving (Road Traffic Act 1988), perjury.
  • Consequence (result) crimes — a prohibited result must be caused: murder (death of a human being in being under the Queen's peace), s.47 OAPA 1861 ABH, criminal damage (Criminal Damage Act 1971, s.1). Causation must be proved.
  • State of affairs crimes — liability attaches to a situation, even without a voluntary act: R v Larsonneur (1933) (deported from England but returned in handcuffs; being 'found' in the UK completed the offence); Winzar v Chief Constable of Kent (1983) (found drunk on hospital premises and carried to the highway). Harsh, but valid — no mens rea required.

Omissions. The baseline rule is that there is no liability for failing to act — English law imposes no general duty of rescue. An omission satisfies the actus reus only where a duty to act exists, from one of six sources:

  • Statutory duty — Children and Young Persons Act 1933, s.1 (neglect of a child); Local Government Act 1972 duties.
  • Contractual duty — R v Pittwood (1902) (railway gatekeeper left the gate open; a haycart was hit by a train).
  • Duty from a relationship — R v Gibbins & Proctor (1918) (father and his partner starved the child to death).
  • Voluntarily assumed duty of care — R v Stone & Dobinson (1977) (couple allowed D's anorexic sister, Fanny, to deteriorate; attempts to help insufficient).
  • Duty from creating a dangerous situation — R v Miller (1983) (squatter's cigarette set the mattress alight; the duty to counteract the danger constituted the actus reus); R v Evans (2009) (heroin supplier owed a duty to call an ambulance for the sister who overdosed).
  • Duty through public office — R v Dytham (1979) (off-duty officer watched a fatal beating and drove away).

Coincidence of actus reus and mens rea. Both elements must coincide in time, but the courts are pragmatic: Fagan v Metropolitan Police Commissioner (1969) (car parked on the officer's foot; the later-formed intention to leave it there made the actus reus a continuing act over which mens rea coincided); R v Thabo Meli (1954) (a sequence of acts treated as a single transaction).

Quick recall — the case bank for the actus reus
  • State of affairs: Larsonneur (1933); Winzar v CC Kent (1983).
  • Omissions duties: Pittwood (contract); Gibbins & Proctor (relationship); Stone & Dobinson (assumed care); Miller and Evans (danger created); Dytham (office); Children and Young Persons Act 1933, s.1 (statutory).
  • Continuing act: Fagan v MPC (1969); R v Thabo Meli (1954).
  • Consequence crimes: murder; s.47, s.20 OAPA 1861; Criminal Damage Act 1971, s.1.

Causation in fact and in law (R v White, R v Pagett, R v Kimsey, R v Cheshire)

Consequence crimes require proof that D caused the prohibited result — a two-stage test.

Stage 1: factual causation — the 'but for' test. Would the result have occurred but for D's conduct? R v White (1910): D poisoned his mother's drink, but she died of a heart attack — no factual causation of death, so only attempted murder succeeded. 'But for' alone is not enough where there are multiple contributing causes: R v Pagett (1983): D used his pregnant girlfriend as a human shield while shooting at armed police, who returned fire and killed her — D's acts must be the substantial and operating cause of the result; she would not have died but for his unlawful acts, and police fire was a response to his act, not an independent cause.

Stage 2: legal causation. D's contribution must be more than minimal: R v Kimsey (1996): death on a high-speed race — the link must be more than a slight or trifling one (the de minimis principle). D need not be the sole or main cause so long as his act remains operating and substantial at the point of death.

Intervening acts (novus actus interveniens) may break the chain of causation:

  • Medical treatment — negligent treatment does not break the chain unless it is independent of D's acts and in itself so potent a cause of death: R v Cheshire (1991) (tracheotomy scarring narrowed the windpipe; the original gunshot wounds remained the operating cause — conviction upheld); R v Malcherek & Steel (1981) (switching off life support after brain death is never a novus actus). The narrow exception is R v Jordan (1956): 'palpably wrong' treatment — the wound had nearly healed and wrongly prescribed drugs plus excessive intravenous fluids caused death.
  • Victim's own condition — the thin skull rule: take the victim as found, physical or psychological, even where unforeseeable: R v Blaue (1975) (Jehovah's Witness refused a blood transfusion after a stabbing; D still caused her death); R v Hayward (1908) (threats caused the wife to run, aggravating a latent heart condition — no break).
  • Victim's reaction to D's act — must be a proportionate and foreseeable response: R v Roberts (1971) (passenger jumped from a moving car to escape sexual advances — injuries caused by D); R v Williams & Davis (1992) (jury must find the reaction was within the range of responses to be expected — hitchhiker leapt at 30mph).
  • Free, deliberate and informed act of the victim breaks the chain: R v Kennedy (No 2) (2007): supplying heroin to a fully informed adult who freely self-injected was not unlawful act manslaughter. Contrast R v Wallace (2018): whether the victim's suicide after an acid attack broke the chain was a matter for the jury.
Quick recall — causation cases
  • But-for: R v White (1910).
  • Substantial and operating: R v Pagett (1983).
  • De minimis: R v Kimsey (1996).
  • Medical: R v Cheshire (1991); R v Malcherek & Steel (1981); exception — R v Jordan (1956).
  • Thin skull: R v Blaue (1975); R v Hayward (1908).
  • Victim's acts: R v Roberts (1971); R v Williams & Davis (1992); R v Kennedy (No 2) (2007); R v Wallace (2018) — suicide.

Mens rea — intention, recklessness, negligence and transferred malice (R v Mohan, R v Woollin, R v Cunningham, R v G)

The mens rea is the fault element the offence requires: intention, recklessness, negligence or knowledge. For murder it is intention (to kill or to cause grievous bodily harm) alone.

Direct intention. The consequence is D's aim or purpose: R v Mohan (1976): 'a decision to bring about, in so far as it lies within the accused's power, the prohibited consequence, no matter whether the accused desired that consequence'. Motive is irrelevant to liability.

Oblique (indirect) intention. Covers consequences D did not aim at but foresaw. The modern authority is R v Woollin (1998): where death or serious injury was a virtual certainty (barring some unforeseen intervention) as a result of D's act and D appreciated that was the case, the jury is not obliged but entitled to find intention. R v Matthews & Alleyne (2003): Woollin lays down a rule of evidence, not substantive law — foresight of a virtual certainty is evidence from which the jury may infer intention. This supersedes the earlier Hyam (1976), Moloney (1985) and Nedrick (1986) probability guidelines.

Recklessness — the Cunningham (subjective) test. D is reckless where he knows an unjustifiable risk exists or will exist and takes it anyway: R v Cunningham (1957) (tore a gas meter from the wall; gas partially asphyxiated a sleeping woman). The objective Caldwell (1982) version — risk obvious to the reasonable person — was overruled in R v G and another (2003): two boys aged 11 and 12 set fire to newspapers behind a shop; the House of Lords held it unjust to convict where the risk was not obvious to them. Subjective recklessness now governs the Criminal Damage Act 1971, s.1 and the 'Cunningham crimes' (assault, s.47, s.20).

Negligence. An objective failure to meet the standard of the reasonable person. In the general criminal law negligence is a low-grade fault; it matters chiefly for gross negligence manslaughter: R v Adomako (1994): (1) a duty of care to the victim, (2) breach of that duty causing death, and (3) the breach so bad as to be criminal (risk of death obvious or serious).

Transferred malice. Intention or recklessness towards one victim transfers to the actual victim of the same kind of offence: R v Latimer (1886) (swung a belt, missed the man, hit a woman). The Supreme Court extended the doctrine in R v Gnango (2011) (mutual gunfight; each shooter's intention transferred). Limit: it cannot transfer between different kinds of offence — R v Pembliton (1874) (threw a stone at people, hit a window; no transfer from assault to criminal damage).

Quick recall — the mens rea ladder
  • Direct: R v Mohan (1976) — aim/purpose.
  • Oblique: R v Woollin (1998) — virtual certainty + appreciation; jury may find intent.
  • Evidential gloss: R v Matthews & Alleyne (2003).
  • Recklessness: R v Cunningham (1957); R v G (2003) overruled Caldwell — subjective throughout.
  • Negligence: R v Adomako (1994) — gross negligence manslaughter only.
  • Transferred malice: R v Latimer (1886); R v Gnango (2011); limit — R v Pembliton (1874).

Strict and absolute liability (Gammon; AQA 3.2.1)

Some offences require no mens rea for one or more elements of the actus reus: strict liability. Most are regulatory — food hygiene, licensing, health and safety, transport — and carry modest penalties; strict liability attaches to 'true' crimes only if Parliament says so expressly. The starting point is the presumption of mens rea (actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea).

Gammon Ltd v A-G for Hong Kong (1985): mens rea is presumed unless the statute expressly or by necessary implication rules it out; the presumption of strict liability is stronger where (1) the offence is regulatory rather than a true crime, (2) the statute is concerned with an issue of social concern (public safety, food hygiene), (3) strict liability will further the enforcement of the statute by encouraging greater vigilance, and (4) the penalty is not severe.

  • Presumption of mens rea upheld: Sweet v Parsley (1970) (landlady 'concerned in the management' of premises used for cannabis smoking — knowledge required); B (A Minor) v DPP (1995) (similar presumption for a serious offence involving the age of the victim — later reversed by statute: the Sexual Offences Act 2003 made age largely strict).
  • Strict liability found: Harrow LBC v Shah (2000) (selling a lottery ticket to an under-16; the shopkeeper was convicted despite staff training and notices — the protective purpose displaced mens rea).
  • Absolute liability — no mens rea of any kind, and no defence even where D acts involuntarily: R v Larsonneur (1933); Winzar v Chief Constable of Kent (1983); Pharmaceutical Society of GB v Storkwain (1986) (forged prescriptions); Alphacell Ltd v Woodward (1972) (pollution without knowledge).
Arguments for strict liabilityArguments against
Protects the public in areas of social concern (food, water, licensing, safety)Convicts the blameless (Shah; Storkwain) — offends the principle that liability requires fault
Easier enforcement — no need to prove a mental state that is hard to evidenceNo deterrent effect where the defendant is unaware of the risk
Encourages greater vigilance and better systems (Gammon)Blurs the criminal/civil boundary; stigma disproportionate to the penalty
Raises the profile of the offence and deters cutting cornersR v G restored the fault principle for criminal damage — the trend is back towards mens rea

Exam technique. Strict liability is a favourite AQA 25-mark evaluate: anchor the answer on the Gammon presumptions, contrast Sweet v Parsley with Harrow LBC v Shah, deploy R v G as the fault-based critique and Larsonneur/Storkwain as the absolute-liability extreme, and finish with a judgement — strict liability is justified for regulatory offences protecting social welfare, but Parliament should signal it expressly and penalties should stay proportionate.

Exam technique — building a liability answer (AQA 3.2.1; OCR H418 Component 3)

Problem questions (AQA 25-mark; OCR/WJEC 20-mark scenario). Structure every answer the same way:

  1. Define the offence elements (AO1) — identify the shape of the actus reus and the mens rea required.
  2. Apply element by element (AO2) — conduct, omission or state of affairs; then causation in fact (R v White) then in law (R v Pagett; R v Kimsey); then mens rea in order (R v Mohan; R v Woollin; R v Cunningham; R v G), applying authorities to the facts rather than describing them.
  3. Run the counter-cases (AO2/AO3) — omission duties, novus actus candidates (R v Cheshire; R v Jordan; R v Roberts; R v Kennedy), the thin skull rule (R v Blaue) and transferred malice (R v Latimer).
  4. Conclude liability per defendant — the mark scheme rewards a definite conclusion with reasons, not 'it depends'.

Essay questions (AQA evaluate, 25 marks). On strict liability or oblique intention: state the law (Gammon; Woollin), evaluate fault, certainty and fairness (R v G), give a reform direction, and finish with a reasoned judgement. A* answers quote the Woollin formula precisely and use cases as evidence for the evaluation, never as a bare list.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: Devon, a qualified marksman, aims a rifle at a scarecrow in his garden. He knows that the bullet will pass through the scarecrow and land in a field where his neighbour, Kai, often walks, but decides to fire anyway, betting that the field is empty. The bullet hits Kai, who dies in hospital despite negligent treatment from a junior doctor. Discuss Devon's criminal liability for the death of Kai. [AQA 7162 Paper 1 style problem question, 25 marks]
Mark-scheme logic: AO1 (law) + AO2 (application) + AO3 (evaluation) the answer must hit: <strong>Actus reus</strong> — murder requires the unlawful killing of a human being in being under the Queen's peace; Devon's firing is a conduct element and Kai's death the consequence element. <strong>Factual causation</strong> — the but-for test (R v White): Kai would not have died but for Devon's act. <strong>Legal causation</strong> — Devon must be a substantial and operating cause (R v Pagett) and more than a slight or trifling link (R v Kimsey); the negligent hospital treatment does not break the chain unless it is so independent of Devon's acts and in itself so potent a cause of death (R v Cheshire; R v Malcherek & Steel; contrast R v Jordan for palpably wrong treatment). <strong>Mens rea</strong> — direct intention (R v Mohan) is absent because Devon did not aim at Kai; oblique intention turns on R v Woollin: was Kai's death a virtual certainty of firing into a field and did Devon appreciate that? If yes, the jury may find intention (R v Matthews & Alleyne — foresight as evidence); if the risk was merely probable, intention is not made out and recklessness (R v Cunningham; R v G) is insufficient for murder, so liability falls to unlawful act manslaughter or a s.20 OAPA 1861 analysis. <strong>Conclusion</strong> — a definite verdict on murder (likely made out on the Woollin route if virtual certainty is found) with reasons, not 'it depends'. A* answers apply each authority to the facts in one or two sentences and evaluate the Woollin virtual-certainty standard (certainty vs probability) before concluding.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Explain the circumstances in which an omission can satisfy the actus reus of a criminal offence. Refer to at least three duty situations. [AQA Paper 1, 10 marks]
Answer: AO1: The general rule is that there is no liability for failing to act — English law imposes no duty of rescue. An omission satisfies the actus reus only where a duty to act exists. AO2 (cases): (1) contractual duty — R v Pittwood (1902), the railway gatekeeper who left the gate open; (2) duty from a relationship — R v Gibbins & Proctor (1918), the father and his partner who starved the child; (3) voluntarily assumed duty of care — R v Stone & Dobinson (1977), the couple who allowed the anorexic sister to deteriorate; (4) duty from creating a dangerous situation — R v Miller (1983), the squatter whose cigarette set the mattress alight, and R v Evans (2009), the heroin supplier who failed to call an ambulance; (5) statutory duty — Children and Young Persons Act 1933, s.1; (6) public office — R v Dytham (1979). AO3/conclusion: liability for omissions is therefore confined to defined duty situations, balancing individual liberty against protection of the vulnerable; outside those duties there is no criminal liability for doing nothing.
Nina stabs Rex in the thigh with a kitchen knife. At hospital a junior doctor fails to diagnose internal bleeding for three days; by the time surgery is performed Rex has developed pneumonia and dies. Rex had also refused an early transfusion on religious grounds. Discuss Nina's criminal liability for the death of Rex. [AQA 7162 Paper 1 style problem question, 25 marks]
Answer: AO1: Murder requires an unlawful killing with intention to kill or cause GBH. AO2: <strong>Actus reus</strong> — a consequence crime; the prosecution must prove Nina caused Rex's death. <strong>Factual causation</strong> — but-for (R v White): Rex would not have died but for the stabbing. <strong>Legal causation</strong> — the stab wound must be the substantial and operating cause (R v Pagett) and more than a slight or trifling link (R v Kimsey). <strong>Medical negligence</strong> — the three-day delay and the pneumonia are unlikely to break the chain: negligent treatment must be so independent of Nina's acts and in itself so potent a cause of death (R v Cheshire; R v Malcherek & Steel); contrast R v Jordan, the narrow exception for palpably wrong treatment, and argue the facts either way. <strong>Thin skull rule</strong> — Rex's religious refusal of a transfusion does not break causation: take the victim as found (R v Blaue). <strong>Mens rea</strong> — a deliberate stabbing is direct intention (R v Mohan); if Nina merely saw a risk, recklessness is insufficient for murder (R v G). <strong>Conclusion</strong> — Nina is likely liable for murder: factual and legal causation are made out despite the medical negligence, and intention is present.
Tessa is due to give evidence against Zane, who blocks the only exit of her home knowing that firefighters have said anyone inside will 'almost certainly' die within minutes. Discuss whether Zane has the mens rea for Tessa's death. [AQA 7162 Paper 1 style problem question, 25 marks]
Answer: AO1: <strong>Direct intention</strong> — the consequence is D's aim or purpose: 'a decision to bring about the prohibited consequence' (R v Mohan). <strong>Oblique (indirect) intention</strong> — where death or serious injury was a virtual certainty as a result of D's act and D appreciated that, the jury may find intention (R v Woollin). AO2: Zane does not desire Tessa's death, so direct intention is absent. But blocking the exit when he knows anyone inside will almost certainly die satisfies the Woollin formula: death was a virtual certainty and he appreciated it, so the jury is entitled to find intention — the jury <em>may</em>, not must. AO3: <em>R v Matthews & Alleyne</em> confirms Woollin is a rule of evidence, not substantive law — foresight of a virtual certainty is evidence of intention, not intention itself, so a jury could still acquit on the facts. Where the risk was merely probable (rather than virtually certain), intention is not made out and recklessness (R v Cunningham; R v G) would not support murder. <strong>Conclusion</strong> — Zane likely has the mens rea for murder via the Woollin virtual-certainty route.
'Offences of strict liability are unjust because they punish the blameless.' Evaluate this statement. Refer to Gammon, Sweet v Parsley, Harrow LBC v Shah and R v G and another. [AQA Paper 1, 25 marks]
Answer: AO1: Strict liability requires no mens rea for at least one element; the presumption is mens rea (<em>actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea</em>), displaced only on the Gammon (1985) indicators — a regulatory (not true) crime, an issue of social concern such as public safety, enforcement furthered by greater vigilance, and non-severe penalties. AO3 (against the statement / for strict liability): strict liability protects the public in food hygiene, licensing and safety; it is easier to enforce because states of mind are hard to prove; it encourages higher vigilance and better systems (Gammon). AO3 (for the statement / against): Harrow LBC v Shah (2000) convicted a shopkeeper with staff training and notices — blameless; absolute liability goes further (R v Larsonneur (1933); Pharmaceutical Society v Storkwain (1986)), punishing conduct D could not control; there is no deterrent value without awareness of the risk; the stigma is disproportionate to the penalty. AO3 (the counter-trend): R v G and another (2003) restored the fault principle for criminal damage, overruling Caldwell, and Sweet v Parsley (1970) shows courts resisting strict liability for serious offences — the courts themselves police the boundary. <strong>Judgement</strong>: the statement is too absolute — strict liability is justified where Parliament targets regulatory offences protecting social welfare, provided it signals this expressly and the penalty stays proportionate; the injustice lies in applying it to true crimes or the blameless, which the Gammon approach and Sweet v Parsley already guard against.
Explain the significance of R v G and another (2003) for the law of recklessness. [AQA Paper 1, 10 marks]
Answer: AO1: <strong>Background</strong> — R v Cunningham (1957) made recklessness subjective: D must see the risk and take it unjustifiably; R v Caldwell (1982) made it objective for criminal damage — a risk obvious to the reasonable person sufficed. <strong>Facts</strong> — two boys aged 11 and 12 set fire to newspapers behind a shop; the fire spread and destroyed the shop. AO2: the House of Lords <strong>overruled Caldwell</strong> and restored the Cunningham subjective test: D must be aware of a risk that exists or will exist (Criminal Damage Act 1971, s.1). AO3: Lord Bingham held the objective test unjust — it could convict a defendant who never appreciated a risk obvious to others; it offended the principle that criminal liability requires fault (<em>actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea</em>); deterrence fails without awareness; and it was harsh on children and vulnerable adults. <strong>Conclusion</strong> — subjective recklessness now governs the criminal law throughout, and R v G is the leading authority on the fault principle.
