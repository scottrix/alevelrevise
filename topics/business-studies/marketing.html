Marketing

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC/Eduqas

Marketing mix (7Ps), market research, segmentation, targeting and positioning, pricing strategies with price elasticity, the product life cycle, Ansoff matrix and digital marketing — A* revision for AQA 7132 3.3, Edexcel Themes 1 and OCR H431.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Set and justify marketing objectives — sales volume and value, market share, market growth, brand loyalty — and analyse the market using size, growth and share data (AQA 3.3.1)
  • Evaluate market research methods — primary and secondary, quantitative and qualitative — including sampling, cost and the reliability of data in dynamic markets (AQA 3.3.2)
  • Apply segmentation (geographic, demographic, psychographic, behavioural), targeting (mass versus niche) and positioning, including market mapping and USPs (AQA 3.3.3; Edexcel 1.1)
  • Apply and evaluate the extended marketing mix (7Ps) — product, price, place, promotion, people, process, physical evidence — including pricing strategies and distribution channels (AQA 3.3.3; Edexcel 1.2)
  • Analyse the product life cycle, extension strategies and the Ansoff matrix, and the risk gradient between its four strategies (AQA 3.3 and 3.8; Edexcel 1.2)
  • Calculate and interpret price elasticity of demand and use it to inform pricing decisions (AQA 3.3.2)
  • Evaluate digital marketing — SEO, PPC, social media, email and e-commerce — against traditional channels

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Market segmentation: Dividing a market into distinct groups of buyers with common characteristics or needs — geographic, demographic, psychographic or behavioural — so that marketing can be targeted at each group.
Market positioning: How consumers perceive a product relative to competing products, often analysed on a market map plotting two attributes (for example price against quality).
Market mapping: A diagram plotting products in a market against two key attributes to identify positioning of competitors and possible gaps in the market.
Unique Selling Point (USP): The distinctive feature or benefit of a product that differentiates it from competitors and gives consumers a reason to buy it.
Price elasticity of demand (PED): The responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in price: the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price.
Penetration pricing: Setting a low initial price to gain market share quickly and discourage competitors, usually raising prices once a customer base is established.
Price skimming: Setting a high initial price for a new or innovative product to maximise revenue from early adopters and recover development costs, lowering the price over time.
Product life cycle (PLC): The stages a product passes through — development, introduction, growth, maturity and decline — each with different sales, profit and cash-flow characteristics.
Extension strategy: Marketing action taken to prolong the maturity stage of the product life cycle, such as new versions, new markets, new uses, repositioning or price changes.
Ansoff matrix: A two-by-two model of growth strategies plotting existing/new products against existing/new markets: market penetration, product development, market development and diversification (in ascending order of risk).

Marketing objectives, market analysis and market research (AQA 3.3.1–3.3.2; Edexcel 1.1; OCR H431)

Marketing objectives must derive from corporate objectives. Common ones: sales volume (units) and sales value (revenue), market share (firm sales ÷ market sales × 100), market growth, brand loyalty/awareness and — in some specifications — customer satisfaction. Objectives should be measurable and realistic: 'increase market share from 12% to 15% within two years' allows performance to be judged.

Market analysis uses market size, growth rate, market share and trends. A growing market offers easier growth (the tide lifts all boats); a static or declining market makes share gains the only route — which intensifies rivalry and usually requires promotion or price competitiveness.

Market research gathers the data behind these decisions:

  • Primary (field) research — data collected first-hand: surveys, questionnaires, interviews, focus groups, observation, test marketing. Up-to-date and specific to the firm's needs, but costly, time-consuming and subject to sample bias.
  • Secondary (desk) research — existing data: government statistics (ONS), market reports, competitor accounts, internet data. Cheap and fast, but may be outdated, generalised or collected for another purpose.
  • Quantitative — numerical data (survey scores, sales figures): easy to analyse statistically but shallow on reasons. Qualitative — opinions and motives (focus groups, interviews): explains why consumers behave as they do, but small samples limit generalisation.

Sampling — random, quota or stratified — lets firms survey a representative subset rather than the whole market. Random sampling avoids bias but may miss target customers; quota sampling guarantees representation of key groups but risks interviewer bias. Limitations to evaluate: cost versus value of information, sample size and bias, respondent dishonesty, and — critically — dynamic markets: research is a snapshot in markets where tastes shift quickly (fashion, technology), so data can be obsolete before it is used. The best answers weigh the reliability of the data against the risk of the decision it informs.

Segmentation, targeting and positioning (STP) (AQA 3.3.3; Edexcel 1.1; OCR H431)

Segmentation divides the market so that marketing matches buyer needs. Four bases:

  • Geographic — by location: urban versus rural, region, climate (a surfwear brand targets coastal regions).
  • Demographic — by population characteristics: age, gender, income, occupation (premium models target high incomes; family cars target parents).
  • Psychographic — by lifestyle, values and personality: environmentally conscious 'green' consumers, thrill-seekers, status-seekers.
  • Behavioural — by usage and buying behaviour: heavy users, first-time buyers, brand loyalty, benefits sought (convenience versus quality).

Effective segments are measurable, accessible, sizeable and distinct. Targeting then chooses which segment(s) to serve: mass marketing (one product for the whole market — economies of scale, but intense competition and generic appeal) versus niche marketing (a specialised segment — less competition, premium pricing, customer loyalty, but limited sales volume and vulnerability if the niche shrinks or attracts larger rivals).

Positioning establishes how the product is perceived relative to rivals — typically on a market map (price against quality, or any two attributes). Gaps in the map reveal unmet positions (a gap for 'high quality, low price' may be an opportunity — or may simply show that position is unprofitable). Positioning is delivered through the USP and consistent branding: Volvo owns 'safety', Aldi owns 'value'. Added value — the difference between the price charged and the cost of inputs — is created by branding, quality, convenience and service, and it is what allows premium pricing. Worked application: a new energy drink targeting 18–25 males segments demographically (age, gender), psychographically (active, risk-taking) and behaviourally (gym-goers); pricing at £1.50 penetrates the market; promotion via social media influencers and gym sponsorships reaches the segment cheaply — every element of the mix flows from the segment chosen, which is the analytical point examiners reward.

The extended marketing mix: 7Ps (AQA 3.3.3; Edexcel 1.2; OCR H431)

The marketing mix is the combination of marketing decisions — the 4Ps plus three service elements. Consistency matters: a premium price with poor packaging confuses positioning.

Pricing strategies — know the mechanics and the risk of each:

  • Penetration — low initial price gains share fast; hard to raise later, may signal low quality, thin margins.
  • Skimming — high initial price harvests early adopters and recovers R&D; attracts competitors, slows volume.
  • Psychological — £9.99 reads as £9; aspirational pricing supports premium images.
  • Competitive — matching rivals' prices; sensible in price-sensitive markets with similar products, but ignites price wars if all follow.
  • Cost-plus — unit cost plus a mark-up; simple and guarantees margin per unit, but ignores demand, competitors and elasticity.
  • Dynamic — real-time adjustment to demand and availability (airline seats, Uber surge, concert tickets); maximises revenue but risks customer resentment.
  • Loss leader — selling below cost to attract footfall that buys profitable items; unsustainable if customers only buy the loss-maker.

Product life cycle, extension strategies and the Ansoff matrix (AQA 3.3 and 3.8; Edexcel 1.2; OCR H431)

The product life cycle tracks sales over time: introduction (slow sales, heavy promotion spend, negative or low cash flow), growth (rapid sales growth, rising profit, competitors enter), maturity (peak sales, price competition, strong positive cash flow), decline (falling sales as tastes and technology move on — DVDs, iPods). Cash-flow implications matter for analysis: growth-stage products consume investment; mature products generate the cash that funds them — the logic behind the Boston Matrix.

Extension strategies prolong maturity: new versions and flavours, new markets (exporting), new uses, repositioning to a new segment, promotional refresh or price adjustments. Coca-Cola is the classic example — over a century of maturity sustained by continuous extension.

The Ansoff matrix maps growth strategies by risk:

  • Market penetration (existing product, existing market) — lowest risk: promotion, loyalty schemes, price tactics to win share from rivals. Limited if the market is saturated.
  • Product development (new product, existing market) — moderate risk: leverages brand loyalty but demands R&D and innovation; failure wastes investment (New Coke).
  • Market development (existing product, new market) — moderate risk: exporting or new segments; leverages the proven product but faces unfamiliar competitors, cultures and regulation.
  • Diversification (new product, new market) — highest risk: no experience of either dimension, but spreads risk across markets; related diversification (synergy) is safer than unrelated (conglomerate).

Evaluation: risk rises as the firm moves further from what it knows, because research is less reliable for new products and markets. But low-risk penetration in a declining market can be the worst strategy — risk must be weighed against reward and market conditions, not minimised blindly. The Ansoff matrix also ignores competition: a 'low-risk' penetration strategy in a market dominated by a powerful incumbent may be effectively impossible.

Price elasticity of demand and digital marketing (AQA 3.3.2–3.3.3; Edexcel 1.2; OCR H431)

Price elasticity of demand (PED) = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price. Worked example: a price rise from £4.00 to £4.40 (a 10% increase) causes sales to fall from 2,000 to 1,700 units (a 15% fall). PED = −15 ÷ 10 = −1.5 — elastic (ignoring the sign, greater than 1). Interpretation: where demand is elastic (|PED| > 1), a price rise reduces revenue (the quantity loss outweighs the price gain) — so cut price to grow revenue; where demand is inelastic (|PED| < 1), a price rise increases revenue. Elastic demand is typical of price-sensitive markets with close substitutes and non-essential products; inelastic demand of necessity products, strong brands and loyal customers (petrol, insulin, iPhone loyalists). Uses: PED informs pricing strategy, forecasting revenue effects of price changes, and — for retailers — loss-leader decisions. Limitations: elasticity is an estimate based on past data and assumes other factors are constant (ceteris paribus); it varies over time, along the demand curve and between segments, so a single figure is unreliable.

Digital marketing uses online channels to reach and convert customers:

  • SEO (search engine optimisation) — structuring content to rank organically in search results; slow to build but free per click and high-trust.
  • PPC (pay-per-click advertising) — paid search and display ads; instant, measurable and targetable, but costs scale with competition.
  • Social media — organic content and paid campaigns; cheap reach, precise targeting by demographics and interests, influencer partnerships; risks of viral backlashes and platform dependence.
  • Email — direct, personalised, near-zero marginal cost; excellent for retention, but depends on an opted-in list.
  • E-commerce — selling online removes geographic limits, cuts property costs and generates rich behavioural data, but faces intense price comparison, delivery/logistics costs and digital exclusion of some segments.

Evaluation: digital offers measurable, targeted, low-cost reach — its data also feeds segmentation (behavioural) and dynamic pricing. But traditional ATL still builds mass brand awareness faster, and the best promotion mixes blend channels by budget, market type and product stage. Strong answers justify the mix against the context: budget, target segment, competition and life-cycle stage.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: BloomTech, a UK smartphone manufacturer with a strong premium brand, is launching a new flagship model. It can either (a) launch in the UK with an innovative sustainable-materials handset at a high price (product development), or (b) launch its existing proven handset range into the Indian market at a lower price point (market development). Evaluate which Ansoff strategy BloomTech should pursue. [AQA 7132 Paper 2 style, 25 marks]
Mark-scheme logic: <strong>Knowledge (AO1):</strong> The Ansoff matrix plots existing/new products against existing/new markets. Product development sells new products to existing customers; market development sells existing products to new markets; both are moderate-risk strategies below diversification and above penetration. <strong>Application (AO2):</strong> BloomTech's premium brand and loyal customer base support product development — the sustainable handset targets existing buyers whose loyalty reduces launch risk, and the premium price reflects an inelastic customer base. Market development leverages a proven product but at a lower price point in India — unfamiliar competitors (Xiaomi, Realme compete aggressively on price), price-sensitive consumers (elastic demand) and a different distribution structure (online-first retail) all raise risk. <strong>Analysis (AO3):</strong> Product development recovers R&D through the premium segment and strengthens the brand's innovative image — but sustainable materials raise unit costs, and if green values do not translate into purchase behaviour, sales disappoint while competitors copy the innovation. Market development opens a vast, growing market and extends the life cycle of proven products — but the lower price point erodes the premium margin, invites price competition BloomTech cannot win profitably, and market research for a new country is less reliable than data from its existing customer base. <strong>Evaluation (AO4):</strong> The decisive criteria are where BloomTech's competitive advantage is strongest and how reliable its information is. Its advantage — brand loyalty and premium positioning — lies with existing customers, and it holds richer data on them; product development therefore carries the lower <em>effective</em> risk despite equal Ansoff risk. Market development at a lower price point conflicts with the brand's premium positioning and would likely fail against established price-competitors. Conclusion: product development, <em>if</em> BloomTech validates demand for sustainable handsets through test marketing first; the Indian market remains a later, better-executed opportunity once it can compete on brand rather than price.</p>

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Explain how a car manufacturer could segment the UK market for electric vehicles. [6 marks]
Answer: Geographic: urban buyers with good charging infrastructure versus rural buyers with limited charge points. Demographic: income segmentation separates premium models (e.g. Tesla, £50,000+) from affordable models (e.g. Nissan Leaf). Psychographic: environmentally conscious 'green' lifestyles who buy on values rather than price. Behavioural: high-mileage commuters and company fleet buyers with heavy usage rates. Application: segmentation allows the manufacturer to match distinct models, price points and promotional messages to each group, increasing the effectiveness of marketing spend.
A smartphone manufacturer is launching a new flagship phone. Evaluate whether it should use penetration pricing or price skimming. [10 marks]
Answer: Skimming: a high launch price targets early adopters, recovers heavy R&D costs quickly and reinforces a premium brand image, as Apple does with each iPhone generation. Risk: it attracts competitors and slows unit volume. Penetration: a low price builds market share fast and exploits economies of scale. Risk: prices are hard to raise later without damaging loyalty, a low price may signal poor quality, and margins stay thin while fixed costs (R&D) are enormous. Evaluation depends on brand strength, price elasticity of demand and competitive intensity: for an established premium brand with loyal customers and strong differentiation, skimming is likely to maximise long-run profit, whereas a new entrant with no reputation would favour penetration to gain traction.
Justify a promotional mix for a newly opened local bakery with a marketing budget of £2,000. [9 marks]
Answer: Below-the-line and digital focus: free in-store sampling to build trial, leaflets distributed locally for cheap targeted reach, Instagram/Facebook posts with an opening-week giveaway for low-cost community engagement, and a loyalty card to drive repeat purchases. Justification: a £2,000 budget rules out above-the-line promotion (TV/radio), the target market is geographically local, and digital channels offer measurable engagement at near-zero cost. Evaluation: reach is limited and word-of-mouth builds slowly, so the bakery should offset this through partnerships with local cafes and encouraging reviews on Google Maps to strengthen local search visibility.
A retailer raises the price of a product from £20 to £22 (a 10% increase) and sales fall from 5,000 to 4,250 units (a 15% fall). Calculate the price elasticity of demand and advise whether the price rise increased revenue. [6 marks]
Answer: PED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price = −15% ÷ +10% = −1.5. Demand is elastic (greater than 1 in magnitude). Revenue before: £20 × 5,000 = £100,000. Revenue after: £22 × 4,250 = £93,500. Revenue fell by £6,500, confirming that with elastic demand a price rise reduces revenue. Advice: the retailer should not raise price further; with elastic demand it should consider price cuts or non-price competition (quality, service) to grow revenue, and investigate why demand is so price-sensitive (close substitutes).
Explain two extension strategies a manufacturer of a mature chocolate bar could use to prolong the product life cycle. [6 marks]
Answer: AO1: An extension strategy is marketing action taken to prolong the maturity stage, when sales peak and then fall. Strategy 1 — product modification: new flavours, limited editions or recipe changes (e.g. sustainable or premium ingredients) create renewed interest and press coverage. Strategy 2 — market development: exporting the bar to new countries or repositioning it towards a new segment (e.g. premium gifting at higher price points) opens new demand without abandoning the proven product. Evaluation point: extensions are cheaper than new product development because the brand already exists, but they delay rather than prevent decline if tastes ultimately move on.
Evaluate the limitations of market research when making marketing decisions in a dynamic market. [12 marks]
Answer: AO1: Market research gathers primary (surveys, focus groups) and secondary (statistics, reports) data to inform decisions. AO2/3: Limitations — samples may be too small or biased (quota sampling risks interviewer bias; respondents give socially desirable answers); primary research is costly, delaying decisions in fast-moving markets; secondary data may be outdated or collected for another purpose. In dynamic markets (fashion, technology) research is a snapshot: tastes shift between data collection and product launch, making findings obsolete — several famous product failures (e.g. New Coke) followed apparently positive research. Yet decisions made with no research are riskier still: research reduces but never eliminates uncertainty, and its value must exceed its cost. Evaluation: the usefulness of research depends on the market's rate of change, the sample's quality and the decision's stakes — in dynamic markets firms should combine research with small-scale test marketing and flexible (agile) launches rather than relying on a single large study.
