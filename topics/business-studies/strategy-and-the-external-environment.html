Strategy and the External Environment

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level All Boards (AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC, CCEA) AQA

A-Level Business Studies revision: Strategy and the External Environment. Learning objectives, key points, worked examples and practice questions across AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC and CCEA.

📌 Key Points

Key Fact: SWOT: internal S/W, external O/T; PESTEL: Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental, Legal
Key Fact: Porter 5 Forces: rivalry, new entrants, substitutes, buyer power, supplier power
Key Fact: Core competences: valuable, rare, inimitable, non-substitutable (VRIN)
Key Fact: Boston Matrix: Stars (high share, high growth), Cash Cows (high share, low growth), Question Marks (low share, high growth), Dogs (low share, low growth)
Key Fact: Ansoff: existing/new products x existing/new markets -> 4 strategies
Key Fact: Porter Generic: Cost leadership (lowest cost), Differentiation (unique value), Focus (niche)
Key Fact: Organic: internal investment; Inorganic: M&A (horizontal, vertical, conglomerate), strategic alliances, joint ventures, franchising
Key Fact: Change: Kotter 8 steps (urgency, coalition, vision, communicate, empower, wins, consolidate, anchor); Lewin: unfreeze, change, refreeze
Key Fact: CSR: Carroll (economic, legal, ethical, philanthropic); Stakeholder theory vs shareholder primacy; Triple bottom line (people, planet, profit)

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Apply strategic analysis: SWOT, PESTEL, Porter's Five Forces, Core Competences, Boston Matrix
  • Evaluate strategic direction: Ansoff Matrix (market penetration, development, product development, diversification)
  • Analyse competitive strategy: Porter's Generic (cost leadership, differentiation, focus)
  • Evaluate growth methods: organic (internal) vs inorganic (M&A, alliances, franchising)
  • Understand strategic implementation: resource allocation, change management (Kotter, Lewin), KPIs
  • Analyse business ethics and CSR: Carroll's pyramid, stakeholders vs shareholders

💡 Worked Example

Exam-Style Question

Question: Use Porter's Five Forces to analyse the UK supermarket industry

Model Answer:

Rivalry: high (Tesco, Sainsbury, Asda, Morrisons, Aldi, Lidl -- price wars). New entrants: low (high capital, established supply chains, brand loyalty). Substitutes: medium (convenience stores, online, markets). Buyer power: high (price-sensitive, low switching cost, loyalty cards). Supplier power: medium (large buyers, but branded goods have power). Overall: low profitability

Q1: Apply PESTEL analysis to the UK electric vehicle industry. (8 marks)

Show Model Answer

Answer: Political: government grants (e.g. plug-in car grant) and bans on new petrol/diesel cars create opportunities. Economic: high fuel prices boost EV demand, but cost-of-living pressure may delay purchases. Social: growing environmental awareness and the image of green driving increase adoption. Technological: battery range improvements and expanding charging networks reduce barriers. Environmental: emissions targets favour EVs, but battery production and disposal raise sustainability questions. Legal: clean air zones (e.g. ULEZ) and zero-emission vehicle mandates push manufacturers to electrify. Application: most factors create opportunities for EV makers, but threats remain -- patchy charging infrastructure and possible withdrawal of subsidies -- so firms should monitor the external environment continuously rather than treat PESTEL as a one-off snapshot.

Q2: Place a technology company's product portfolio (e.g. Apple's) in the Boston Matrix and recommend a strategy for each category. (8 marks)

Show Model Answer

Answer: iPhone: Cash Cow -- high market share in a mature smartphone market; strategy is to defend share and use the cash generated to fund new products. New categories (e.g. Vision Pro headset): Question Mark -- low share in a high-growth market; invest to build share or divest if growth stalls. Mac and iPad: depending on market growth, either Stars (invest to maintain growth) or Cash Cows (harvest). iPod: Dog -- low share, low growth; divested (discontinued in 2022). Evaluation: the matrix is a simplification -- market share and growth alone do not determine success, and synergies across the product range (ecosystem lock-in) matter more than any single quadrant.

Q3: Evaluate cost leadership (e.g. Ryanair) versus differentiation (e.g. British Airways) as competitive strategies. (10 marks)

Show Model Answer

Answer: Cost leadership: Ryanair offers a no-frills service using a single aircraft type (economies of scale, cheaper maintenance) and secondary airports, targeting price-sensitive customers. Risks: low margins leave it vulnerable to fuel and cost shocks, and price wars can erode profits. Differentiation: British Airways competes on full service, premium branding, hub routes through Heathrow and loyalty programmes, targeting less price-sensitive customers. Risks: the cost of differentiation may exceed what customers will pay, and a downturn shifts demand towards budget carriers. Porter argues firms "stuck in the middle" underperform both. Evaluation: the best strategy depends on the target segment and cost structure; both succeed through consistent focus -- Ryanair on cost discipline, BA on service quality -- and the key is matching the strategy to the segment rather than the strategy itself.
