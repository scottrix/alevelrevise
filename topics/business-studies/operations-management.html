Operations Management

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level All Boards (AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC, CCEA) AQA

A-Level Business Studies revision: Operations Management. Learning objectives, key points, worked examples and practice questions across AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC and CCEA.

📌 Key Points

Key Fact: Job: one-off, high skill, high cost; Batch: groups, flexible; Flow: continuous, high volume, low variety; Cell: U-shape, multi-skilled; Mass custom: flow + flexibility
Key Fact: QC (inspection) vs QA (prevention); TQM: culture, continuous improvement; Kaizen: small incremental; Six Sigma: DMAIC, 3.4 defects/million; Benchmarking: best practice
Key Fact: Capacity utilisation = actual/max x100%; Efficiency = standard hours/actual hours; Productivity = output/input
Key Fact: Lean: eliminate Muda (overproduction, waiting, transport, over-processing, inventory, motion, defects); JIT: pull system; Kanban: visual signal; 5S: Sort, Set, Shine, Standardise, Sustain
Key Fact: Supply chain: procurement (supplier selection), logistics (transport, warehousing), outsourcing (core vs non-core), supplier development
Key Fact: Industry 4.0: cyber-physical systems, IoT, big data, AI, additive manufacturing, digital twins

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Compare production methods: job, batch, flow, cell, mass customisation
  • Evaluate quality management: QC vs QA, TQM, Kaizen, Six Sigma, benchmarking
  • Analyse capacity utilisation, efficiency, productivity
  • Understand lean production: JIT, Kanban, waste reduction (Muda), 5S
  • Analyse supply chain: procurement, logistics, outsourcing, supplier relationships
  • Evaluate technology impact: automation, AI, IoT, Industry 4.0

💡 Worked Example

Exam-Style Question

Question: A furniture maker switches from batch to flow production. Analyse implications for cost, quality, flexibility

Model Answer:

Cost: lower unit cost (economies of scale, less setup), but high capital investment. Quality: more consistent (standardised), but defects affect large volumes. Flexibility: reduced (dedicated line), harder to customise. Workforce: deskilling, potential redundancy. Inventory: lower WIP with JIT

Q1: Compare job production and flow production for a bespoke furniture maker and a soft drinks bottler. (6 marks)

Show Model Answer

Answer: Job production: one-off items made to exact customer specification, requiring skilled labour and carrying high unit costs, but allowing premium prices and full customisation -- ideal for a bespoke furniture maker with low volume demand. Flow production: continuous, capital-intensive output of a standardised product, achieving very low unit costs through economies of scale but offering little variety -- ideal for a soft drinks bottler with high, stable demand. Application: the choice depends on the nature of demand -- customisation versus standardisation -- and on whether the market values uniqueness or low price.

Q2: Explain each stage of the DMAIC approach used in Six Sigma quality management. (5 marks)

Show Model Answer

Answer: Define the problem and the customer requirements; Measure current process performance and defect rates; Analyse the data to identify root causes of defects; Improve the process by removing those causes; Control the improved process with monitoring to sustain the gains. The goal of Six Sigma is to reduce defects to 3.4 per million opportunities, raising quality and cutting waste-related costs.

Q3: A manufacturing firm is considering outsourcing its IT support to a specialist provider based overseas. Evaluate this decision. (10 marks)

Show Model Answer

Answer: Benefits: lower labour costs than retaining an in-house team, access to specialist expertise and 24/7 cover, and it frees managers to focus on core competences (Porter) such as production efficiency. Drawbacks: communication and time-zone difficulties, loss of direct control over quality and data security, redundancy costs and damage to staff morale, and dependence on a single supplier (supply-chain risk). Evaluation: the decision depends on whether IT support is core or non-core to the business -- for a manufacturer it is non-core, so outsourcing is likely sensible financially. However, the reputational and data-security risks of offshoring must be weighed: a UK-based provider may cost more but reduce risk, so the final choice should balance cost savings against control and quality assurance.
