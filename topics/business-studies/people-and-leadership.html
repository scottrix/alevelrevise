People and Leadership

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC/Eduqas

Motivation theories (Taylor, Mayo, Maslow, Herzberg, McClelland, Vroom, Adams), leadership styles on the Tannenbaum-Schmidt continuum, organisational design (tall/flat, matrix, delayering), and HRM — recruitment, training, appraisal and employee relations — A* revision for AQA 7132 3.2 and 3.6, Edexcel Theme 1 and OCR H431.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Evaluate content theories of motivation — Taylor (scientific management), Mayo (human relations), Maslow (hierarchy of needs), Herzberg (two-factor theory), McClelland (achievement, affiliation, power) (AQA 3.6.3; Edexcel 1.4)
  • Apply process theories — Vroom (expectancy: valence × expectancy × instrumentality) and Adams (equity) — as diagnostic tools for managers (AQA 3.6.3)
  • Evaluate financial and non-financial methods of motivation — piece rate, salary, bonus, commission, profit sharing; job rotation, enlargement, enrichment, empowerment, teamwork (AQA 3.6.3; Edexcel 1.4)
  • Analyse leadership styles on the Tannenbaum-Schmidt continuum — autocratic (tells) through democratic (consults) to laissez-faire — plus situational and transformational leadership, and the factors that determine the best style (AQA 3.2.1)
  • Evaluate organisational design — span of control, hierarchy, tall versus flat structures, delayering, centralisation versus decentralisation, matrix structures (AQA 3.6.3; Edexcel 1.4)
  • Analyse HRM — hard versus soft HRM, recruitment and selection methods, training types, appraisal (360°, management by objectives), labour turnover and employee relations (trade unions, collective bargaining, ACAS) (AQA 3.6.1–3.6.3)

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Motivation: The internal drive and external incentives that determine the effort employees put into work — a key determinant of productivity, quality and labour turnover.
Hygiene factors (Herzberg): Factors that prevent dissatisfaction but do not motivate — pay, working conditions, company policy, supervision, job security. Their absence causes dissatisfaction; their presence alone does not create motivation.
Motivators (Herzberg): Factors that genuinely motivate — achievement, recognition, the work itself, responsibility, advancement and growth.
Self-actualisation (Maslow): The highest level of Maslow's hierarchy — reaching one's full potential; only motivates once lower needs (physiological, safety, social, esteem) are satisfied.
Expectancy theory (Vroom): Motivation = valence (value of the reward) × expectancy (belief effort leads to performance) × instrumentality (belief performance leads to reward); if any element is zero, motivation collapses.
Tannenbaum-Schmidt continuum: A continuum of leadership styles running from boss-centred (manager tells, sells) to subordinate-centred (manager consults, joins, delegates) — the best point depends on the manager, subordinates and situation.
Span of control: The number of subordinates directly reporting to a manager — narrow spans create tall hierarchies; wide spans create flat structures.
Delayering: Removing layers of management from a hierarchy to flatten the structure, cutting costs and speeding communication, but increasing spans of control and risking overload.
Job enrichment: Giving employees more challenging, meaningful work with greater responsibility and autonomy — a vertical extension of the job that targets Herzberg's motivators.
Labour turnover: (Number of staff leaving in a year ÷ average number employed) × 100 — measures the rate at which employees leave; high turnover raises recruitment and training costs.

Motivation theories: Taylor, Mayo, Maslow, Herzberg, McClelland (AQA 3.6.3; Edexcel 1.4; OCR H431)

Taylor (scientific management) — workers are motivated primarily by money; measure and standardise tasks, and pay by results (piece rate). Useful in mass production (Amazon warehouse targets, call-centre scripts), but modern evidence shows pay alone is insufficient and piece rate can harm quality and safety. Mayo (human relations) — the Hawthorne studies found workers respond to attention, teamwork and social factors more than physical conditions: motivation comes from belonging and being valued — the foundation of teamwork and employee-voice practices. Maslow (hierarchy of needs) — five levels: physiological (pay, conditions), safety (job security), social (teamwork), esteem (recognition, status), self-actualisation (development, challenge). Lower needs must be satisfied before higher ones motivate, so managers secure pay and safety first, then offer recognition and growth. Criticisms: the hierarchy is culturally biased (self-actualisation is a Western concept) and needs are hard to measure or order strictly. Herzberg (two-factor theory) — hygiene factors (pay, conditions, policy, supervision) prevent dissatisfaction but cannot motivate; only motivators (achievement, recognition, responsibility, advancement, the work itself) genuinely motivate. Practical power: raising pay removes dissatisfaction but does not create motivation — motivation requires job enrichment, recognition and responsibility. Criticism: his critical-incident methodology has been accused of attributing good experiences to motivators and bad ones to hygiene factors. McClelland — three acquired needs: achievement (challenging targets, feedback), affiliation (teamwork, harmony), power (influence, leadership roles) — individuals differ in which dominates, so motivating methods should match the person. Exam technique: evaluate theories against each other — Herzberg directly challenges Taylor's pay-based view; Mayo complements Maslow's social level; McClelland explains why one method never fits all. Worked application: a call centre with high turnover applies Herzberg — hygiene first (competitive pay, ergonomic conditions, supportive supervision, fair policies) then motivators (team targets with recognition, employee-of-the-month, job enrichment through team-leader roles, career paths) — because pay alone cannot fix turnover if the work itself is monotonous.

Motivation in practice: financial and non-financial methods (AQA 3.6.3; Edexcel 1.4; OCR H431)

Financial methods: piece rate (pay per unit — motivates output, risks quality); salary and time rate (security, but no output incentive); bonus (one-off reward for targets); commission (percentage of sales — strong output incentive for sales staff, risks aggressive selling); profit sharing (aligns employees with business performance — but individual effort is diluted across the firm); fringe benefits (pensions, healthcare, discounts — retention aids). Evaluation: financial methods target Taylor's view, but Herzberg warns most pay is a hygiene factor — pay rises remove dissatisfaction only temporarily; commission schemes work only where valence and instrumentality are high (Vroom).

Non-financial methods target Herzberg's motivators:

  • Job rotation — switching tasks: variety, broader skills, less boredom; but no added responsibility.
  • Job enlargement — more tasks at the same level (horizontal): variety without challenge — risk of mere workload increase.
  • Job enrichment — more challenging, meaningful work with responsibility (vertical): targets motivators directly, but not all employees want responsibility and training costs rise.
  • Empowerment — delegating real decision-making authority: motivation and faster decisions; risks inconsistency and requires trust and training.
  • Teamwork — social needs (Maslow/Mayo) and shared purpose; quality circles involve staff in improvement decisions (kaizen link).

Process theories as diagnostics: Vroom's expectancy theory — motivation = valence × expectancy × instrumentality. Applied to sales commission: commission motivates only if the reward is valued (valence), staff believe effort produces sales (expectancy — so training and realistic targets are essential; unreachable targets collapse expectancy to zero), and staff trust that hitting targets reliably triggers payment (instrumentality). If any element is zero, motivation collapses — explaining why identical schemes motivate some staff and not others. Criticisms: perceptions are hard to measure, and the rational-calculating assumption ignores emotional and habitual motivation. Adams' equity theory — employees compare their reward-to-effort ratio with others (referent groups); perceived inequity causes demotivation — explaining why pay transparency, unequal bonuses or perceived unfairness damage morale even among well-paid staff. Exam technique: the strongest answers match methods to theories (enrichment → Herzberg motivators; piece rate → Taylor) and judge against the workforce: money-motivated production staff respond to piece rate; professional staff value enrichment and development.

Leadership styles: Tannenbaum-Schmidt, situational and transformational (AQA 3.2.1; Edexcel 1.4; OCR H431)

The Tannenbaum-Schmidt continuum arranges leadership styles from boss-centred to subordinate-centred:

  1. Tells — autocratic: the manager decides and announces; fast decisions, clear direction, useful in crisis or with inexperienced teams — but demotivating for skilled staff and ignores employee ideas.
  2. Sells — the manager decides but persuades: still fast, gains some buy-in.
  3. Consults — democratic: the manager consults before deciding — better decisions (employee knowledge), higher motivation and ownership, but slower.
  4. Joins — the manager and team decide together: maximum ownership and creativity — but slowest and risks indecision.
  5. Laissez-faire (delegates) — the team decides with minimal interference: works for expert, self-motivated teams (design studios, research labs) — but with unskilled or unmotivated teams it produces drift and poor coordination.

Choosing a style depends on: the manager's own personality and skill, the subordinates' skill and motivation, the situation (crisis favours telling; innovation favours joining) and the organisational culture. Situational leadership (Hersey-Blanchard) formalises this: adapt the style to follower readiness — telling for low readiness, selling for some, participating for moderate-to-high, delegating for high readiness. Transformational leadership — inspiring through vision, intellectual stimulation and individual attention, creating change and extraordinary commitment (contrast with transactional leadership based on exchange: rewards for compliance); transformational leaders suit change situations (turnarounds, digital transformation) but depend on charisma and can mask over-centralisation. McGregor's Theory X and Theory Y underpins style choice: Theory X managers (workers dislike work) lean autocratic; Theory Y managers (workers seek responsibility) lean democratic — self-fulfilling expectations shape how staff actually behave. Exam technique: there is no universally best style — evaluate against the context (urgency, team expertise, culture) and show the consequences of a mismatch: autocratic with experts wastes knowledge and drives turnover; laissez-faire with novices produces chaos.

Organisational design: hierarchy, span of control, delayering, matrix (AQA 3.6.3; Edexcel 1.4; OCR H431)

Organisational design structures people, authority and communication. Key concepts: hierarchy (levels of authority), chain of command (the line of authority from top to bottom), span of control (subordinates per manager), delegation (passing authority down — motivating and developing staff, but requires accountability), and authority and responsibility.

  • Tall structures — many layers, narrow spans: tight supervision, clear promotion paths, but slow communication (messages distorted through layers), high management cost and junior staff feel remote.
  • Flat structures — few layers, wide spans: faster communication and decisions, empowerment and lower cost — but managers overloaded, less supervision and fewer promotion prospects.
  • Delayering — removing management layers: cuts cost, speeds decisions, empowers survivors — but increases spans of control, risks overload and lost coordination, and causes redundancy costs and morale damage ('survivor syndrome').
  • Centralised — decisions made at the top: consistency, control, economies of scale in decision-making; but slow, ignores local knowledge and demotivates lower levels.
  • Decentralised — authority delegated to divisions/branches: faster local decisions, motivation and responsiveness; but inconsistency, duplication and loss of control.
  • Matrix structure — staff report to both a functional manager and a project manager: flexible deployment of specialists, cross-functional communication and innovation — but dual reporting creates conflicting priorities, stress, unclear accountability and slower decisions.
  • Organisational culture (Handy) — power culture (central, charismatic), role culture (procedures, bureaucracy), task culture (project teams), person culture (individuals first) — structure and culture must align; a task-culture firm with a rigid role-culture structure creates friction.

Exam technique: link structure to strategy and context — a growing start-up often moves from flat to tall as it scales (more layers for control); a mature bureaucracy delayers to respond faster. Evaluate with consequences: delayering 'saves cost' only if remaining managers can handle wider spans; a matrix 'improves innovation' only with clear roles and conflict resolution — otherwise coordination problems outweigh collaboration benefits.

HRM: recruitment, training, appraisal and employee relations (AQA 3.6.1–3.6.3; Edexcel 1.4; OCR H431)

Hard versus soft HRM: hard treats employees as a resource to be deployed and minimised (workforce planning, redundancy as a tool); soft treats employees as assets to develop (development, involvement, long-term commitment). Soft HRM builds engagement and retention; hard HRM controls costs — the trade-off is a classic evaluation point.

Recruitment and selection: the process runs from workforce planning to job analysis, producing a job description (duties, responsibilities) and person specification (skills, qualifications, attributes). Internal recruitment (promoting or transferring): cheaper, faster, candidates are known, motivation boost — but no new ideas and creates another vacancy. External recruitment: fresh ideas, wider talent pool — but slower, costlier and riskier (unknown quantity). Selection methods: CVs and applications (cheap but easy to exaggerate); interviews — structured interviews (consistent questions, comparable, more reliable) versus unstructured (flexible but biased); psychometric tests (measure aptitude and personality — objective but imperfect predictors); assessment centres (tasks, group exercises, multiple observers — the most reliable predictor of performance, but expensive and slow). Worked example — a graduate scheme: person specification (degree, analytical skills, teamwork), screening by application and online tests, then an assessment centre with a group exercise and presentation, because the cost of a poor graduate hire (training plus salary) justifies the most reliable method.

Training: induction (new starters — reduces early turnover and errors); on-the-job (shadowing, coaching, mentoring — cheap, job-relevant, but quality depends on the trainer and mistakes can affect customers); off-the-job (courses, e-learning — expert delivery and focus, but costly and not job-specific). Benefits: productivity, quality, motivation (Herzberg growth) and retention; costs: direct expense, staff time away from output, and the risk of trained staff leaving (though not training them leaves unskilled staff — the classic dilemma).

Appraisal: systematic review of performance against objectives — management by objectives (MBO) (agreed SMART objectives reviewed periodically), 360-degree feedback (multi-source: manager, peers, subordinates, customers — rounded but can become a popularity contest), self-appraisal. Purposes: identify training needs, inform pay and promotion, motivate through recognition and targets; risks: bureaucratic form-filling, defensive behaviour, and appraisals that never translate into action.

Employee relations: trade unions represent workers through collective bargaining (pay, conditions); industrial action ranges from strikes and work-to-rule to overtime bans — costly for both sides; ACAS provides conciliation, arbitration and advice to resolve disputes. Good employee relations (consultation, employee voice, fair procedures) reduce conflict, turnover and disruption; adversarial relations raise costs and reputational risk. Worked calculation — labour turnover: a firm employing an average of 400 staff loses 48 in a year: turnover = 48 ÷ 400 × 100 = 12%; each leaver costs an estimated £5,000 in recruitment and training, so 48 × 5,000 = £240,000 a year — quantifying turnover's cost justifies investment in retention (pay benchmarking, development, engagement), which is exactly the analytical chain examiners reward.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: Centrex, a UK insurance call centre, suffers labour turnover of 28% a year, well above the industry average of 15%. Exit interviews cite pay below competitors, monotonous work and 'no career path'. The managing director believes higher pay is the only answer; the HR director proposes job enrichment and career development instead. Evaluate whether Centrex should respond to high labour turnover with higher pay or with non-financial methods of motivation. [AQA 7132 Paper 2 style, 25 marks]
Mark-scheme logic: <strong>Knowledge (AO1):</strong> Herzberg distinguishes hygiene factors (pay, conditions, policy — which prevent dissatisfaction) from motivators (achievement, recognition, responsibility, advancement — which genuinely motivate). Taylor argues workers are motivated by money; Vroom's expectancy theory holds that motivation = valence × expectancy × instrumentality. Labour turnover = leavers ÷ average staff × 100. <strong>Application (AO2):</strong> Centrex's 28% turnover against a 15% norm is nearly double, so the problem is Centrex-specific, not sector-wide. Exit interviews cite <em>three</em> causes: below-market pay (a hygiene factor), monotonous work and no career path (absent motivators). Each leaver carries recruitment, training and productivity-loss costs — at scale, turnover of this level is a major cost line. <strong>Analysis (AO3):</strong> Higher pay addresses only the hygiene deficit: by Herzberg, raising pay removes dissatisfaction but does not create motivation — and if work remains monotonous with no progression, staff hired on better pay will leave anyway once the pay gap closes elsewhere. Pay rises are also permanent cost increases that compress margins in a price-competitive insurance market, and they do nothing for expectancy: if staff see no path from effort to advancement (instrumentality), no wage settles the issue. Job enrichment and career development target the motivators — responsibility, achievement, advancement — raising motivation, productivity and retention at lower ongoing cost; but enrichment requires training investment, not all call-centre staff want added responsibility, and pay below market will still drive some departures however enriched the work. <strong>Evaluation (AO4):</strong> The decisive criterion is which factor dominates the turnover — and the evidence says both: pay below competitors is a hygiene failure, monotonous work an absent motivator. A pay-only strategy fixes hygiene but leaves the motivational deficit; a motivation-only strategy ignores a below-market wage that itself signals unfairness (Adams' equity theory — perceived inequity demotivates). On balance, Centrex should do both but weighted towards non-financial methods: benchmark pay to market level (removing the hygiene deficit) and invest in enrichment, team-leader routes and visible career paths (creating motivators and restoring instrumentality). Pay alone is likely to fail; the combined, motivation-weighted response best fixes turnover at sustainable cost.</p>

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Compare Maslow's hierarchy of needs with Herzberg's two-factor theory as explanations of motivation. [6 marks]
Answer: Maslow: five-level hierarchy (physiological, safety, social, esteem, self-actualisation) in which lower needs must be satisfied before higher ones motivate; managers apply it by securing pay and safety first, then offering recognition and development. Herzberg: hygiene factors (pay, working conditions, company policy) prevent dissatisfaction but do not motivate, while motivators (achievement, recognition, responsibility, advancement) genuinely motivate. Similarity: both recognise that non-financial factors drive motivation beyond basic pay. Difference: Herzberg directly challenges the assumption that higher pay motivates, whereas Maslow treats pay as a lower-level need. Evaluation: Maslow's hierarchy is culturally biased and difficult to measure, and Herzberg's original methodology has been criticised for attributing good outcomes to motivators and bad ones to hygiene factors.
Apply Vroom's expectancy theory to a sales team paid commission, and evaluate its usefulness to managers. [10 marks]
Answer: Motivation = valence × expectancy × instrumentality. Valence: the value the salesperson places on the reward — commission motivates money-motivated staff. Expectancy: staff must believe effort leads to sales, so training and realistic targets are essential; if targets are unreachable, expectancy falls to zero and effort stops. Instrumentality: staff must trust that hitting targets reliably results in commission being paid. If any one element is zero, overall motivation collapses. Evaluation: the theory explains why some staff respond to commission schemes and others do not (valence differs between individuals), giving managers a diagnostic framework. However, the perceptions involved are hard to measure, the theory ignores hygiene factors and social motivators, and it assumes people make rational calculations — in practice motivation may be more emotional and habitual.
A rapidly growing tech firm is considering moving to a matrix structure. Analyse the advantages and disadvantages of this decision. [9 marks]
Answer: Advantages: specialists can be deployed flexibly across projects, improving communication between functions and encouraging innovation through cross-fertilisation of ideas; staff develop broader skills through varied experience, aiding retention. Disadvantages: dual reporting lines create conflicting priorities and employee stress, decision-making slows as two managers must agree, accountability becomes unclear, and coordination costs rise. Evaluation: a matrix suits project-driven, innovative firms where flexibility outweighs bureaucracy, but it only works with clearly defined roles and strong conflict-resolution — otherwise the coordination problems can outweigh the benefits of collaboration.
A retailer with 500 staff loses 70 employees in a year; each leaver costs an estimated £4,000 in recruitment and training. Calculate labour turnover and the annual cost, and explain one response. [6 marks]
Answer: Labour turnover = 70 ÷ 500 × 100 = 14%. Annual cost = 70 × £4,000 = £280,000. Response: invest in retention — e.g. benchmark pay against competitors (removing a hygiene-factor deficit), or structured induction and development (cutting early-leaver rates and building motivators). Evaluation: £280,000 of turnover cost justifies retention spending only if it measurably cuts the leaver rate — measure turnover again after the intervention to confirm the return.
A mature bank delayers three management layers. Analyse the likely effects on communication, motivation and costs. [9 marks]
Answer: Communication: fewer layers speed messages up and down the chain and reduce distortion — decisions reach the front line faster; but wider spans of control mean managers spread thinner, and some coordination is lost. Motivation: survivors gain empowerment, wider roles and faster promotion potential (Herzberg motivators — responsibility, advancement); however redundancy costs and 'survivor syndrome' — fear and reduced loyalty among those retained — can damage morale and productivity in the short term. Costs: salary savings from removed layers are substantial and recurring, but set against redundancy payments and possible re-hiring or overtime to fill supervision gaps. Evaluation: delayering best suits over-bureaucratic firms where slow communication is the core problem; success depends on training remaining managers for wider spans and rebuilding trust quickly.
Evaluate the usefulness of Taylor's scientific management for motivating modern service-sector workers. [12 marks]
Answer: AO1: Taylor argued workers are motivated by money alone; tasks should be measured, standardised and paid by results (piece rate). AO2/3: In services — call centres, warehouses, fast food — Taylor's logic survives in targets, scripts and performance-related pay: measurable output incentives do raise short-term effort. But service quality depends on judgement, courtesy and problem-solving that scripts cannot capture: piece-rate pressure can harm quality and customer experience, and Taylor ignores non-financial motivators — Mayo showed attention and teamwork matter, Herzberg showed pay is a hygiene factor that cannot motivate, and Vroom shows incentives only work if expectancy and instrumentality hold. Modern evidence: monotonous, tightly-measured service jobs show high turnover — the opposite of what money-motivation should deliver. Evaluation: Taylor remains useful for standardised, measurable tasks where output is the goal, but as a complete theory it fails for services: the best practice blends financial incentives (Taylor) with enrichment, teamwork and development (Herzberg/Mayo), matching methods to the workforce and task.
