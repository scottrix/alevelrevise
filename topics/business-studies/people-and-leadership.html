People and Leadership

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level All Boards (AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC, CCEA) AQA

A-Level Business Studies revision: People and Leadership. Learning objectives, key points, worked examples and practice questions across AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC and CCEA.

📌 Key Points

Key Fact: Content theories: Maslow (hierarchy), Herzberg (hygiene/motivators), McClelland (need achiev/affil/power); Process: Vroom (expectancy), Adams (equity)
Key Fact: Styles: Autocratic (decides alone), Democratic (consults), Laissez-faire (delegates); Situational: telling/selling/participating/delegating; Transformational: inspire, intellectually stimulate
Key Fact: Tall: many layers, narrow span; Flat: few layers, wide span; Centralised: top decides; Decentralised: delegated; Matrix: dual reporting
Key Fact: Recruitment: job description, person spec; Internal vs external; Selection: CV, interview (structured/unstructured), psychometric, assessment centre
Key Fact: Training: on-job (shadowing, coaching), off-job (courses, e-learning); Appraisal: 360 deg (multi-source), MBO (objectives)
Key Fact: Unions: represent workers, collective bargaining, industrial action (strike, work-to-rule); ACAS: conciliation, arbitration, advisory

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Evaluate motivation theories: Taylor, Mayo, Maslow, Herzberg, McClelland, Vroom, Adams
  • Analyse leadership styles: autocratic, democratic, laissez-faire, situational (Hersey-Blanchard), transformational
  • Understand organisational structures: tall/flat, centralised/decentralised, matrix, network
  • Evaluate recruitment/selection: job analysis, person spec, methods (interviews, tests, assessment centres)
  • Understand training/development: on/off job, induction, CPD, appraisal (360 deg , MBO)
  • Analyse employee relations: trade unions, collective bargaining, industrial action, ACAS

💡 Worked Example

Exam-Style Question

Question: Apply Herzberg to a call centre with high turnover. Propose changes

Model Answer:

Hygiene factors: pay, conditions, supervision, policy -> ensure competitive pay, ergonomic chairs, supportive managers, fair policies. Motivators: achievement, recognition, responsibility, growth -> team targets with bonuses, employee of month, job enrichment (team leader roles), career path to management

Q1: Compare Maslow's hierarchy of needs with Herzberg's two-factor theory as explanations of motivation. (6 marks)

Show Model Answer

Answer: Maslow: five-level hierarchy (physiological, safety, social, esteem, self-actualisation) in which lower needs must be satisfied before higher ones motivate; managers apply it by securing pay and safety first, then offering recognition and development. Herzberg: hygiene factors (pay, working conditions, company policy) prevent dissatisfaction but do not motivate, while motivators (achievement, recognition, responsibility, advancement) genuinely motivate. Similarity: both recognise that non-financial factors drive motivation beyond basic pay. Difference: Herzberg directly challenges the assumption that higher pay motivates, whereas Maslow treats pay as a lower-level need. Evaluation: Maslow's hierarchy is culturally biased and difficult to measure, and Herzberg's original methodology has been criticised for attributing good outcomes to motivators and bad ones to hygiene factors.

Q2: Apply Vroom's expectancy theory to a sales team paid commission, and evaluate its usefulness to managers. (10 marks)

Show Model Answer

Answer: Motivation = valence x expectancy x instrumentality. Valence: the value the salesperson places on the reward -- commission motivates money-motivated staff. Expectancy: staff must believe effort leads to sales, so training and realistic targets are essential; if targets are unreachable, expectancy falls to zero and effort stops. Instrumentality: staff must trust that hitting targets reliably results in commission being paid. If any one element is zero, overall motivation collapses. Evaluation: the theory explains why some staff respond to commission schemes and others do not (valence differs between individuals), giving managers a diagnostic framework. However, the perceptions involved are hard to measure, the theory ignores hygiene factors and social motivators, and it assumes people make rational calculations -- in practice motivation may be more emotional and habitual.

Q3: A rapidly growing tech firm is considering moving to a matrix structure. Analyse the advantages and disadvantages of this decision. (9 marks)

Show Model Answer

Answer: Advantages: specialists can be deployed flexibly across projects, improving communication between functions and encouraging innovation through cross-fertilisation of ideas; staff develop broader skills through varied experience, aiding retention. Disadvantages: dual reporting lines create conflicting priorities and employee stress, decision-making slows as two managers must agree, accountability becomes unclear, and coordination costs rise. Evaluation: a matrix suits project-driven, innovative firms where flexibility outweighs bureaucracy, but it only works with clearly defined roles and strong conflict-resolution -- otherwise the coordination problems can outweigh the benefits of collaboration.
