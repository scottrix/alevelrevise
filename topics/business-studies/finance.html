Finance and Accounts

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC/Eduqas

Sources of finance, cash flow and working capital, budgets and variance analysis, income statements and balance sheets, profitability, liquidity, efficiency and gearing ratios, break-even and investment appraisal (payback, ARR, NPV) with step-by-step calculations — A* revision for AQA 7132 3.5, Edexcel Themes 2 and OCR H431.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Evaluate internal and external sources of finance — retained profit, asset sales, overdrafts, trade credit, loans, bonds, share capital, venture capital — and the factors determining the choice (AQA 3.5.3; Edexcel 2.1)
  • Construct and interpret cash-flow forecasts, explain why profitable businesses fail from cash shortages, and evaluate remedies (AQA 3.5.2; Edexcel 2.2)
  • Analyse budgets and variance analysis — zero-based versus incremental budgeting, adverse and favourable variances (AQA 3.5.2)
  • Interpret income statements and statements of financial position, and calculate profitability (gross margin, net margin, ROCE), liquidity (current, acid test), efficiency (inventory, receivables, payables days) and gearing ratios with step-by-step workings (AQA 3.5.2; Edexcel 2.3 and 3.5)
  • Perform break-even analysis — contribution, break-even output, margin of safety, target profit — and evaluate its assumptions (AQA 3.5)
  • Apply investment appraisal — payback, average rate of return (ARR) and net present value (NPV) with discounting — and evaluate which technique suits which decision (AQA 3.5; Edexcel 3.3)

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Retained profit: Profit kept within the business after dividends have been paid; an internal source of finance with no interest or dilution, limited by the size of past profits.
Working capital: The finance available for day-to-day operations: current assets minus current liabilities.
Net cash flow: Cash inflows minus cash outflows in a period; the closing balance equals the opening balance plus net cash flow. Profit is not cash — a profitable business can fail from a cash shortage.
Contribution per unit: Selling price per unit minus variable cost per unit — the amount each unit contributes towards fixed costs and then profit.
Margin of safety: The difference between actual (or forecast) output and break-even output — how far demand can fall before the business makes a loss.
Return on Capital Employed (ROCE): Operating profit divided by capital employed (non-current liabilities plus equity) × 100; the key profitability ratio comparing profit with the long-term capital invested.
Gearing: Non-current liabilities as a percentage of capital employed (or of equity). Above roughly 50% a business is highly geared — interest must be paid regardless of profit, raising financial risk.
Acid test ratio: Current assets excluding inventory, divided by current liabilities — a stricter liquidity measure than the current ratio because inventory may be slow to convert to cash.
Net present value (NPV): The sum of future net cash flows discounted to today's values, minus the initial investment; a positive NPV means the project beats the discount rate (cost of capital).
Average rate of return (ARR): Average annual profit divided by average investment × 100; expresses an investment's profitability as a comparable percentage, but ignores the timing of returns.

Sources of finance: internal, external and the choice (AQA 3.5.3; Edexcel 2.1; OCR H431)

Internal sources generate funds from within the business: retained profit (no interest, no dilution of control, immediate — but limited by past profitability and hoarding cash risks starving investment); sale of assets (no repayment, but a one-off source that may reduce capacity — sale-and-leaseback keeps use of the asset at the cost of ongoing rent); and better working-capital management (tighter credit control, lower stock levels — releases cash but strains supplier and customer relationships).

External sources:

  • Short-term: bank overdraft (flexible, quick, interest only on the amount used — but high interest and repayable on demand); trade credit (delay payment to suppliers — effectively free short-term finance, but strains suppliers and forgoes discounts); debt factoring (sell receivables for immediate cash at a discount — improves liquidity but loses part of the debt's value).
  • Long-term: bank loans (large sums, predictable repayments, no dilution — but interest paid regardless of profit, security/collateral required and gearing rises); corporate bonds/debentures (long-term debt instruments for plcs — fixed interest, but similar risk considerations); share capital (issue shares: no repayment or interest, but dilutes ownership and control, dividend expectations grow, and only plcs can offer shares publicly); venture capital/business angels (equity investment plus expertise — for high-risk start-ups, at the cost of significant ownership and influence); crowdfunding (many small investors via platforms — also a marketing test, but success is uncertain and ideas are exposed).

Factors determining the choice: cost (interest versus dividends versus fees), control (debt preserves ownership; equity dilutes it), purpose and time period (match short-term needs to short-term finance — matching principle), the firm's existing gearing and risk appetite, size and legal form (a sole trader cannot issue shares), and flexibility (overdrafts flex with need). Strong evaluation: debt is usually cheaper than equity but riskier — interest is paid regardless of profit, so a highly geared business in a downturn faces failure; equity is safer in downturns but permanently dilutes control. The right answer matches the source to the purpose, time horizon and existing capital structure.

Cash flow, working capital and why profit is not cash (AQA 3.5.2; Edexcel 2.2; OCR H431)

A cash-flow forecast projects monthly inflows (cash sales, receivables collected, capital injections) and outflows (wages, rent, materials, VAT), then calculates: net cash flow = inflows − outflows; closing balance = opening balance + net cash flow. Profit is not cash — the single most examined finance distinction: profit is revenue minus costs when earned (including credit sales not yet collected, and non-cash costs such as depreciation); cash is money actually available. A business selling on credit with 60-day terms can be profitable on paper yet unable to pay wages — insolvency, not lack of profit, kills firms.

Causes of cash-flow problems: overtrading (expanding sales faster than cash and capacity allow — growth consumes cash in stock, wages and premises before revenue arrives), over-investment in fixed assets, poor credit control (slow-paying customers), excessive credit taken by the firm eroding supplier goodwill, seasonal demand, and loss-making trading. Remedies: improve inflows (tighter credit control, factoring receivables, deposits/upfront payments, promotion), reduce outflows (delay non-essential spending, lease rather than buy, negotiate longer supplier terms), arrange an overdraft facility as a buffer, and — structurally — slow growth to a pace the cash can fund.

Working capital = current assets − current liabilities. Too little and the business cannot pay its way (risk of insolvency); too much suggests idle cash and inefficient use of funds. Working capital is managed through the operating cycle: inventory days + receivables days − payables days = the days cash is tied up. A supermarket converts stock to cash daily and pays suppliers late, so its cycle is short and even a negative working-capital position can be safe; a manufacturer with slow-moving stock and 90-day customer terms needs far more. Worked example: a business with current assets of £120,000 (including £40,000 inventory) and current liabilities of £80,000 has working capital of £40,000, a current ratio of 1.5, but an acid test of (£120,000 − £40,000) ÷ £80,000 = 1.0 — the gap between the two ratios reveals how much of its liquidity depends on selling inventory.

Budgets and variance analysis (AQA 3.5.2; Edexcel 2.2; OCR H431)

Budgets set financial targets for revenue and cost per department, and serve four purposes: planning (forcing forward thinking), coordination (aligning departments with corporate objectives), motivation (targets give staff goals — though unrealistic budgets demotivate) and control (variances flag where spending deviates from plan).

  • Incremental budgeting — last year's budget adjusted (typically + inflation). Quick and stable, but perpetuates past waste and encourages end-of-year spending to protect next year's budget.
  • Zero-based budgeting (ZBB) — every budget line starts at zero and must be justified from scratch. Eliminates inherited waste and focuses spending on objectives, but time-consuming and demanding for managers — suited to periods of cost pressure or strategic change.
  • Flexible/rolling budgets adjust during the year as conditions change, keeping targets meaningful.

Variance analysis compares actual with budget: a favourable variance improves profit (revenue above budget, costs below budget); an adverse variance reduces it (revenue below budget, costs above budget). Worked example: a department budgets £20,000 for marketing but spends £25,000 — an adverse variance of £5,000 (25% over budget). Interpretation matters more than the calculation: an adverse variance is not automatically bad — overspending on marketing that generated sales 40% above budget may be a sound investment; a favourable variance on training may signal under-investment that damages quality later. Strong answers investigate the cause of variances (demand changes, price changes, efficiency, poor forecasting) and judge them against the corporate objective. Evaluation: budgets are only as good as their forecasts — built on past data in dynamic markets — and rigid budget adherence can distort behaviour (budgetary slack: padding budgets to make targets easy). Variance analysis directs management attention; it does not by itself explain performance.

Financial statements and ratio analysis, step by step (AQA 3.5.2; Edexcel 2.3 and 3.5; OCR H431)

The income statement (profit and loss account) measures performance over a period: revenue − cost of sales (COGS) = gross profit; gross profit − expenses = operating profit; operating profit − net finance costs − tax = profit for the year. The statement of financial position (balance sheet) is a snapshot: non-current assets (property, plant, equipment) + current assets (inventory, receivables, cash) = current liabilities + non-current liabilities + equity (share capital, retained earnings).

Worked example — profitability ratios: revenue £500,000, cost of sales £300,000, expenses £120,000, capital employed £400,000. Gross profit = 500,000 − 300,000 = £200,000 → GPM = 200,000 ÷ 500,000 × 100 = 40%. Operating profit = 200,000 − 120,000 = £80,000 → operating margin = 80,000 ÷ 500,000 × 100 = 16%. ROCE = 80,000 ÷ 400,000 × 100 = 20% — above a 6% borrowing rate, so the business creates value from its capital. The 24-point gap between GPM and operating margin signals heavy overheads: management should investigate expense control. Liquidity worked example: current assets £120,000 (inventory £60,000), current liabilities £80,000 → current ratio = 1.5; acid test = 60,000 ÷ 80,000 = 0.75 — liquidity depends heavily on selling inventory; if stock is slow-moving, the true position is weaker than the current ratio suggests.

Limitations of ratio analysis: ratios are historical (past performance ≠ future), distorted by differing accounting policies between firms (depreciation, valuation of stock), ignore qualitative factors (brand strength, staff, market conditions), and single-year figures can mislead — always compare over time (trend) and against competitors (benchmarking), and pair ratios with qualitative analysis (SWOT, market data).

Break-even analysis and investment appraisal, step by step (AQA 3.5; Edexcel 2.2 and 3.3; OCR H431)

Break-even finds the output at which total revenue equals total cost. Contribution per unit = price − variable cost per unit. Break-even output = fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit. Margin of safety = actual output − break-even output. Output for target profit = (fixed costs + target profit) ÷ contribution per unit. Full worked example: fixed costs £50,000, variable cost £5 per unit, price £15, actual output 12,000 units, target profit £30,000:

  1. Contribution = 15 − 5 = £10 per unit.
  2. Break-even output = 50,000 ÷ 10 = 5,000 units.
  3. Margin of safety = 12,000 − 5,000 = 7,000 units — or 7,000 ÷ 12,000 × 100 = 58% of current output; demand can fall 58% before losses begin.
  4. Output for £30,000 profit = (50,000 + 30,000) ÷ 10 = 8,000 units.

Assumptions and limitations to evaluate: assumes constant price and variable cost per unit (no economies of scale), fixed costs fixed over the range, output = sales (no stock change), and a single-product (or constant mix) business — real cost and revenue curves are rarely straight lines, so break-even is an estimate. Still valuable: quick, shows margin of safety and the effect of price/cost changes, and supports 'what-if' analysis.

Investment appraisal compares capital projects:

  • Payback period — time to recover the initial outlay. Worked example: £100,000 invested; net cash flows £30,000 a year → payback = 100,000 ÷ 30,000 = 3.33 years (3 years 4 months). For uneven flows, accumulate year by year. Strengths: simple, favours liquidity and limits exposure to forecasting error. Weaknesses: ignores cash flows after payback and the time value of money, and says nothing about profitability.
  • ARR — average annual profit ÷ average investment × 100. Worked example: £100,000 invested, total profit over 5 years £75,000 → average annual profit = £15,000; average investment = (100,000 + 0) ÷ 2 = £50,000 → ARR = 15,000 ÷ 50,000 × 100 = 30%. Compares projects as percentages, but uses forecasts and ignores timing.
  • NPV — discounts future cash flows to today's values. Worked example: £100,000 invested; £30,000 a year for 5 years at 10% (discount factors 0.909, 0.826, 0.751, 0.683, 0.621): present value = 30,000 × (0.909 + 0.826 + 0.751 + 0.683 + 0.621) = 30,000 × 3.790 = £113,700 → NPV = 113,700 − 100,000 = +£13,700. Positive NPV → the project beats the 10% cost of capital and adds value. Strengths: accounts for time value of money and all cash flows. Weaknesses: sensitive to the discount rate chosen and entirely dependent on forecasts.

Which technique when: NPV is theoretically superior; payback suits liquidity-constrained or risk-averse firms; ARR suits percentage comparison with other returns. A* answers combine them — a project with a positive NPV but slow payback may still be rejected if cash flow is tight — and stress that all three depend on forecast accuracy.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: Fenwick Ltd, a family-owned furniture manufacturer, has non-current liabilities of £1.2m and equity of £0.8m (gearing 60%) and currently earns operating profit of £90,000. The directors are considering a £400,000 factory expansion funded by a bank loan at 7% interest, forecast to add £65,000 of annual operating profit. Assess the financial information the directors should consider and evaluate how useful financial analysis is in making this decision. [AQA 7132 Paper 2 style, 25 marks]
Mark-scheme logic: <strong>Knowledge (AO1):</strong> Key financial tools: gearing (non-current liabilities ÷ capital employed × 100), ROCE (operating profit ÷ capital employed × 100), break-even, cash-flow forecasting and investment appraisal (payback, ARR, NPV). <strong>Application (AO2):</strong> Fenwick's gearing is already 60% (£1.2m ÷ £2.0m × 100) — above the 50% threshold at which firms are considered highly geared. Current ROCE = 90,000 ÷ 2,000,000 × 100 = 4.5%. The expansion adds £65,000 of operating profit for £400,000 — an incremental ROCE of 16.25% — against a 7% borrowing rate (£28,000 of annual interest). Break-even on the loan: interest of £28,000 must be paid regardless of profit. <strong>Analysis (AO3):</strong> The incremental return (16.25%) comfortably exceeds the borrowing cost (7%), so the project is profitable in isolation and raises overall ROCE — after the expansion, operating profit of £155,000 on capital employed of £2.4m gives ROCE of 6.5%, better than 4.5% but still below the cost of debt, meaning the business as a whole still destroys value relative to borrowing costs. More critically, gearing rises to £1.6m ÷ £2.4m × 100 = 67%: interest must be paid in full even in a downturn, and furniture demand is highly cyclical — a 20% profit fall would leave operating profit of £124,000 against £56,000 of total interest (existing plus new), squeezing shareholder returns and cash flow. Cash flow, not profit, is the binding constraint: the £400,000 outlay precedes revenue, so the cash-flow forecast must confirm the firm can service both the loan and its working-capital needs through the growth period. <strong>Evaluation (AO4):</strong> The decisive criteria are the cyclical risk of furniture demand and the already-high gearing. The project is financially attractive in isolation (16.25% incremental ROCE vs 7% cost), and financial analysis — ROCE against the interest rate, gearing, break-even and NPV — is genuinely useful for structuring exactly this judgement. But ratios are historical, forecast-based and silent on qualitative factors (market conditions, capacity, family control). A balanced conclusion: proceed <em>only if</em> the £65,000 forecast is stress-tested (a downturn scenario still covering interest) and Fenwick can reduce gearing elsewhere (e.g. from retained profit) — otherwise defer, since 67% gearing on a cyclical demand base risks the family's control of the business if expansion coincides with a recession. Financial analysis frames the decision well but cannot replace a stress-tested judgement about demand risk.</p>

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

A business reports revenue of £500,000, cost of sales of £300,000 and operating expenses of £120,000. Calculate the gross profit margin and the operating profit margin. (4 marks)
Answer: Gross profit = 500,000 − 300,000 = £200,000, so gross margin = 200,000 ÷ 500,000 × 100 = 40%. Operating profit = 200,000 − 120,000 = £80,000, so operating margin = 80,000 ÷ 500,000 × 100 = 16%. The 24-percentage-point gap between the two margins shows heavy overheads: management should investigate expense control, since a rising cost base erodes operating profit even when gross margin is stable.
A supermarket chain has a current ratio of 0.8 while a furniture manufacturer has a current ratio of 1.5. Assess which business faces the greater short-term liquidity risk. (6 marks)
Answer: A current ratio of 0.8 means current liabilities exceed current assets. However, supermarkets sell stock for cash daily and pay suppliers on credit, so a ratio below 1 is normal for the sector and may indicate efficient working capital management rather than risk. The manufacturer's 1.5 looks healthy on paper, but current assets include slow-moving furniture stock: the acid test (current assets excluding inventory ÷ current liabilities) would reveal true liquidity. Evaluation: liquidity must be judged against industry norms and the composition of current assets. On balance, the manufacturer may carry the greater hidden risk, because if demand falls, much of its 'liquid' assets cannot be converted to cash quickly.
A project requires an initial investment of £100,000 and returns £30,000 each year for 5 years. Using a 10% discount rate (discount factors: 0.909, 0.826, 0.751, 0.683, 0.621), calculate the NPV and advise whether the business should proceed. (8 marks)
Answer: Present value = 30,000 × (0.909 + 0.826 + 0.751 + 0.683 + 0.621) = 30,000 × 3.790 = £113,700. NPV = 113,700 − 100,000 = +£13,700. Advice: proceed, because a positive NPV means the project returns more than the cost of capital and adds shareholder value. Evaluation: the decision is sensitive to the discount rate chosen (a higher rate could turn the NPV negative) and to forecast accuracy; payback would be roughly 3.3 years, so management should also compare the NPV per pound invested with alternative projects before committing funds.
A project requires an initial investment of £200,000. Expected net cash flows are £60,000 in Year 1, £80,000 in Year 2 and £90,000 in Year 3. Calculate the payback period and advise the board on this investment. (8 marks)
Answer: After Year 1, £140,000 is recovered; after Year 2, £60,000 remains. In Year 3 the project generates roughly £7,500 per month (90,000 ÷ 12), so 60,000 ÷ 7,500 = 8 further months. Payback = 2 years and 8 months. Advice: a payback under three years reduces exposure to forecasting error and improves liquidity, which supports approval. Evaluation: payback ignores cash flows received after the payback point (the remaining £30,000 of Year 3) and the time value of money, so the board should also calculate NPV or ARR and compare this project against alternative uses of the funds before committing.
A supermarket group plans to fund a new store expansion with debt. Evaluate the usefulness of financial ratio analysis (e.g. ROCE and gearing) in supporting this strategic decision. (10 marks)
Answer: ROCE compares operating profit with capital employed: if the expected ROCE of the new store exceeds the interest rate on the debt, the expansion adds value and services the borrowing. Gearing shows the added financial risk — higher gearing makes profits more sensitive to downturns and may deter investors. Ratios also allow comparison over time and against competitors (benchmarking), supporting evidence-based decision-making. Limitations: ratios are historical and affected by accounting policies that differ between firms, they ignore qualitative factors such as brand strength, staff and market conditions, and a single year's figures can mislead. Evaluation: ratio analysis is a valuable starting framework but must be combined with qualitative analysis (e.g. SWOT, market data); the decision also depends on the economic climate, since high gearing is considerably riskier in a recession.
A department budgets £20,000 for marketing spend but actual spend is £25,000. Calculate and interpret the variance. (4 marks)
Answer: Variance = actual − budget = 25,000 − 20,000 = £5,000 adverse (an overspend of 25%). Interpretation: an adverse variance reduces profit versus plan, but the cause matters — if the extra spend generated sales materially above budget, it may be a sound investment; if not, it signals poor cost control or an unrealistic budget. Management should investigate the cause before judging performance.
