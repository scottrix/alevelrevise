Business Strategy

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC/Eduqas

SWOT and PESTEL analysis, Porter's five forces and core competences, strategic positioning (Porter's generic strategies and Bowman's clock), the Ansoff matrix, decision trees with expected values, investment appraisal (NPV, ARR, payback) and change management (Lewin, Kotter) — A* revision for AQA 7132 3.7–3.10, Edexcel Theme 3 and OCR H431.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Apply SWOT analysis — internal strengths and weaknesses, external opportunities and threats — and evaluate its limitations as a strategic tool (AQA 3.7)
  • Apply PESTEL analysis to assess political, economic, social, technological, environmental and legal change in the external environment (AQA 3.7; Edexcel Theme 3)
  • Analyse competitive environment and advantage — Porter's five forces, core competences (VRIN criteria) and the sustainability of competitive advantage (AQA 3.7)
  • Evaluate strategic direction and positioning — Porter's generic strategies (cost leadership, differentiation, focus), 'stuck in the middle', Bowman's strategic clock and the Ansoff matrix (AQA 3.8)
  • Apply decision trees with expected values, and investment appraisal (payback, ARR, NPV), and evaluate their usefulness for strategic decisions (AQA 3.2 and 3.7; Edexcel 3.3)
  • Evaluate change management — Lewin's force-field analysis and unfreeze/change/refreeze, Kotter's eight steps, Kotter and Schlesinger's reasons for resistance — and the risk of strategic drift (AQA 3.10; Edexcel 3.6)

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

SWOT analysis: A framework auditing internal strengths and weaknesses against external opportunities and threats; strong strategies match strengths to opportunities and correct weaknesses before threats exploit them.
Porter's five forces: A framework analysing industry profitability through competitive rivalry, the threat of new entrants, the threat of substitutes, buyer power and supplier power.
Core competences: The unique capabilities that underpin competitive advantage — valuable, rare, inimitable and non-substitutable (VRIN) — such as Apple's ecosystem or Toyota's production system.
Cost leadership: Porter's generic strategy of achieving the lowest unit costs in an industry, allowing the lowest prices or industry-average prices at above-average margins.
Differentiation: Porter's generic strategy of offering unique perceived value — through quality, brand, service or innovation — that customers will pay a premium for.
Stuck in the middle: Porter's argument that firms failing to commit to either cost leadership or differentiation underperform both, because they achieve neither low costs nor distinctive value.
Decision tree: A diagram of strategic choices (squares), chance outcomes (circles), probabilities and financial outcomes; the expected value of each option guides the decision.
Expected value: The sum of each outcome's financial value multiplied by its probability — the weighted average return of a risky option.
Force-field analysis (Lewin): A framework listing driving forces for change and restraining forces against it; successful change strengthens drivers and weakens restraints.
Strategic drift: The gradual divergence between a firm's strategy and the changing environment — typically because the culture and routines that brought past success resist change (Johnson).

Analysing the strategic position: SWOT and PESTEL (AQA 3.7; Edexcel Theme 3; OCR H431)

SWOT audits the strategic position: internal strengths and weaknesses (capabilities, resources, cost base, brand, staff) against external opportunities and threats (market growth, technology, legislation, competitors). Used well, SWOT is not a list but a bridge to strategy: match strengths to opportunities (use a strong brand to enter a new market), convert weaknesses (outsource a weak IT function), and defend against threats that exploit weaknesses (a price war against a high-cost base). Limitations to evaluate: SWOT is static (a snapshot in dynamic markets), subjective (different managers list different items), unweighted (no prioritisation — a minor strength sits beside a critical one) and descriptive rather than analytical unless converted into actions. Examiners penalise SWOT used as a bare list; reward it as the basis for a judgement.

PESTEL structures external change:

  • Political — government policy, grants, trade policy, instability.
  • Economic — growth, interest rates, inflation, exchange rates, unemployment, consumer income.
  • Social — demographics, lifestyles, values (environmental awareness, ageing population, remote working).
  • Technological — innovation, automation, e-commerce, AI — creative destruction threatens established business models.
  • Environmental — sustainability pressure, emissions targets, weather and resource scarcity.
  • Legal — employment law, consumer protection, competition regulation, minimum wage.

Worked application — the UK electric vehicle industry: political — government grants and bans on new petrol/diesel cars create opportunities; economic — high fuel prices boost EV demand, but cost-of-living pressure may delay purchases; social — environmental awareness and the image of green driving increase adoption; technological — battery range improvements and expanding charging networks reduce barriers; environmental — emissions targets favour EVs, but battery production and disposal raise sustainability questions; legal — clean air zones (ULEZ) and zero-emission vehicle mandates push manufacturers to electrify. Evaluation: most factors create opportunities for EV makers, but threats remain — patchy charging infrastructure and possible subsidy withdrawal — so firms should monitor the external environment continuously rather than treat PESTEL as a one-off snapshot. Link PESTEL to opportunity-cost and risk: every strategic choice is made against a moving external backdrop, which is why strategic analysis is repeated, not one-off.

Competitive environment and advantage: Porter's five forces and core competences (AQA 3.7; Edexcel Theme 3; OCR H431)

Porter's five forces explain why some industries are more profitable than others — and where a firm's profit is vulnerable:

  • Competitive rivalry — the more rivals of similar size, the fiercer the fight (price wars, promotion wars).
  • Threat of new entrants — low when barriers are high: capital requirements, economies of scale, brand loyalty, regulation, established supply chains.
  • Threat of substitutes — products meeting the same need differently (video streaming vs cinema; plant milk vs dairy) cap prices.
  • Buyer power — strong when buyers are concentrated, price-sensitive or face low switching costs; they squeeze prices and terms.
  • Supplier power — strong when suppliers are concentrated, inputs are unique, or switching suppliers is costly.

Worked application — the UK supermarket industry: rivalry is high (Tesco, Sainsbury's, Asda, Morrisons, Aldi, Lidl — persistent price wars); new entrants are unlikely (huge capital, established supply chains, brand loyalty); substitutes are a moderate threat (convenience stores, online, markets); buyer power is high (price-sensitive shoppers, low switching costs, easy comparison); supplier power is moderate (supermarkets are huge buyers, but branded goods suppliers retain some power). The overall force profile explains why supermarket margins are thin despite enormous scale — five forces links industry structure to profitability, exactly the analysis chain examiners reward.

Core competences are the capabilities that give durable competitive advantage, judged by the VRIN criteria: Valuable (creates customer value), Rare (few competitors have it), Inimitable (hard to copy — embedded in culture and routines), Non-substitutable (no alternative achieves the same). Examples: Toyota's production system, Apple's ecosystem integration, Dyson's engineering. Competences built on culture and accumulated learning resist imitation; competences based on a single feature (a price point, one technology) are copied or substituted quickly. Evaluation: competitive advantage erodes — Kodak's film dominance, BlackBerry's keyboard — so strategy must renew competences as the environment shifts; a strength today can become a rigidity tomorrow (strategic drift). Strong answers link the five forces to the chosen generic strategy: in a five-forces-hostile industry, either cost leadership (compete on price) or differentiation (escape price competition) is the route — which is Porter's bridge from analysis to direction.

Strategic direction and positioning: Porter's generic strategies, Bowman's clock, Ansoff (AQA 3.8; Edexcel Theme 3; OCR H431)

Porter's generic strategies — the two routes to above-average performance:

  • Cost leadership — lowest unit costs in the industry, through economies of scale, process efficiency, standardisation and tight overheads; allows the lowest prices (Ryanair, Aldi, IKEA). Risks: thin margins are vulnerable to cost shocks (fuel, wage inflation) and price wars; cost-cutting can erode quality; imitation erodes the advantage.
  • Differentiation — unique perceived value through brand, quality, service, design or innovation, supporting premium prices (Apple, British Airways, Dyson). Risks: the cost of differentiation may exceed what customers will pay; imitation narrows the difference; downturns shift demand to budget rivals.
  • Focus — either strategy applied to a narrow niche: cost focus (lowest cost within the niche) or differentiation focus (unique value within the niche — Rolls-Royce, specialist B2B suppliers). Risks: niche size limits scale, and larger rivals may enter.

Stuck in the middle: firms that commit to neither route underperform both — without low costs they cannot win on price; without differentiation they cannot justify premiums. (Counterpoint to evaluate: some firms blend both — IKEA offers low cost and distinctive design — so 'stuck in the middle' is contestable; Porter's defence is that such firms achieve one advantage at genuine scale.) Bowman's strategic clock extends the analysis along price and perceived value: routes include low price/low value (cheap and cheerful), low price (cost leadership), hybrid (low price with differentiation — IKEA again), differentiation (premium pricing for value), focused differentiation (premium for a niche), and the danger zones of raised prices without added value — which lose customers fast.

The Ansoff matrix maps strategic direction by product and market: market penetration (existing/existing — lowest risk: promotion, loyalty, share gains), product development (new/existing — leverage brand loyalty, R&D risk), market development (existing/new — exporting, new segments, unfamiliar-competitor risk), diversification (new/new — highest risk, spreads risk across markets; related safer than conglomerate). Evaluation: risk rises as the firm moves from what it knows, but a 'safe' penetration strategy in a declining or saturated market can be the worst choice — risk must be weighed against reward, market conditions and the firm's core competences: the strongest direction exploits what the firm is genuinely best at (VRIN competences) in markets with attractive five-forces profiles.

Decision tools: decision trees and investment appraisal (AQA 3.2 and 3.7; Edexcel 3.3; OCR H431)

A decision tree quantifies risky choices: squares mark decisions, circles mark chance outcomes, and each outcome carries a probability and financial value. Method: multiply each outcome by its probability, sum to get the expected value (EV) of each chance node, then compare EVs — for a launch-with-cost option, subtract the launch cost from the EV. Full worked example: a firm must launch a product (cost £50,000) or not. If launched: high demand (probability 0.6) yields £120,000; low demand (0.4) yields £10,000.

  1. EV of high demand branch: 0.6 × 120,000 = £72,000.
  2. EV of low demand branch: 0.4 × 10,000 = £4,000.
  3. EV of launching = 72,000 + 4,000 = £76,000.
  4. Net gain of launching = 76,000 − 50,000 = £26,000 — positive, so launch; not launching yields £0.

Uses: quantifies risk, forces explicit probabilities and options, and shows which outcomes drive the decision. Limitations to evaluate: probabilities are subjective estimates (garbage in, garbage out); EV is an average — it can hide a catastrophic low-demand outcome that a risk-averse firm cannot survive; it ignores qualitative factors (brand, staff, strategy fit) and non-financial objectives. A* answers state the net gain, then judge: the EV supports launching, but the £10,000 downside must be survivable and the 0.6 probability must be well-evidenced (market research).

Investment appraisal (full step-by-step workings in the Finance topic — recap here for strategy use):

  • Payback — time to recover the outlay: £100,000 at £30,000 a year = 3.33 years; favours liquidity, ignores post-payback flows and time value.
  • ARR — average annual profit ÷ average investment × 100; comparable percentages, forecast-based, ignores timing.
  • NPV — discount future cash flows (10% factors 0.909, 0.826, 0.751, 0.683, 0.621): £100,000 outlay returning £30,000 a year for 5 years → 30,000 × 3.790 = £113,700 − 100,000 = +£13,700; positive NPV beats the cost of capital.

Evaluation: NPV is theoretically superior; payback suits liquidity-constrained firms; decision trees add probability structure that appraisal lacks. All depend on forecast accuracy, and none captures strategy fit — a positive-NPV project that dilutes the brand or over-stretches management may still be wrong. The strongest strategic answers combine quantitative tools (decision trees, NPV) with qualitative analysis (five forces, SWOT, core competences) and reach a conditional judgement.

Managing strategic change: Lewin, Kotter, resistance and strategic drift (AQA 3.10; Edexcel 3.6; OCR H431)

Change is driven internally (new leadership, restructuring, culture programmes) and externally (PESTEL shifts, technology, competition). Lewin's model — unfreeze (create the case for change, challenge current behaviour), change (implement the new way), refreeze (embed and stabilise). His force-field analysis lists driving forces for change (competition, technology, leadership vision, financial pressure) against restraining forces (staff habits, fear, cost, culture); successful change strengthens drivers and — more effectively — weakens restraints. Kotter's eight steps operationalise it: create urgency; build a guiding coalition; form a strategic vision; communicate the vision; empower employees to act; generate short-term wins; consolidate gains and push further; anchor changes in culture. The steps matter for analysis: change fails most often at the start (no urgency, weak coalition) — which is why Kotter front-loads them.

Kotter and Schlesinger's reasons for resistance: self-interest (fear of losing status, pay or job), misunderstanding (poor communication, lack of trust), low tolerance for change (feeling overwhelmed), and different assessments (staff genuinely disagree that change is needed). Methods to overcome resistance — matched to cause: education and communication (misunderstanding); participation and involvement (different assessments — co-ownership builds commitment); facilitation and support (low tolerance — training, counselling); negotiation and agreement (self-interest — e.g. enhanced redundancy terms); manipulation and co-option (fast but ethically dubious — destroys trust if exposed); explicit/implicit coercion (fastest, but breeds resentment and sabotage — a last resort). Organisational culture (Handy) — power, role, task, person — explains why change is hard: routines and values that delivered past success resist new ways; culture change (Kotter's anchor step) is slow, which is why quick structural change without cultural change usually reverts.

Strategic drift (Johnson): gradual divergence between strategy and a changing environment — incremental change lags accelerating external change until a crisis forces transformational change (or the business fails): Kodak clung to film as digital matured; BlackBerry clung to keyboards. Leading and evaluating change: communicate the vision relentlessly, resource the change, celebrate early wins, and monitor KPIs (productivity, turnover, customer satisfaction, financial performance) to confirm the change delivers. Evaluation: the success of change management depends on the cause and depth of resistance (participation works for disagreement, not for self-interest), the speed demanded (coercion is fast but toxic), and the culture — the strongest answers judge change methods against context and stress that embedding change in culture is what makes it last.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: Ridgeline, a leading UK outdoor-equipment retailer with a strong brand and 220 stores, is considering diversifying into the budget fitness-club market — a new product (gyms) in a new market (leisure), requiring an investment of £60m funded by debt at 8%. Market research suggests a 0.7 probability of success yielding £180m of present value, and a 0.3 probability of failure yielding £30m. Evaluate whether Ridgeline should pursue the diversification. [AQA 7132 Paper 3 style, 25 marks]
Mark-scheme logic: <strong>Knowledge (AO1):</strong> Diversification (Ansoff: new product, new market) is the highest-risk growth strategy because the firm has no experience of either dimension. The five forces analyse industry attractiveness; decision trees quantify risky choices through expected values; NPV tests whether returns beat the cost of capital. <strong>Application (AO2):</strong> Ridgeline's EV = (0.7 × £180m) + (0.3 × £30m) = £126m + £9m = £135m; net of the £60m investment, the expected net gain is <strong>+£75m</strong> — positive and large relative to the outlay. Debt funding at 8% requires £4.8m of annual returns just to service the interest, and Ridgeline's core competences (retail brand, store network, buying power) lie in equipment retail, not leisure operations. <strong>Analysis (AO3):</strong> The EV analysis and positive NPV (£135m vs £60m outlay) strongly support the diversification — the expected return comfortably exceeds the borrowing cost. But the decision tree's 0.3 failure branch yields only £30m against £60m invested — a £30m loss on that outcome — and the EV average conceals it: probabilities are research-based estimates, and the fitness-club market is fiercely contested (low-cost gyms, five-forces pressure from high rivalry and low switching costs for members). Diversification also dilutes focus — Porter's 'stuck in the middle' risk applies at corporate level: managing gyms requires different competences (site selection, membership churn, leisure staffing) that Ridgeline would have to build or buy, and debt-funded failure would strain the core retail business's balance sheet. Against this, diversification spreads risk across two markets: if outdoor-equipment retail turns down, fitness income cushions group performance — the classic diversification argument — and Ridgeline's strong brand could transfer to gyms (differentiation focus within budget fitness), though brand transfer is not guaranteed. <strong>Evaluation (AO4):</strong> The decisive criteria are the quality of the 0.7 success probability, the survivability of the £30m downside, and whether the diversification exploits or dilutes core competences. The quantitative case is strong (+£75m expected net gain), but the EV rests on forecast probabilities and the strategy moves Ridgeline furthest from what it is genuinely best at. On balance, the diversification is justified <em>if</em> Ridgeline first validates the research (a pilot club or two, testing brand transfer and membership economics) and secures the downside (staged investment rather than £60m at once, keeping gearing survivable if the failure branch materialises). A staged entry — pilot first, then scale — best balances the large expected gain against the dilution risk of moving beyond its core competences.</p>

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Apply PESTEL analysis to the UK electric vehicle industry. [8 marks]
Answer: Political: government grants (e.g. plug-in car grant) and bans on new petrol/diesel cars create opportunities. Economic: high fuel prices boost EV demand, but cost-of-living pressure may delay purchases. Social: growing environmental awareness and the image of green driving increase adoption. Technological: battery range improvements and expanding charging networks reduce barriers. Environmental: emissions targets favour EVs, but battery production and disposal raise sustainability questions. Legal: clean air zones (e.g. ULEZ) and zero-emission vehicle mandates push manufacturers to electrify. Application: most factors create opportunities for EV makers, but threats remain — patchy charging infrastructure and possible withdrawal of subsidies — so firms should monitor the external environment continuously rather than treat PESTEL as a one-off snapshot.
Place a technology company's product portfolio (e.g. Apple's) in the Boston Matrix and recommend a strategy for each category. [8 marks]
Answer: iPhone: Cash Cow — high market share in a mature smartphone market; strategy is to defend share and use the cash generated to fund new products. New categories (e.g. Vision Pro headset): Question Mark — low share in a high-growth market; invest to build share or divest if growth stalls. Mac and iPad: depending on market growth, either Stars (invest to maintain growth) or Cash Cows (harvest). iPod: Dog — low share, low growth; divested (discontinued in 2022). Evaluation: the matrix is a simplification — market share and growth alone do not determine success, and synergies across the product range (ecosystem lock-in) matter more than any single quadrant.
Evaluate cost leadership (e.g. Ryanair) versus differentiation (e.g. British Airways) as competitive strategies. [10 marks]
Answer: Cost leadership: Ryanair offers a no-frills service using a single aircraft type (economies of scale, cheaper maintenance) and secondary airports, targeting price-sensitive customers. Risks: low margins leave it vulnerable to fuel and cost shocks, and price wars can erode profits. Differentiation: British Airways competes on full service, premium branding, hub routes through Heathrow and loyalty programmes, targeting less price-sensitive customers. Risks: the cost of differentiation may exceed what customers will pay, and a downturn shifts demand towards budget carriers. Porter argues firms 'stuck in the middle' underperform both. Evaluation: the best strategy depends on the target segment and cost structure; both succeed through consistent focus — Ryanair on cost discipline, BA on service quality — and the key is matching the strategy to the segment rather than the strategy itself.
A firm must decide whether to launch a product costing £50,000. High demand (probability 0.6) yields £120,000; low demand (probability 0.4) yields £10,000. Calculate the expected value of launching and the net gain, and advise the firm. [8 marks]
Answer: EV of high demand: 0.6 × £120,000 = £72,000. EV of low demand: 0.4 × £10,000 = £4,000. EV of launching = £76,000. Net gain = £76,000 − £50,000 = +£26,000 — positive, versus £0 for not launching. Advice: the EV supports launching. Evaluation: the 0.6 probability must be well-evidenced (market research, not optimism); the low-demand outcome yields only £10,000 — a £40,000 loss — which the firm must be able to absorb; and the tree ignores qualitative factors (brand fit, staff capacity), so the quantitative result should be combined with strategic analysis before committing.
A supermarket group's board is planning a digital transformation. Explain how Kotter's eight-step model could be applied to lead the change. [10 marks]
Answer: (1) Create urgency — present competitor e-commerce data showing share loss to online rivals; (2) build a guiding coalition — a senior team spanning IT, stores and marketing; (3) form a strategic vision — e.g. 'seamless omnichannel shopping within two years'; (4) communicate the vision — consistently, through all channels and town halls; (5) empower employees — train store staff, remove procedural barriers, resource the IT programme; (6) generate short-term wins — launch click-and-collect in pilot regions and publicise the results; (7) consolidate gains — roll out what worked, keep pushing further change; (8) anchor the changes in culture — embed digital-first thinking in recruitment, appraisal and routines so it survives leadership change. Evaluation: change fails most often at the start — without genuine urgency and a credible coalition the programme stalls; embedding culture (step 8) is slow, which is why quick structural change without cultural change usually reverts.
Evaluate why established businesses suffer strategic drift. [12 marks]
Answer: AO1: Strategic drift is the gradual divergence between a firm's strategy and the changing environment (Johnson). AO2/3: Causes — past success breeds complacency: routines and culture that delivered growth resist new ways (Handy's role culture, bureaucratic habits); incremental change lags accelerating external change (technology, tastes) because managers extrapolate from the past; core competences harden into rigidities (Kodak's film expertise, BlackBerry's keyboard focus); and short-term financial pressure rewards protecting the existing business over investing in uncertain new models. Drift continues until a crisis — falling sales, share collapse — forces transformational change (phase of flux) or failure. Evaluation: drift is not inevitable — firms that monitor the external environment continuously (PESTEL, five forces), refresh core competences and empower challenge to existing strategy avoid it; but avoidance requires leadership willing to cannibalise current success, which self-interest and culture routinely resist — hence drift remains common among long-successful incumbents.
