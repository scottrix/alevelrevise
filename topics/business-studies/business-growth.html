Business Growth and Objectives

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC/Eduqas

Business objectives (profit, growth, survival, cash flow, social and ethical), forms of business ownership, stakeholder needs and conflict, and organic versus external growth (mergers, takeovers, joint ventures, franchising) — A* revision for AQA 7132 3.1 and 3.9, Edexcel Theme 3 and OCR H431.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain the nature and purpose of business activity and the common objectives — profit, growth, survival, cash flow, social and ethical — and how the balance of objectives changes between a start-up and an established plc (AQA 3.1.1)
  • Distinguish the main forms of business — sole trader, partnership, private and public limited companies, not-for-profit organisations and franchises — and evaluate the advantages and disadvantages of each form (AQA 3.1.2)
  • Identify internal and external stakeholders, analyse their interests and potential conflicts, and apply stakeholder mapping by power and interest (AQA 3.1.3; Edexcel Theme 3)
  • Compare organic (internal) growth with external growth through mergers, takeovers, joint ventures, strategic alliances and franchising (AQA 3.9; Edexcel 3.2)
  • Assess why growth strategies fail — overtrading, diseconomies of scale, culture clash and cash-flow strain — and the criteria for judging a growth decision
  • Calculate and interpret market share, revenue growth and return on capital employed when evaluating growth options and how they are funded

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Mission statement: A brief statement of the overall purpose and values of an organisation, designed to communicate what the business does and why it exists to stakeholders.
Limited liability: The legal position where the financial liability of the owners (shareholders) for the debts of a business is limited to the amount they invested; their personal assets are protected.
Unlimited liability: The legal obligation of a sole trader or partner to pay all debts of the business, potentially requiring the sale of personal assets if the business fails.
Organic (internal) growth: Growth generated from within the business, for example by opening new outlets, expanding output, developing new products or entering new markets using the firm's own resources.
Horizontal integration: A merger or takeover of a business operating at the same stage of production in the same industry, for example one supermarket chain buying another.
Vertical integration: A merger or takeover of a business at a different stage of production in the same industry — backwards towards suppliers or forwards towards distributors and retailers.
Conglomerate integration: A merger or takeover of a business operating in an entirely different industry, spreading risk across unrelated markets.
Joint venture: An agreement between two or more businesses to work together on a project, sharing costs, risks and expertise, while remaining separate legal entities.
Stakeholder: Any individual or group with an interest in, or affected by, the activities of a business — for example shareholders, employees, customers, suppliers, the local community and government.
Diseconomies of scale: The rise in average unit costs that occurs when a business grows beyond its optimum size, typically through communication failures, coordination problems and de-motivation in large organisations.

Why businesses exist: objectives — profit, growth, survival and beyond (AQA 3.1.1; Edexcel Theme 1 and 3; OCR H431)

Businesses exist to satisfy the needs of customers, owners and society by producing goods and services. The mission statement sets the overall purpose and values; from it flow corporate objectives, and from those functional (departmental) objectives. Examiners reward answers that show the hierarchy: mission → corporate objectives → functional objectives → targets, and that objectives must be consistent with each other.

The common business objectives are:

  • Profit — profit maximisation is rarely pursued literally; most owners satisfice, targeting a satisfactory return. Profit matters because it rewards risk-takers, funds reinvestment and attracts finance.
  • Growth — sales growth or market share growth increases market power, economies of scale and often managerial rewards; growth may be pursued even at the cost of short-term profit (penetration pricing, heavy investment).
  • Survival — the dominant objective for new businesses and during recessions or crises; roughly a fifth of UK start-ups fail in year one and around half within three years, so cash preservation outranks profit.
  • Cash flow — a profitable business can still fail if it runs out of cash; maintaining liquidity is a genuine objective in its own right.
  • Social and ethical objectives — for social enterprises and increasingly for plc brands, environmental and ethical goals (sustainability targets, fair pay) shape decisions and reputation.

Objectives change over time. A start-up prioritises survival and cash flow; an established plc balances shareholder returns (dividends, share price) with growth and reputation. Objectives also conflict: growth financed by debt raises risk; an ethical sourcing policy raises unit costs. Strong evaluation always identifies these trade-offs and judges which objective should dominate in the given context — an objective is only 'right' relative to the business's situation, stakeholders and stage of development.

Forms of business and ownership (AQA 3.1.2; Edexcel Theme 3; OCR H431)

The form of ownership determines liability, access to finance, control and how objectives are set — a core AO1/AO2 distinction across all boards.

  • Sole trader — one owner with unlimited liability. Quick and cheap to set up, full control, keeps all profit and privacy (no accounts published). Weaknesses: unlimited liability, hard to raise finance, no continuity if the owner dies or retires, workload and skill bottlenecks.
  • Partnership — two to twenty owners sharing profit, risk and expertise under the Partnership Act 1890 (a Limited Partnership or LLP gives some partners limited liability). More capital and shared decision-making, but partners are jointly liable and disagreements can paralyse the firm.
  • Private limited company (Ltd) — shares sold privately to known investors; shareholders have limited liability, the business has separate legal identity and continuity. Finance is easier to raise than for a sole trader, but shares cannot be advertised for sale to the public, so growth capital is still restricted.
  • Public limited company (plc) — shares traded on the stock market after a flotation. Access to very large-scale finance and enhanced public profile, but costly to float, subject to regulation and scrutiny, exposed to divorce of ownership and control (professional managers may pursue their own objectives rather than shareholders'), and vulnerable to takeover because shares can be bought openly.
  • Not-for-profit organisations — charities, mutuals and social enterprises whose surplus is reinvested in the cause rather than distributed to owners; objectives centre on stakeholder benefit, and funding comes from donations, grants and trading.
  • Franchise — the franchisor grants the franchisee the right to trade under its brand in return for fees and royalties. For the franchisee: a proven business model, brand recognition and training, at the cost of fees, limited independence and the franchisor's rules. For the franchisor: rapid, low-capital expansion by others.

Shareholders own limited companies, receive dividends and typically one vote per share; institutional investors (pension funds, insurers) exert heavy influence on plcs. In exams, link the choice of legal form to objectives and growth: a sole trader converting to an Ltd gains limited liability and capital, but loses some control and privacy — whether that trade-off is worthwhile depends on the scale of growth planned and the owner's appetite for risk.

Choosing a form in exams. The assessment angle is almost always judgement: the best legal form depends on objectives, funding needs and attitude to risk. A high-growth technology start-up may stay private to protect control through early losses, then float once brand and profitability de-risk the issue; a community-focused enterprise may stay not-for-profit because shareholder returns would conflict with the mission. Note also that liability drives risk appetite: unlimited liability makes owners cautious — sensible for stability, but it can starve a business of the investment needed to grow, which is why many firms convert to limited companies precisely when they plan expansion.

Stakeholders: needs, conflict and mapping (AQA 3.1.3; Edexcel Theme 3; OCR H431)

Stakeholders are groups affected by or with an interest in the business. Internal stakeholders include employees, managers and owners; external stakeholders include customers, suppliers, creditors, the local community, government and pressure groups. Each has distinct needs:

  • Shareholders — dividends, capital growth, ethical governance.
  • Employees — fair pay, job security, development, working conditions.
  • Customers — quality, value for money, honest marketing, safety.
  • Suppliers — reliable orders, prompt payment, long-term relationships.
  • Local community — employment, low pollution, investment in the area.
  • Government — tax revenue, compliance with legislation, employment.

Stakeholder conflict arises because needs compete: a takeover rewarding shareholders may mean redundancies for employees and reduced choice for customers if a competitor is absorbed; a pay rise pleases employees but raises costs for owners; cost-cutting that pleases shareholders may damage quality for customers. The stakeholder map (power/interest grid) helps managers prioritise:

  • High power, high interest — manage closely (key shareholders, senior managers).
  • High power, low interest — keep satisfied (regulators, major creditors).
  • Low power, high interest — keep informed (employees, local community).
  • Low power, low interest — monitor with minimal effort.

In evaluation, note that objectives set by management must reconcile these conflicts — a shareholder-primary approach (Friedman) maximises owner returns but risks reputational damage from ignored stakeholders, while a stakeholder approach builds long-term legitimacy at the cost of some short-term profit. AQA 3.2.3 also treats stakeholder influence on management decision-making: the more powerful and interested a stakeholder group, the more it shapes strategy — use this to explain why a business chose a particular course, not just to list groups.

Growth: internal (organic) versus external (AQA 3.9; Edexcel 3.2; OCR H431)

Organic growth expands the business using its own resources: opening new outlets, increasing capacity, developing new products (Ansoff), entering new markets, or franchising the concept. It is slower but lower-risk: financed from retained profit and steady borrowing, it keeps control within existing management and grows culture and capability gradually. External growth acquires scale instantly through mergers (two firms agree to combine) and takeovers/acquisitions (one firm buys a majority stake, sometimes hostile), joint ventures, strategic alliances and franchising. Types of integration:

  • Horizontal — same stage, same industry: market share, removal of a competitor, economies of scale (e.g. supermarket mergers). Risk: Competition and Markets Authority intervention if market dominance results.
  • Vertical backwards — acquiring a supplier: secures inputs, quality and margins; risk of complacency and higher fixed costs.
  • Vertical forwards — acquiring distribution or retail: control of the customer interface and retail margin.
  • Conglomerate — unrelated industry: spreads risk; but no synergy or expertise, which is why conglomerates often underperform.

Why grow? Economies of scale (purchasing, technical, marketing), greater market power and pricing influence, reduced risk through diversification, access to new markets and talent, and increased brand recognition. Why external growth fails: research consistently suggests a majority of mergers destroy value — through overtrading (expanding sales faster than cash and capacity allow), culture clash between merged workforces, overpayment in bidding wars, key staff departures, integration costs exceeding synergies, and management distraction. The Greiner growth model adds that growth passes through phases, each ending in a crisis (leadership, autonomy, control) that demands structural change. Worked application: a firm with revenue of £40m in a market worth £500m has market share of £40m ÷ £500m × 100 = 8%; after acquiring a rival with £65m of sales (assuming no market growth), share rises to £105m ÷ £500m × 100 = 21% — quantifying the jump in market power an acquisition delivers is exactly the AO2 application examiners reward.

Organic versus external in evaluation. The two routes are not mutually exclusive — many plcs grow organically and acquire selectively. The exam question is which route better fits the context: speed of growth needed, availability and price of targets, existing gearing, management capacity, and cultural compatibility. Organic growth compounds learning (each new store informs the next); external growth imports capability (technology, brands, people) that organic growth could take years to build — but only if integration succeeds.

Judging growth decisions: finance, risk and evaluation criteria (AQA 3.5 and 3.9; Edexcel 3.2 and 3.5)

A* answers evaluate growth against explicit criteria rather than asserting benefits. Use financial evidence:

  • Return on Capital Employed (ROCE) = operating profit ÷ capital employed × 100. If the expected ROCE of the growth option exceeds the cost of borrowing (and the current ROCE), it adds value. Worked example: a £25m acquisition funded by a loan at 6% must deliver enough additional operating profit to beat 6% on £25m = £1.5m a year; expected synergies of £2.5m a year clear this hurdle, but only forecasts — hence the need for sensitivity analysis.
  • Gearing = non-current liabilities ÷ capital employed × 100. Debt-funded growth raises gearing; above roughly 50% the business is financially risky because interest is paid regardless of profit, so a downturn amplifies losses. Retained-profit growth avoids this but is slower and may mean missing market opportunities.
  • Cash flow — growth consumes cash before it generates it: stock, premises and recruitment all precede revenue. Assess the cash-flow forecast for the growth period, not just profit projections.

Non-financial criteria matter equally in AO4: cultural compatibility in a merger, management capacity to integrate, impact on existing stakeholders (redundancies, customer service), regulatory risk, and whether the growth fits the mission. The strongest conclusions are conditional: 'growth by acquisition is justified if synergies are realistic and cultures integrate; otherwise organic expansion better preserves value'. Finally, link growth back to objectives: growth is a means — judge it by whether it better delivers the objectives (profit, survival, shareholder value, social goals) that the business actually pursues, in its context.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: NorthPeak, a fast-growing UK outdoor clothing manufacturer with annual revenue of £40m, is considering acquiring a struggling high-street rival with revenue of £65m for £25m, funded by a bank loan at 6% interest, rather than expanding organically into new European markets. The rival has a similar product range but a very different, hierarchical management culture. Evaluate whether NorthPeak should pursue growth by acquisition rather than organic expansion. [AQA 7132 Paper 3 style, 25 marks]
Mark-scheme logic: <strong>Knowledge (AO1):</strong> External growth by takeover combines two firms under single ownership instantly — here a horizontal acquisition increasing market share and scale. Organic growth expands output and markets from within using the firm's own resources. <strong>Application (AO2):</strong> NorthPeak's takeover would raise market share from 8% to roughly 21% of its £500m market (£105m ÷ £500m × 100), deliver purchasing and marketing economies of scale, and remove a competitor. The £25m loan at 6% requires £1.5m of annual operating profit from synergies just to service the financing cost, and the rival's 'very different, hierarchical management culture' flags a high integration risk. Organic European expansion preserves NorthPeak's culture and control but builds revenue slowly against established local competitors. <strong>Analysis (AO3):</strong> A takeover delivers instant scale and market power — economies of scale could lower unit costs, and the acquired brand adds distribution — but the immediate liabilities are substantial: interest must be paid regardless of profit, gearing rises sharply, and culture clash between a fast-growing entrepreneurial firm and a hierarchical target commonly causes key-staff departures, integration delays and the destruction of expected synergies. Overtrading is a further risk: combining two businesses stretches working capital before synergies materialise. Organic growth avoids these shocks and allows learning before each step, but takes years, during which rivals — or another acquirer of the target — may act first, and the CMA would not scrutinise it. <strong>Evaluation (AO4):</strong> The decisive criteria are whether the £2.5m of forecast synergies is realistic (synergies depend on cultural integration, which the stimulus suggests is doubtful) and whether NorthPeak can absorb the gearing increase without endangering cash flow. On balance, the acquisition is likely to create more value <em>if</em> NorthPeak invests heavily in cultural integration and retains the rival's best managers — but given the explicit culture warning, organic European expansion is the safer route to sustainable long-term growth, even though it sacrifices immediate market share. A staged judgement — organic first, revisiting the acquisition if the target's price falls — would best balance risk against opportunity.</p>

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Explain two reasons why the owner of a new business may set survival, rather than profit maximisation, as the main objective. [AQA 3.1.1, 6 marks]
Answer: AO1: Survival is the ability of a business to continue trading; profit maximisation means producing at the output where the difference between revenue and total cost is greatest. Reason 1: most new businesses fail early — cash runs out before the business becomes established — so preserving cash and covering costs matters more than the size of profit; a focus on profit maximisation may involve aggressive expansion that drains cash. Reason 2: new businesses lack brand recognition and face established competitors, so the priority is winning customers and building a reputation; price-cutting to maximise profit may be impossible, whereas achieving breakeven and staying solvent keeps the business alive to pursue profit later.
Analyse the advantages and disadvantages of a successful sole trader converting the business into a private limited company. [9 marks]
Answer: AO1: An Ltd sells shares privately to known investors; shareholders benefit from limited liability and the company has separate legal identity. AO2/analysis: Advantages — limited liability protects the owner's personal assets, improving willingness to take risks; selling shares raises capital for growth that a sole trader could not borrow against; the company enjoys continuity beyond the owner and a more professional image with suppliers and banks. Disadvantages — accounts must be filed and partly published, ending financial privacy; setting up costs money and time; sharing ownership means sharing profit (dividends) and some control; lenders may still require personal guarantees, diluting the limited-liability benefit. Conclusion: the conversion is most valuable when significant growth funding is needed and the owner accepts diluted control; a stable lifestyle business may gain little.
A plc announces a £800m takeover of a rival supermarket chain, including the closure of 40 stores. Explain the likely stakeholder conflicts. [6 marks]
Answer: AO1/2: Shareholders may welcome the deal if expected synergies raise dividends and the share price, but risk-averse shareholders fear the debt taken on. Employees of the 40 closing stores face redundancy — direct conflict with shareholders' cost-saving interest; remaining staff may fear future cuts and reduced union power. Customers may lose local stores and face less competition and potentially higher prices. Suppliers may lose contracts as the merged business consolidates purchasing. The local communities of closing stores lose employment and footfall, while the government monitors competition and job impacts. Conclusion: the announcement creates a classic shareholder-versus-employee/customer conflict, managed only through consultation, redundancy support and demonstrable customer benefits.
Firm X has revenue of £12m in a market worth £80m. It expects a merged entity (X plus a rival with £16m revenue) to raise total market revenue to £90m. Calculate X's market share before and after the merger. [4 marks]
Answer: Before: £12m ÷ £80m × 100 = 15% market share. After: combined revenue £12m + £16m = £28m; £28m ÷ £90m × 100 = 31.1% (to 1 d.p.). Interpretation: market share roughly doubles despite the market growing, materially increasing market power — though a jump to over 30% may trigger Competition and Markets Authority scrutiny.
Evaluate whether a leading UK supermarket should grow by horizontally merging with a direct competitor rather than opening new stores organically. [12 marks]
Answer: AO1: A horizontal merger combines two supermarkets at the same stage of production; organic growth opens new outlets using internal resources. AO2/3: The merger instantly adds sites, market share and purchasing power — buying economies of scale lower unit costs on huge volumes — and removes a competitor, potentially raising prices. However, it risks CMA referral if dominance results, demands a large outlay (raising gearing), and overlapping stores may be closed with redundancy costs and community damage. Organic growth is slower and depends on finding viable sites, but preserves culture, avoids integration risk and spreads capital spend over time, matching cash-flow capacity. Evaluation: the decision depends on the urgency of gaining share, the price of the target, and current gearing; a chain with strong cash flow and low gearing can justify a merger at a fair price, but in a saturated market where planning permission restricts new sites, the merger is the more credible route — provided regulatory approval is likely.
Assess the advantages and disadvantages to a franchisor of expanding through franchising. [AQA 3.9 style, 10 marks]
Answer: AO1: Franchising grants franchisees the right to trade under the franchisor's brand for fees and royalties. AO2/3: Advantages — rapid expansion using franchisees' capital rather than the franchisor's (low capital risk); franchisees are highly motivated owner-operators, often outperforming employed managers; royalty income scales with sales. Disadvantages — the franchisor loses day-to-day control: a poor franchisee damages the brand everywhere; profit per outlet is limited to fees and royalties rather than full trading profit; costs of training, support and quality monitoring are continuous; franchise agreements require careful legal drafting. Evaluation: franchising suits brands with replicable, easily-taught models (fast food, coffee), where brand control can be enforced through contracts and audits; for businesses dependent on finely-tuned service quality, company-owned outlets better protect the customer experience.
