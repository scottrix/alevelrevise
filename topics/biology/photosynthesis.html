Photosynthesis

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC/Eduqas, CCEA

A-Level Biology revision on photosynthesis: chloroplast structure and pigments, the light-dependent reaction, photolysis and photophosphorylation, the Calvin cycle, limiting factors and required practicals (AQA RP7/RP8, OCR PAG11), with multi-board exam practice.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Describe chloroplast structure and photosynthetic pigments; interpret absorption and action spectra (AQA 3.5.1; OCR 5.1.1)
  • Describe the light-dependent reaction: photolysis, electron transport chain, photophosphorylation and NADP reduction (AQA 3.5.1; OCR 5.1.1)
  • Describe the light-independent reaction (Calvin cycle): RuBisCO, GP/TP, and the uses of triose phosphate (AQA 3.5.1; OCR 5.1.1)
  • Explain limiting factors (light, CO₂, temperature) and interpret limiting-factor graphs (AQA 3.5.1; OCR 5.1.1)
  • Describe the required practicals: chromatography of pigments (Rf values) and dehydrogenase activity (AQA RP7/RP8; OCR PAG11)
  • Apply the compensation point and agricultural optimisation of photosynthesis to exam scenarios

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Photosynthesis: The synthesis of organic molecules (glucose) from CO₂ and water using light energy, producing oxygen: 6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂.
Photosystem: A protein-pigment complex in the thylakoid membranes (PSI and PSII) that absorbs light energy and transfers excited electrons to an electron transport chain; each has a primary pigment (reaction centre).
Photolysis: Light-dependent splitting of water in photosystem II: 2H₂O → 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ + O₂, replacing electrons lost from chlorophyll and providing protons for NADP reduction.
Photophosphorylation: Phosphorylation of ADP to ATP using light energy, via the proton gradient generated by the electron transport chain in the thylakoid membranes (chemiosmosis).
RuBisCO: Ribulose bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase - the enzyme in the stroma that catalyses the fixation of CO₂ with ribulose bisphosphate (RuBP) to form two molecules of glycerate 3-phosphate (GP).
Glycerate 3-phosphate (GP): The 3-carbon product of CO₂ fixation in the Calvin cycle; reduced to triose phosphate using ATP and NADPH from the light-dependent reaction.
Triose phosphate (TP): The 3-carbon sugar produced by reduction of GP; used to regenerate RuBP and to synthesise glucose, starch, cellulose, lipids and amino acids.
Limiting factor: The factor in shortest supply that determines the rate of photosynthesis; usually light intensity, CO₂ concentration or temperature.
Light compensation point: The light intensity at which the rate of photosynthesis equals the rate of respiration, so net gas exchange is zero.
Rf value: In chromatography, the distance moved by a pigment ÷ distance moved by the solvent front; each pigment has a characteristic Rf used to identify it.

📌 Key Points

Chloroplast: thylakoid membranes (photosystems) stacked into grana; stroma contains Calvin-cycle enzymes; own DNA/ribosomes
Pigments: chlorophyll a (PSI/PSII reaction centre, blue-red absorption), chlorophyll b, carotenoids (accessory); absorption vs action spectra
LDR (thylakoid): photolysis 2H₂O → 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ + O₂; ETC pumps H⁺ into thylakoid lumen; ATP synthase makes ATP; NADP + H⁺ + e⁻ → NADPH
Calvin cycle (stroma): CO₂ + RuBP → 2 GP (RuBisCO); GP reduced to TP (ATP + NADPH); 5/6 TP regenerates RuBP, 1/6 makes glucose
Limiting factors: light (plateaus - other factors limit), CO₂ (plateaus), temperature (enzyme kinetics); greenhouse optimisation
Practicals: chromatography of pigments with Rf values (AQA RP7); DCPIP dehydrogenase activity (AQA RP8); OCR PAG11

Chloroplast structure, pigments and spectra (AQA 3.5.1; OCR 5.1.1; Edexcel A T5)

Photosynthesis occurs in the chloroplast: a double-membraned organelle with thylakoid membranes stacked into grana connected by lamellae, suspended in the stroma. Structure-function links to quote: thylakoid membranes hold the photosystems (PSI and PSII) and electron transport chain for the light-dependent reaction; their stacking (grana) gives a large surface area for light absorption; the stroma contains the Calvin-cycle enzymes (RuBisCO), sugars and starch grains for the light-independent reaction; chloroplasts have their own circular DNA and 70S ribosomes (endosymbiotic origin) so they can make some photosynthetic proteins quickly.

Pigments absorb light: chlorophyll a is the primary pigment at the reaction centre of both photosystems (absorbs blue-violet and red, reflects green); chlorophyll b and carotenoids (xanthophylls, carotene) are accessory pigments broadening the wavelengths absorbed, transferring energy to the reaction centre. The absorption spectrum (light absorbed per wavelength) matches the action spectrum (photosynthesis rate per wavelength) - their overlap is evidence that the pigments absorb the light that drives photosynthesis. Chromatography separates pigments (AQA RP7; OCR PAG11): crush leaves in solvent, spot on chromatography paper/silica TLC plate, run in solvent; pigments separate by solubility and affinity for the stationary phase; calculate Rf = distance moved by pigment ÷ distance moved by solvent front and identify against known Rf values. Exam questions ask for the method, the Rf calculation and why pigments separate (different solubilities/adsorption). Chlorophyll appears red under UV fluorescence when excited - occasionally examinable as evidence of energy absorption.

The light-dependent reaction (AQA 3.5.1; OCR 5.1.1; WJEC C1)

The light-dependent reaction (LDR) occurs in the thylakoid membranes and produces ATP and NADPH (reduced NADP) plus oxygen - all for the Calvin cycle. Sequence: (1) photolysis: light energy (absorbed by PSII) splits water - 2H₂O → 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ + O₂ - replacing electrons lost from chlorophyll, releasing protons into the lumen and oxygen as a waste product; (2) light excites electrons in the chlorophyll a reaction centre of PSII to a higher energy level; (3) excited electrons pass along an electron transport chain of carriers from PSII to PSI, losing energy; (4) this energy pumps H⁺ from the stroma into the thylakoid lumen, building a steep proton gradient (also fed by photolysis protons); (5) H⁺ flow back into the stroma through ATP synthase, driving photophosphorylation of ADP + Pi to ATP (chemiosmosis - exactly parallel to mitochondria); (6) at PSI, electrons are re-excited and, along with H⁺ from the stroma, reduce NADP to NADPH (NADP + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → NADPH). NADP is the terminal electron acceptor - the direct analogue of oxygen in respiration, a favourite comparison question (compare chemiosmosis in mitochondria vs chloroplasts: membrane, proton source, acceptor, products).

Herbicide questions test the same sequence: DCMU/atrazine block electron transport at PSII, so photolysis stops, no proton gradient forms, no ATP/NADPH - the Calvin cycle stops; DCPIP (blue → colourless when reduced) substitutes for NADP as the electron acceptor in the dehydrogenase practical (AQA RP8), letting you measure electron transport rate by the rate of decolourisation. Cyclic photophosphorylation (electrons return to PSI producing ATP only, no NADPH) is examinable on some boards (OCR/WJEC) as balancing the ATP:NADPH ratio.

The light-independent reaction (Calvin cycle) (AQA 3.5.1; OCR 5.1.1; CCEA A21)

The light-independent reaction (Calvin cycle) occurs in the stroma and uses ATP and NADPH from the LDR to fix CO₂ into carbohydrate. Sequence: (1) carbon fixation: RuBisCO catalyses CO₂ combining with ribulose bisphosphate (RuBP, 5C) to form two molecules of glycerate 3-phosphate (GP, 3C); (2) reduction: each GP is reduced to triose phosphate (TP) using ATP and NADPH from the LDR (ATP provides energy, NADPH provides H/electrons); (3) regeneration: five of every six TP molecules regenerate RuBP (using ATP), so the cycle continues; (4) the remaining one in six TP is the product used to synthesise glucose (hexose: two TP combine), starch (storage), cellulose (walls), lipids and amino acids (with nitrogen). Balance the numbers: 3 CO₂ fixed → 6 GP → 6 TP → 5 TP regenerate 3 RuBP + 1 TP surplus. Interpretation questions are the highest-value skill: in darkness, GP accumulates (cannot be reduced without NADPH/ATP) while RuBP and TP fall; when light returns, TP recovers first. If CO₂ is removed, GP and TP fall (no fixation) while RuBP accumulates (cannot be used) - learn both directions and which molecule moves which way under each change; this distinguishes candidates who memorise from those who understand the cycle. RuBisCO is the most abundant enzyme on Earth and is inefficient - it also catalyses oxygen fixation (photorespiration), reducing yield; the 6-marker often asks why RuBisCO's oxygenase activity wastes fixed carbon.

Regulation and yield: the cycle's output depends on the balance between carboxylation (using RuBP) and reduction (using ATP/NADPH) - if either runs short, TP is diverted away from RuBP regeneration and sugar synthesis slows; stromal phosphate concentration and stromal pH also regulate the cycle, since ATP synthase needs a proton gradient maintained by the LDR. RuBisCO's inefficiency is a high-value point: it works slowly (~3 reactions per second) and also catalyses the reaction of O₂ with RuBP (photorespiration), wasting fixed carbon and ATP with no sugar gain - explaining why crop yields are limited and why C4 plants (maize) with CO₂-concentrating mechanisms outperform C3 plants in hot, dry conditions.

Limiting factors and agricultural applications (AQA 3.5.1; OCR 5.1.1; Edexcel A T5)

The limiting factor is the factor in shortest supply determining the rate. Three are examinable: light intensity - rate increases linearly at low light (light is limiting), then plateaus at high light because another factor (CO₂ or temperature) becomes limiting; the plateau level depends on CO₂ concentration and temperature. CO₂ concentration - atmospheric CO₂ (~0.04%) is usually the main limiting factor for C3 crops; increasing CO₂ (greenhouses at ~0.1%) raises the rate until light or temperature limits. Temperature - rate follows enzyme kinetics: increases to an optimum (~25-30 °C for most plants), then falls as enzymes (RuBisCO) denature; also increases photorespiration and transpiration. Graph interpretation is the standard exam skill: on a light-response curve, the initial gradient shows the photosystems' efficiency, the plateau shows the next limiting factor, and the x-intercept is the compensation point - the light intensity at which photosynthesis rate = respiration rate, so net gas exchange is zero; below it, plants respire more than they photosynthesise and lose biomass. Two curves at different CO₂ concentrations: the higher CO₂ curve plateaus higher - identify which factor limits on each section (low light = light; plateau at low CO₂ = CO₂; plateau at high CO₂ = temperature or another factor).

Agricultural optimisation (glasshouse questions, AQA/Edexcel): commercial glasshouses control light (supplementary lighting, reflective surfaces), CO₂ (burners/enrichment to ~0.1%), temperature (heating, ventilation) and water (hydroponics) to raise yields; the economics matter - added CO₂/light must raise yield enough to pay for it (marginal cost vs marginal yield), an A* evaluation point. Application to exam scenarios: given a glasshouse scenario, identify the limiting factor from the data (e.g. yield rises with CO₂ enrichment until it plateaus - then light or temperature limits), and suggest the next intervention. Photosynthesis and global carbon: net primary productivity feeds ecosystems (see ecology topic), and increased atmospheric CO₂ can raise photosynthesis (CO₂ fertilisation) but is limited by other factors - linking this topic to climate-change questions.

CO₂ fertilisation and crop data: raising CO₂ raises photosynthesis until another factor limits - interpret two-curve graphs by identifying the limiting factor on each segment (initial slope = light; plateau at low CO₂ = CO₂; plateau at high CO₂ = temperature/enzymes). Economics is the evaluation angle: enrichment only pays if the marginal yield increase exceeds the marginal cost of fuel/lamps - so commercial glasshouses enrich CO₂ during daylight when light is not limiting, and supplement light only when natural light falls below the compensation point; ventilation prevents overheating. Global context: elevated atmospheric CO₂ raises NPP slightly (CO₂ fertilisation) but the effect is limited by nutrients and water - linking photosynthesis to ecosystem productivity questions.

Required practicals: investigating photosynthesis (AQA RP7/RP8; OCR PAG11)

AQA RP7 (chromatography of leaf pigments): crush leaves in propanone (solvent), spot onto chromatography paper or a TLC plate, run in a suitable solvent, then calculate Rf values for each pigment (Rf = pigment distance ÷ solvent distance) and identify chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, carotene and xanthophyll. Marks for: safe solvent handling, applying small concentrated spots, keeping the origin above the solvent line, and correct Rf calculations with comparison to reference values.

AQA RP8 (dehydrogenase activity in chloroplasts): isolate chloroplasts in cold isotonic buffer (cold - slows enzyme damage; isotonic - prevents osmotic lysis), add DCPIP, and illuminate; DCPIP (blue) accepts electrons from the electron transport chain and decolourises, substituting for NADP; the rate of decolourisation (measured by colorimetry against a calibration curve, or by time to decolourise) estimates the rate of electron transport. Variables to test: light intensity (distance from lamp), wavelength (coloured filters), herbicide presence (DCMU stops decolourisation). Controls and errors: keep a dark control tube (should not decolourise - confirms light-dependence), keep temperature constant, use the same chloroplast concentration; quote sources of error (variation in chloroplast concentration, lamp heating the samples - use a water bath/heat filter).

OCR PAG11 covers measuring photosynthesis rate: pondweed (Elodea) oxygen production (counting bubbles or collecting O₂ in a gas syringe - volume per minute), or data-logging CO₂/pH. Bubbles are imprecise (size varies, some dissolve); gas-syringe collection is more accurate. Interpretation: rate ∝ oxygen produced; compare with respiration at night; the compensation point explains why plants grown indoors need supplementary light. Isotope evidence (occasionally examinable): ¹⁸O-labelled water shows the oxygen evolved in photosynthesis comes from water (photolysis), not CO₂ - a classic experiment supporting the LDR mechanism.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: AQA-style (3.5.1, 5 marks): Describe how ATP and NADPH are produced in the light-dependent reaction of photosynthesis.
Mark-scheme logic: Model answer logic (1 mark per point, 5 total): (1) Light absorbed by chlorophyll a in photosystem II excites electrons to a higher energy level. (2) Electrons lost from chlorophyll are replaced by photolysis of water (2H₂O → 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ + O₂). (3) Electrons pass along an electron transport chain; energy released pumps H⁺ from the stroma into the thylakoid lumen, building a proton gradient. (4) H⁺ flow back through ATP synthase into the stroma drives photophosphorylation of ADP + Pi to ATP (chemiosmosis). (5) At photosystem I, electrons are re-excited and, with H⁺ from the stroma, reduce NADP to NADPH (NADP + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → NADPH).

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Describe the light-independent reaction of photosynthesis and state the uses of triose phosphate. (6 marks)
Answer: In the stroma, RuBisCO catalyses CO₂ combining with ribulose bisphosphate (RuBP, 5C) to form two molecules of glycerate 3-phosphate (GP, 3C). Each GP is reduced to triose phosphate (TP) using ATP and NADPH from the light-dependent reaction. Five of every six TP regenerate RuBP (using ATP) so the cycle continues. The remaining TP is used to synthesise glucose (two TP), starch for storage, cellulose for cell walls, and lipids/amino acids. The cycle therefore requires the continuous supply of ATP and NADPH from the thylakoid membranes.
Explain what happens to the concentrations of GP, TP and RuBP if a plant is placed in darkness. (4 marks)
Answer: In darkness, no ATP or NADPH are produced by the light-dependent reaction, so GP cannot be reduced to TP - GP accumulates. TP concentration falls because it is still being used (to regenerate RuBP and make glucose) but is not being replaced. RuBP falls because it continues to be carboxylated to GP but is not regenerated from TP. Glucose synthesis therefore slows or stops.
Explain why oxygen is essential for aerobic ATP production. (3 marks)
Answer: Oxygen acts as the terminal electron acceptor at complex IV of the electron transport chain, combining with electrons and protons to form water (½O₂ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂O). This maintains the flow of electrons along the chain and the proton gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane. Without oxygen, oxidative phosphorylation stops and the chemiosmotic ATP yield is lost.
A student runs a chromatogram of leaf pigments; a pigment moves 4.2 cm and the solvent front moves 8.4 cm. Calculate the Rf value and explain how Rf values are used. (3 marks)
Answer: Rf = distance moved by pigment ÷ distance moved by solvent front = 4.2 ÷ 8.4 = 0.50. Rf values are characteristic of each pigment under fixed conditions; they are compared with reference values to identify chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, carotene and xanthophylls. Consistent conditions (same solvent, temperature) are required for valid comparison.
Describe how the rate of photosynthesis can be investigated using DCPIP and isolated chloroplasts. (5 marks)
Answer: Chloroplasts are isolated in cold isotonic buffer (cold slows enzyme degradation; isotonic prevents osmotic lysis). DCPIP, which is blue and accepts electrons like NADP, is added to the chloroplast suspension. The mixture is illuminated; electrons passing along the electron transport chain reduce DCPIP, decolourising it from blue to colourless. The rate of decolourisation (time taken or colorimetry with a calibration curve) estimates the rate of electron transport and hence photosynthesis. A dark control tube confirms light-dependence; variables such as light intensity or wavelength can be tested.
Explain how a commercial glasshouse increases the rate of photosynthesis, and evaluate the economics. (5 marks)
Answer: Supplementary lighting increases light intensity when natural light is limiting. CO₂ enrichment (to ~0.1%) raises the rate when CO₂ is limiting. Heating maintains the optimum temperature for enzymes such as RuBisCO. Together these raise yields beyond field conditions. Evaluation: each intervention costs money (fuel, lamps, CO₂ burners); they should only be applied when that factor is limiting, and only up to the point where the extra yield pays for the extra cost - beyond the plateau, further input is wasted. Ventilation and shading may be needed to prevent overheating.
