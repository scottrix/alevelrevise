Drama: Modern and Set Plays

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC/Eduqas, CCEA

A-Level drama revision: A Streetcar Named Desire, A Doll's House and The Duchess of Malfi - dramatic methods (staging, dialogue, dramatic irony), genre debate, context, AO5 critical readings and essay structure for AQA, Edexcel and OCR.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Analyse dramatic methods: staging, props, lighting, sound, dialogue registers, dramatic irony, asides and soliloquy (AO2).
  • Evaluate genre debates - modern tragedy, problem play, revenge tragedy, 'plastic theatre' - as AO5 arguments with a committed judgement.
  • Situate plays in contexts of production and reception: Jacobean court, 1879 Norway, post-war America (AO3).
  • Adapt planning between passage-based and whole-text questions, using stage directions as evidence (AO1/AO2).
  • Weigh critical readings (Eliot on Webster, Shaw on Ibsen, Williams on his own craft) and reach a supported view (AO5).

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Dramatic irony: The audience knows what a character does not: the audience knows Blanche's Laurel past when Mitch demands truth, and knows Krogstad's letter before the Helmers do.
Soliloquy: A speech alone on stage exposing thought directly; Bosola's soliloquies turn the audience into his confidants and judges.
Aside: A remark to the audience unheard by other characters; Jacobean drama uses asides to share a malcontent's private evaluation of the scene.
Malcontent: A disaffected observer-participant who comments on and meddles in the action - Bosola in The Duchess of Malfi, descended from the Vice figure.
Well-made play: A tightly plotted 19th-century form (Scribe) built on withheld secrets and climactic revelation; Ibsen adopts its machinery in A Doll's House only to detonate it.
Problem play: A play structured around a debatable social problem rather than plot resolution; Shaw's term for Ibsen's drama of discussion.
Plastic theatre: Williams's own term (from his production notes) for expressionist devices - music, lighting, screen imagery - externalising a character's inner state.
Lycanthropia: Ferdinand's diagnosed wolf-madness in The Duchess of Malfi; Jacobean staging makes mental disorder visible spectacle.

📌 Key Points

Boards: Streetcar (AQA A Modern times core text; Edexcel 9ET0 post-1900 drama), A Doll's House (AQA B Option 2B political and social protest writing; OCR pre-1900 drama), The Duchess of Malfi (OCR Component 01 pre-1900 drama).
Write about staging as AO2 evidence: props, light, sound and exits are authorial choices, not scene-setting.
AQA essays are 25 marks; genre debate ('aspects of tragedy', 'elements of protest') must be explicit, not implied.

Dramatic methods: staging, dialogue, irony

A Streetcar Named Desire: the paper lantern is the play in miniature - Blanche's shading of the bulb is illusion as prop; the blue piano and the Varsouviana polka score her psyche (the polka recalls Allan Grey's death and stops with the gunshot); the poker night's harsh light and Stanley's smashing of the crockery stage realism defeating romance. Blanche's lyric monologues ('I don't want realism. I want magic!') fight Stanley's blunt monosyllables - register IS character. Dramatic irony runs the second half: the audience knows the Laurel history and the lost Belle Reve as Mitch learns it.

A Doll's House: Ibsen weaponises well-made play machinery - the concealed macaroons, the forged signature, the letterbox and locked door all discipline Nora. The tarantella is staged performance of her performance: she dances to postpone the letter. Her register migrates from 'little lark' dependence to measured, legally precise prose in the final dialogue, so the language itself enacts selfhood. The closing stage direction - the door slam - is the most famous sound effect in modern drama: action replaces explanation.

The Duchess of Malfi: Webster makes spectacle carry meaning - the wax corpses (Antonio's 'dead body's echo'), the madmen masque, the tomb and the echo scene locate tyranny in staged imagery. Bosola's soliloquies and asides recruit the audience as judge; Ferdinand's lycanthropia turns corrupted power into visible disease; the Duchess's wit under threat ('I am Duchess of Malfi still') makes composure the play's most radical act.

Genre debate: tragedy, problem play, revenge

Genre is an AO5 argument to conduct, not a label to state. Streetcar: is Blanche a tragic heroine or a victim of social change? AQA A candidates should weigh tragic features - a flawed protagonist, illusion as hamartia, partial self-knowledge ('Whoever you are - I have always depended on the kindness of strangers') - against the absence of catharsis: the play ends not with insight but with another gentleman caller. Williams's 'plastic theatre' (expressionist music and light) supports a modern-tragedy reading in which fate is psychological, not metaphysical.

A Doll's House: Shaw read Ibsen as the master of the problem play - drama of discussion replacing drama of plot. The ending invites the central debate: tragedy (children lost, marriage destroyed) or liberation (a comic exit inverted - the door opens outward onto freedom rather than inward onto reconciliation). Ibsen's own notes call Nora's development a modern tragedy; the forced German alternative ending (Nora collapses before her children) was dismissed by Ibsen as a 'barbaric outrage' - evidence that the genre question is built into reception history.

The Duchess of Malfi: a revenge tragedy whose conventional machinery - malcontent, mad scene, murders, delayed retribution - serves a heroine who lacks the conventional hamartia: her 'fault' is marrying beneath rank. The tragedy is structural misogyny, so the genre's own rules become the target. Weigh 'tragedy of villainy' (Ferdinand, Bosola) against 'tragedy of endurance' (the Duchess's conduct on the scaffold).

Context, AO5 and essay structure

Streetcar: premiered 1947, directed by Elia Kazan; New Orleans's Elysian Fields ironically names a dying myth amid industrial immigration; Blanche's Belle Reve nostalgia registers the defeated agrarian South against Stanley's post-war, second-generation America. Gender matters: Blanche's survival strategies are the only economy a woman of her class is permitted. A Doll's House: 1879 Norway, where married women could not legally borrow without consent - Nora's forgery dramatises the law itself; reception was scandalous across Europe. The Duchess of Malfi: written c.1613-14 for the Jacobean court, a culture fascinated by corruption, widows' contested autonomy and poisoning; Webster inherits revenge conventions while probing whether a woman may own her body and title.

AO5 readings: T.S. Eliot - 'Webster was much possessed by death / And saw the skull beneath the skin' - frames Webster's realism about mortality; Shaw's The Quintessence of Ibsenism read Nora as the herald of individual freedom; on Streetcar, productions since Kazan alternate sympathy between Blanche and Stanley - cite performance history as evolving interpretation.

Essay structure (25 marks): open by naming the debate the question stages; each paragraph takes ONE dramatic method (a prop, a register, a sound cue) and tracks its development across the play; quote stage directions as readily as dialogue - they are authorial text; AO3 appears as production and reception context, not biography; finish with a judgement that answers the question's proposition. For passage questions, analyse the extract densely first, then two or three wider sentences outward - 'developed elsewhere when...'.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: AQA B (7717) Paper 2, Option 2B style (25 marks): 'Nora's rebellion is justified by the society that has made her powerless.' In the light of this view, discuss how Ibsen presents Nora in A Doll's House.
Mark-scheme logic: Mark-scheme logic: AO1 - thesis with sustained judgement; AO2 - dramatic methods (props, register, staging) with effects; AO3 - context of production and reception woven in; AO5 - protest-writing lens weighed against other readings. Thesis (AO1): Ibsen engineers the play so Nora's rebellion reads as the only rational response to legal and emotional infantilisation - justification is structural, not personal. Paragraphs (AO2): the macaroons and forgery as staged transgressions against her 'doll' status; the tarantella as performance of the performance; the shift from 'little lark' diction to legal prose as liberation made audible; the door slam as the argument's final clause. AO3: 1879 Norwegian marriage law, the German alternative ending Ibsen called a 'barbaric outrage', Shaw's reading. AO5: weigh 'elements of political and social protest writing' (oppression, power, rebellion) against readings that stress family tragedy; conclude that Ibsen stages both - and makes the social reading win on the evidence.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

'Blanche is destroyed by her own illusions rather than by Stanley's brutality.' In the light of this view, discuss how Williams presents Blanche (Edexcel 9ET0 style).
Answer: AO5: conduct the debate. Illusion reading: the paper lantern, bathings as purification ritual, 'I don't want realism. I want magic!' - self-deception precedes Stanley. Brutality reading: Stanley's investigation of her past, the birthday scene's staged cruelty (bus ticket, 'Queen Blanche'), the assault - society finishes what illness started. AO2: light and sound cues (bare bulb, polka) trace the collapse; register conflict between lyricism and monosyllables. AO3: post-war New Orleans, the fallen gentry South. Judgement: Williams makes illusion her armour and Stanley the swordsman - the two readings are one tragedy; commit to the better-evidenced emphasis.
'The Duchess is destroyed by her own transgression rather than her brothers' cruelty.' How far do you agree? (OCR H472 style).
Answer: AO5: first define the 'transgression' - a widow's secret marriage beneath her rank, a legal right that custom polices. Against the view: Ferdinand and the Cardinal's responses (imprisonment, madmen, wax figures, strangling) are disproportionate spectacle, exposing tyranny rather than justice; Bosola's eventual judgement confirms the brothers' guilt. For the view: secrecy invites the tragedy; the play punishes concealment more than marriage. AO2: the proposal scene's ring and contract language makes her agency visible; 'I am Duchess of Malfi still' as composure under erasure. AO3: Jacobean court culture, widows' contested autonomy. Judgement: Webster loads the genre's punitive machinery against a lawful act - the tragedy indicts the brothers' world; commit.
How does Williams use staging and sound to present Blanche's inner life in A Streetcar Named Desire?
Answer: Method-first paragraphs. Light: the shaded paper lantern vs Mitch tearing it off vs the paper lantern re-bought - illusion's lifecycle in one prop. Sound: the Varsouviana polka stops with the revolver shot; the blue piano voices the district's vitality and Stanley's world; street cries and locomotives intrude raw reality. Space: the two rooms force coexistence of registers; the back wall becomes transparent when Blanche imagines death. AO2 effect: plastic theatre externalises psychology so the audience hears her mind before she speaks it. AO3: expressionism entering American stagecraft. Judgement: staging carries the tragedy Stanislavskian dialogue alone could not.
'The play's ending is a victory, not a tragedy.' In the light of this view, discuss how Ibsen presents Nora's exit.
Answer: AO5: victory reading - the door slam as decisive, articulate action; Nora's register shift to legal prose shows selfhood achieved; the 'greatest miracle' reframed as education ('I must stand quite alone... to know myself'). Tragedy reading - children lost, Torvald's 'the most wonderful thing' left as fragile conditional; the well-made machinery (letter, letterbox) ensures the exit is engineered, not earned. AO2: doorway as the play's final image; Torvald alone on stage, waiting for 'the most wonderful thing'. AO3: 1879 marriage law; the scandalised receptions; Ibsen's 'barbaric outrage' alternative ending proving the ending's design. Judgement: Ibsen builds both readings into reception - commit to the protest reading on textual evidence (register, props, structure).
