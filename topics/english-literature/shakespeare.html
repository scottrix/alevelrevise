Shakespeare

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC/Eduqas, CCEA

A-Level Shakespeare revision: tragedy and comedy conventions, dramatic methods (soliloquy, dramatic irony, verse and prose, imagery), Othello, King Lear and Hamlet by board option, with context, critical readings (AO5) and 25-mark essay structure for AQA, Edexcel and OCR.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Analyse dramatic techniques - soliloquy, aside, dramatic irony, foil, subplot - and argue their effect on an audience, not just spot them (AO2)
  • Interpret Shakespearean language: blank verse, prose shifts, imagery clusters and wordplay, linking method to meaning (AO2)
  • Evaluate staging: set, lighting, costume, movement and directorial choices, including how performance reshapes character (AO2)
  • Explore themes of power, ambition, love, betrayal and appearance versus reality through a thesis-led argument (AO1)
  • Understand Elizabethan/Jacobean context and theatrical conventions, and weave them into analysis (AO3)
  • Deploy critical readings (feminist, Marxist, psychoanalytic; Bradley versus Leavis) as AO5 debate within your own argument

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Soliloquy: A speech delivered when the character is alone, revealing inner thoughts directly to the audience - the main engine of Shakespearean interiority.
Aside: A brief remark spoken directly to the audience that other characters on stage do not hear.
Dramatic irony: When the audience knows more than the characters do; creates tension because the outcome is visible to us before it is visible to them.
Foil: A character who contrasts with the protagonist to illuminate a particular quality - Laertes and Fortinbras as foils to Hamlet's delay; Emilia as foil to Desdemona.
Blank verse: Unrhymed iambic pentameter - the dominant medium of noble Shakespearean speech, signalling status, deliberation and elevated thought.
Iambic pentameter: A line of five iambic feet (unstressed-stressed pairs, ten syllables); deviations - shared lines, feminine endings, breaks - are analysis points, not accidents.
Hamartia: The fatal flaw or error that precipitates the tragic hero's downfall - 'vaulting ambition' in Macbeth, jealous credulity in Othello.
Peripeteia: The reversal of fortune; in Othello it arrives in the temptation scene (3.3) rather than near the end, which is itself an analytical point.
Anagnorisis: The moment of critical recognition - Othello's 'O fool, fool, fool!' (5.2) comes too late for action, intensifying tragedy.
Catharsis: Aristotle's purging of pity and fear, achieved as the audience witnesses the hero's fall and the restoration of order (Malcolm, Fortinbras, Edgar).

📌 Key Points

Soliloquy: character alone, reveals inner thoughts; aside: to audience, others do not hear
Dramatic irony: audience knows more than characters; creates tension - Iago's soliloquies versus 'honest Iago'
Blank verse: unrhymed iambic pentameter (nobles); prose: lower status, madness, intimacy
Shakespearean imagery clusters: clothing, blood, disease, nature, light/dark - track one per play
Stagecraft: minimal sets, emphasis on language in the early modern playhouse; modern productions: lighting, sound, multimedia
Tragedy: hamartia, peripeteia, anagnorisis, catharsis; comedy: marriage, mistaken identity, restoration of community

Tragedy and comedy conventions (AQA B 7717 Paper 1 Aspects of Tragedy; Edexcel 9ET0 Component 1; OCR H472 Component 1)

Tragedy. Aristotle's Poetics supplies the vocabulary examiners expect: hamartia (the fatal flaw or error of the tragic hero), hubris (destructive pride), peripeteia (reversal of fortune), anagnorisis (recognition) and catharsis (the purging of pity and fear). Shakespeare adapts rather than mechanically follows these terms, and saying so earns credit: in Othello the peripeteia comes early, in the temptation scene (3.3), where his worshipful 'Excellent wretch! Perdition catch my soul' collapses into 'Damn her, lewd minx!'; his anagnorisis ('O fool, fool, fool!') arrives in 5.2 only when action is impossible, which is precisely why the catastrophe feels inevitable. In King Lear, recognition is distributed and cruelly timed: 'I am a man more sinned against than sinning' (3.2) and the reconciliation with Cordelia ('Pray you now, forget and forgive', 4.7) precede, rather than prevent, her death - tragedy deepened, not relieved, by insight. Hamlet grafts Aristotelian tragedy onto revenge tragedy conventions inherited from Kyd's Spanish Tragedy: a ghost demanding revenge, a play-within-a-play (the Mousetrap, 3.2), a revenger who delays, and a final scene of multiple deaths that clears the court.

Genre expectations (AO3). Jacobean audiences expected tragedy to trace the fall of a great figure through a fatal error, disturbing and then restoring order: the endings install successors (Fortinbras, Malcolm, Edgar) as political closure. Comedy runs on opposite logic: marriage and reconciliation resolve the plot, mistaken identity and disguise generate error, and festive community is restored - Twelfth Night's 'golden time' or the multiple weddings of As You Like It. Where your board pairs tragedy and comedy (OCR H472; Edexcel 9ET0 offer both pathways), the highest-value move is comparative: comedy's restorability throws tragedy's irreversibility into relief, because no wedding or succession can restore Cordelia or Desdemona. For AQA B (7717) Paper 1, explicitly label the tragic features of your set play - hamartia, peripeteia, anagnorisis, catharsis - and then complicate the labels: is Othello's flaw jealousy, credulity, or a society that makes a Moorish general perpetually insecure? That last move converts a list of terms into an argument (AO1).

Dramatic methods: soliloquy, aside, dramatic irony, foil and subplot (AO2)

Soliloquy is Shakespeare's window into consciousness: character alone, thoughts exposed. The dagger soliloquy (Macbeth 2.1) is the classic worked example: the hallucinated dagger shows guilt operating before action. 'Is this a dagger which I see before me?' - a rhetorical question that dramatises a mind interrogating itself; 'bloody business' - euphemism that distances Macbeth from what he intends; the invocation of witchcraft and Hecate - the supernatural as projection of desire; 'Words to the heat of deeds too cold breath gives' - hesitation made metrical; and the rhymed 'knell/summon' couplet - finality, the decision closing like a door. An A* answer tracks the sequence: hallucination, euphemism, supernatural appeal, hesitation, resolution - the murder decided in language before it is committed on stage. Asides do smaller, quicker work: Iago's asides to the audience in 2.1 let us watch him constructing the plot while Othello and Desdemona exchange courtesies.

Dramatic irony - the audience knows more than the characters - is the master-tension of Othello: Iago's soliloquies ('Thus do I ever make my fool my purse', 1.3; 'I hate the Moor') give the audience knowledge that Othello lacks, so every repetition of 'honest Iago' agonises the spectator. Desdemona's demonstrable innocence set against Othello's growing certainty creates tragic tension, and the handkerchief plot makes catastrophe feel inevitable rather than merely possible - Shakespeare weaponises the audience's superior knowledge. Note the reversal at the end: only when Othello finally learns what we have known all along does irony collapse into recognition, and the play into tragedy.

Foils illuminate by contrast: Laertes (immediate, unreflecting revenge) and Fortinbras (armies marching for a 'straw') measure Hamlet's delay; Emilia's plain-spoken cynicism about men ('Methinks it is not very well', 4.3; the truth-telling of 5.2) throws Desdemona's faithfulness into relief and supplies the evidence that destroys Iago. Subplots thicken theme: the Gloucester story in King Lear mirrors the main plot - a father misjudges a loyal child and trusts a treacherous one - and literalises the play's sight/blindness motif: Gloucester is physically blinded only after he has been morally blind ('I stumbled when I saw', 4.1). Structural parallel is AO2 gold: two plots, one argument about misjudgement.

Language: verse, prose shifts and imagery clusters (AO2)

Blank verse (unrhymed iambic pentameter) carries the weight of noble deliberation; prose marks lower status, madness and intimacy - but the analytical point is the shift. In Hamlet, blank verse powers the reflective soliloquies such as 'To be or not to be', signalling nobility, deliberation and authentic interiority. Prose marks performance and demotion: Hamlet adopts prose when feigning his 'antic disposition' with Polonius (2.2) and in the gravedigger scene (5.1), where comic wordplay over skulls masks genuine disillusionment with mortality. The shifts chart Hamlet's oscillation between role-play and authentic thought, distinguishing performance from reality within a single character. In Othello, Iago speaks prose with Roderigo (businesslike manipulation) but verse with Othello (the counterfeit noble confidant); more devastatingly, Othello's own elevated verse disintegrates into broken prose in the epileptic fit (4.1: 'Lie with her? lie with her?') - the loss of the grand style is the loss of the self. In King Lear, verse fractures under the storm ('unaccommodated man' is prose-adjacent bareness), so that stripped language matches stripped status.

Imagery clusters - clothing, blood, disease, nature, light and dark - are the quickest way to sound like a critic rather than a spotter. Track one cluster per play and argue what it does: Othello - animal imagery ('old black ram', 'barbary horse') supplied by Iago contaminates Othello's own diction; black/white oppositions and heaven/hell language ('divine Desdemona' versus damnation) chart his moral collapse; poison imagery ('I'll pour this pestilence into his ear') makes manipulation physical. King Lear - 'nothing' as recurring word; sight and blindness ('See better, Lear'); beasts of prey; the storm as externalised mind. Hamlet - disease and rot ('something is rotten in the state of Denmark'; the 'unweeded garden'); poison poured into the ear as image of words corrupting. Macbeth - blood that will not wash, 'borrowed robes' (clothing as ill-fitting status), and darkness that eats the stage directions. Quote cluster members verbatim, name the technique, and attach each to a moment in the argument's development - that is AO2 scoring behaviour.

Set plays by board option: Othello, King Lear, Hamlet

Know your board's format before you revise scenes, because format dictates revision. AQA A (7712) Paper 1 Section A: a Shakespeare passage question (25 marks) where you must anchor in the extract and range across the whole play; AQA B (7717) Paper 1 Aspects of Tragedy: extract work plus a whole-text essay; Edexcel (9ET0) Component 1: typically a passage-based part (a) and a whole-text part (b); OCR (H472) Component 1: close reading of an extract plus an essay. Hamlet is a Shakespeare option on Edexcel and OCR; Othello appears on AQA A and AQA B's tragedy list; King Lear anchors AQA B tragedy and Edexcel's tragedy pathway. Whatever the pairing, the same revision spine works: five key scenes per play, each with two short quotations and one method point.

Themes to organise around (and they recur in every specification): power (who holds it, who performs it - Lear divides a crown but not its burdens), ambition (Macbeth's 'vaulting ambition'), love (Othello's possessive 'murderous love', Lear's testing of it), betrayal (Iago; Goneril and Regan; Claudius), and appearance versus reality ('I am not what I am'; 'seems, madam? nay, it is'). For passage questions, practise the two-step: what is happening in the extract (language, form, staging) and where else in the play the same idea recurs - examiners' top-band wording demands 'a assured management of... connections across texts'.

Context (AO3), critical readings (AO5) and essay structure

Context (AO3) must be woven into analysis, never a bolted-on paragraph. Load-bearing contextual facts for Shakespeare: the early modern playhouse used minimal sets, so language built the scene (daylight, few props - which is why verse does so much work); companies were all-male, with boys playing Desdemona, Cordelia and Ophelia - instantly complicating gender readings; the Great Chain of Being and belief in divine order make regicide and patricide cosmic crimes; James I's Daemonologie and obsession with treason shadow Macbeth; Venice and Cyprus let Othello stage race and otherness at one remove from London. Weave, don't list: 'Iago's animal imagery trades on the period's racialised stereotypes of Moors, making Othello's self-hatred a social product as much as a personal flaw' - that sentence does AO3 and AO1 together.

Critical readings (AO5) are debate material, not decoration. Character-based criticism: A.C. Bradley (Shakespearean Tragedy, 1904) reads Othello as a noble hero destroyed by Iago - the traditional reading. The counter-tradition: F.R. Leavis dismantled Bradley's Othello as 'self-dramatising' - vain, egotistical, already prepared to believe the worst. Feminist readings recentre Desdemona and Emilia: women whose testimony is dismissed until it is too late, in a play where the men decide what truth is; Lady Macbeth's 'unsex me here' and 'take my milk for gall' invert patriarchal norms - a claim to power expressed as punishment of femininity. Marxist readings of Lear foreground dispossession: 'O, reason not the need', Poor Tom as vagrant, the stripping on the heath as social exposure. Psychoanalytic readings: Ernest Jones's Oedipal Hamlet (procrastination as repressed desire) and Macbeth's guilt as divided psyche. Use critics as springboards: quote or paraphrase, then test against the text - 'Jones's Oedipal reading explains the closet scene (3.4), but the Mousetrap's explicit purpose - to catch the king's conscience - suggests political, not psychosexual, motive.'

Essay structure. Plan for 5 minutes: thesis in one sentence, then 4-5 PEEL paragraphs (Point, Evidence, Explanation, Link back to the question), then 5 minutes checking. For a 25-mark essay: AO1 = a clear, consistent argument; AO2 = methods (soliloquy, dramatic irony, verse/prose, imagery, staging) with effects; AO3 = context woven into those effects; AO5 = at least one alternative reading genuinely weighed. For the passage question, spend the first third inside the extract (annotate it), then deliberately sweep to two or three moments elsewhere in the play - the mark scheme rewards 'connections across the whole text'. Time discipline: 5 minutes planning, 45 writing, 5 checking, and answer the question actually set, not the one you revised.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: AQA B (7717) Paper 1 style (25 marks): 'Othello's tragedy is caused by Iago's manipulation rather than any flaw in Othello himself.' In the light of this view, discuss how Shakespeare presents Othello.
Mark-scheme logic: Mark-scheme logic: AO1 consistent argument (thesis + judgement), AO2 dramatic methods with effects, AO3 context woven, AO5 alternative readings weighed. Thesis (AO1): Iago is the proximate cause, but Shakespeare engineers the tragedy so that Iago's poison lands on soil Othello has already prepared - jealousy and insecurity are Othello's hamartia, fertilised by a racist society. AO2 paragraph 1: Iago's soliloquies and asides build the plot ('Thus do I ever make my fool my purse', 1.3), and dramatic irony makes every 'honest Iago' agonising - but note that Iago never lies outright; he arranges truths (the handkerchief, Cassio's exit) for Othello to misread, so credulity is Othello's contribution. AO2 paragraph 2: the temptation scene's staged two-hander (3.3) shows manipulation as technique - Iago's hesitant 'Ha! I like not that' plants suspicion while appearing reluctant; Othello's own diction then degrades ('Damn her, lewd minx!'), and his verse collapses into broken prose in the fit of 4.1 - method proving internal collapse, not merely external pressure. AO2 paragraph 3: animal and poison imagery - once Iago's 'old black ram' and 'pestilence' enter Othello's speech, we watch a mind colonised: the imagery tracks the transfer of fault between villain and hero. AO3: Venice needs Othello's soldiering but makes his status conditional; a Jacobean audience primed by racialised stereotypes of Moors makes 'credulity' partly a social product - woven, one sentence. AO5: Bradley's noble hero (tragedy of manipulation) versus Leavis's self-dramatising egotist (tragedy of vanity); a feminist counter-view centres Desdemona and Emilia - the real tragedy is that women's testimony is structurally unheard. Judgement: Iago supplies the mechanism, Othello the flaw, Venice the conditions - a three-cause thesis that answers 'to what extent' with a proportion, which is what the top band requires.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

How does Shakespeare use dramatic irony in 'Othello' to heighten tension?
Answer: Iago's soliloquies ('Thus do I ever make my fool my purse', 1.3; 'I hate the Moor') give the audience knowledge that Othello lacks, so every repetition of 'honest Iago' agonises the spectator. Desdemona's demonstrable innocence against Othello's growing certainty creates tragic tension, and the handkerchief plot makes catastrophe feel inevitable rather than merely possible - the widening gap between what the audience knows and what Othello believes is the engine of the play's suspense. Extend for A*: the irony inverts in 5.2, when Othello finally learns what the audience has always known; irony collapses into anagnorisis, and suspense into tragedy. Emilia's late truth-telling ('Moor, she was chaste') transfers knowledge from audience to stage, ending the play's ironies with her death.
Compare the effects of Shakespeare's use of verse and prose in 'Hamlet'.
Answer: Blank verse (unrhymed iambic pentameter) carries the weight of the reflective soliloquies such as 'To be or not to be', signalling nobility, deliberation and authentic interiority. Prose marks madness, status drops and intimacy: Hamlet adopts prose when feigning his 'antic disposition' with Polonius (2.2) and in the gravedigger scene (5.1), where comic wordplay over Yorick's skull masks genuine disillusionment with mortality. The shifts between verse and prose therefore chart Hamlet's oscillation between performance and authentic thought, letting Shakespeare distinguish role-play from reality within a single character. Add for A*: prose also belongs to Claudius's courtiers and to Hamlet's coarse teasing of Ophelia (3.2) - prose as social levelling and as the register in which uncomfortable truths (death, corruption, sex) can be spoken.
'The tragic hero's downfall is caused by external forces rather than his own hamartia.' To what extent do you agree with reference to 'Macbeth'?
Answer: External forces are powerful: the witches' equivocation ('none of woman born'), Lady Macbeth's manipulation ('unsex me here'; taunts of cowardice) and Duncan's fateful visit create the conditions for murder. Yet the Aristotelian hamartia matters more: 'vaulting ambition' (1.7) exists before the witches speak ('rapt withal' at their first greeting shows the desire already there), and Macbeth freely chooses regicide knowing its moral cost ('we but teach bloody instructions'), then compounds it with Banquo's murder without prompting. Judgement: external forces precipitate the tragedy, but Shakespeare's emphasis on deliberate choice makes hamartia the decisive cause, satisfying the cathartic logic of Jacobean tragedy in which order is restored by Macbeth's destruction (Malcolm's 'Graces... exhale'). AO3 woven: regicide after the Gunpowder Plot (1605) made the crime politically electric for James I's court.
Analyse the significance of the Gloucester subplot in 'King Lear'.
Answer: The Gloucester story mirrors the main plot with architectural precision: a father misjudges a loyal child (Edgar as Cordelia) and trusts a treacherous one (Edmund as Goneril/Regan), so Shakespeare builds theme into structure - two plots, one argument about misjudgement. Gloucester's blinding literalises the play's central metaphor: he is physically blinded only after being morally blind ('I stumbled when I saw', 4.1), while Lear, who sees 'plainly' on the heath, loses his authority before his eyes - the two plots distribute the same insight differently. Edmund's malcontent soliloquy ('Thou, Nature, art my goddess') adds a social and Machiavellian dimension: grievance against primogeniture drives villainy, making the subplot do political (AO3) as well as structural work. A* extension: the subplot is also more comic and more grotesque (the blinding on stage, Gloucester's 'rescue' at Dover), testing whether tragedy can absorb spectacle - a genuine AO5 performance-history debate.
Evaluate a feminist reading of Desdemona's role in 'Othello'.
Answer: Support: Desdemona's voice is structurally discounted - she pleads for Cassio and is not believed; Emilia's testimony ('Moor, she was chaste') arrives too late; the play's women are discussed in property terms ('my frustrate stirring') and punished for male insecurity. In an all-male theatre, a boy actor playing Desdemona makes her silencing doubly staged. Challenge: Desdemona is not passive - she elopes, defends her marriage before the senate ('I saw Othello's visage in his mind'), and her final lines protect Othello ('A guiltless death I die') - though that last loyalty can be read either as agency or as internalised obedience. Judgement: the feminist reading is strongest when it attacks the play's evidentiary structure - men decide what counts as proof (the handkerchief, Emilia's witness) - rather than when it simply labels Desdemona a victim; that refined version earns AO5 credit without flattening the character.
How does the dagger soliloquy (Macbeth 2.1) reveal Macbeth's state of mind?
Answer: The hallucinated dagger shows guilt operating before action: 'Is this a dagger which I see before me?' - a rhetorical question dramatising a mind interrogating itself, projecting desire outward as a weapon. 'Bloody business' is euphemism - Macbeth names the murder obliquely, showing he cannot face it directly; the appeal to witchcraft and Hecate externalises blame onto the supernatural. 'Words to the heat of deeds too cold breath gives' concedes hesitation in the very metre that then resolves: the rhymed 'knell/summon' couplet gives the decision finality, the murder fixed in language before it is committed. Track the sequence for AO2 - hallucination, euphemism, externalisation, hesitation, resolution - and link (AO1): the soliloquy establishes that Macbeth's tragedy is psychological, made in the mind, which is why Bradley's character-centred reading has proved so durable (AO5).
