Comparative and Critical Reading

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC/Eduqas, CCEA

A-Level comparative and critical reading revision: comparative essay method across AQA, Edexcel and OCR, critical lenses (feminist, Marxist, historicist, psychoanalytic), AQA critical anthologies, unseen prose technique and AO weightings.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Structure integrated comparative essays - one point of contact per paragraph across both texts, never text-then-text (AO1/AO4).
  • Apply critical lenses (feminist, Marxist, historicist, psychoanalytic) to textual evidence and weigh the readings produced (AO5).
  • Use AQA critical anthologies (7712 NEA; 7717 Theory and independence) as springboards for readings, not substitutes for evidence.
  • Execute a timed unseen-prose method for contextual linking questions (AQA A Paper 2 Section B, 25 marks).
  • Recall AO weightings and per-question AO emphasis across boards, and direct revision accordingly.

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

AO4: Explore connections across literary texts - 12% of the AQA A-level; rewarded whenever comparisons go beyond shared theme to shared method and genre effect.
Comparative essay: An essay organised by points of contact (idea plus method) with both texts' evidence in each paragraph and a judgement about similarity and difference.
Critical lens: A coherent critical framework (feminist, Marxist, historicist, psychoanalytic) used to generate readings; a tool to apply to evidence, not a name to drop.
Feminist criticism: Reads texts through gender, power and the construction of women; Showalter's gynocriticism and Gilbert and Gubar's 'madwoman' readings are A* touchstones.
Marxist criticism: Reads texts through class, labour and commodification (Eagleton); base and superstructure explain Gatsby's geography, Gilead's economy of bodies and Nora's contract marriage.
New historicism: Reads texts as embedded in the discourses of their moment (Greenblatt); insists interpretation changes over time - the assumption behind AQA's NEA requirement.
Psychoanalytic criticism: Reads texts through desire, repression and the double (Freud's uncanny, dream-work); illuminates Frankenstein's Creature and Blanche's rehearsals of self.
Synoptic assessment: Assessment drawing on the whole course: connections, methods and contexts across genres and periods, built into every AQA paper.

📌 Key Points

AQA A-level AO weightings (same AOs set by Ofqual for all boards): AO1 28%, AO2 24%, AO3 24%, AO4 12%, AO5 12%.
AQA A NEA (Independent critical study, texts across time): 2,500 words + bibliography, two texts, at least one pre-1900, 50 marks - AO1 7, AO2 6, AO3 6, AO4 3, AO5 3; interpretations over time required on at least one text.
AQA B Paper 2 is open book: set-text essay, unseen passage, and an essay linking two texts - comparison is the core skill.

Comparative essay method across the boards

Every board's comparative task rewards the same architecture. AQA B (7717) Paper 2: essays linking two genre texts, open book, plus an unseen passage in contextual linking. AQA A (7712) Paper 2: Section B pairs an unseen extract (25) with an essay linking two texts (25), open book. AQA A NEA: a 2,500-word critical study of two texts (one pre-1900) with bibliography - 50 marks, teacher-assessed, moderated by AQA. Edexcel (9ET0) Paper 2: one comparative essay on two prose texts, one pre-1900, linked by theme. OCR (H472) Component 2: a comparative and contextual study organised by topic areas such as Dystopia, The Gothic or Women in Literature.

The method that scores: a thesis with tension ('both texts dramatise X, but where Text A makes X structural, Text B makes it psychological'); each paragraph opens with ONE point of contact - idea fused with method ('both use confined spaces: Gilead's wardrobe, the Helmers' letterbox') - then gives both texts' evidence, hinged with 'whereas', 'while', 'where'. Keep evidence 50/50 and alternate its order so neither text dominates. Close by judging the significance of the difference, not summarising the similarity.

Critical lenses and the AQA anthologies

Lenses are engines for readings. Feminist: reads power and the construction of women - Offred as state property ('We are two-legged wombs'), Nora as a doll, the Duchess as a widow whose autonomy the law resents, Briony as a woman claiming authorial power. Marxist: reads class and commodification - Gatsby's West Egg aspiration against inherited East Egg capital, Gilead's rationing of bodies, Streetcar's landlord economy, marriage as contract in A Doll's House. New historicist: reads texts inside their discourses and insists interpretation moves - the assumption behind AQA's requirement that NEA considers interpretations over time on at least one text. Psychoanalytic: reads desire and the double - Frankenstein's Creature as Victor's uncanny double, Blanche's rehearsed selves, Briony's guilt-driven revision.

The anthologies: AQA's 7712 NEA critical anthology supplies short extracts on feminist, Marxist, postcolonial, ecocritical and other approaches; AQA B's Theory and independence anthology adds narrative theory alongside critical extracts. Use them as springboards: quote one phrase of a critic, then out-argue it with two of your own textual examples. A* AO5 is not a critics list - it is a reading generated by a lens, tested against evidence, and weighed against a second reading before you commit.

Lens-to-evidence drills
  • Claim (lens) + evidence (method) + counter-reading + judgement - in that order, every time.
  • One critic per paragraph maximum; name them only to deploy or displace an idea.
  • Turn every lens claim into an AO2 sentence: what the author DOES to make the reading available.

Unseen prose technique and AO weightings

Unseen prose method (AQA A Paper 2 Section B - unseen extract, 25 marks, about 50 minutes): read twice - first for situation and narrative voice, second for method; fix tense and time (retrospective? present-tense immediacy?); annotate imagery, form, register and any gap between narrator and protagonist; note context hints the text itself supplies; plan five minutes; write method-first paragraphs, linking to your set texts where the question requires (Section B also asks for an essay linking two texts, so comparison muscles must be warm). AQA B's unseen passage can be poetry, prose, drama or literary non-fiction - the same checklist holds.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: AQA B (7717) Paper 2, Option 2B style (25 marks, open book): 'Texts of protest make power visible by staging powerlessness.' Compare how two of your set texts present power and powerlessness.
Mark-scheme logic: Mark-scheme logic: AO1 - comparative thesis with tension and judgement; AO2 - methods (narrative voice, props, colour coding, form) with effects in both texts per paragraph; AO3 - contexts of oppression woven in; AO5 - critical lenses weighed. Thesis (AO1): both texts stage powerlessness to indict the power that produces it - Atwood's colour-coded castes make powerlessness visible as uniform, while Ibsen's props (letterbox, locked door) make Nora's powerlessness domestic and legal. Paragraphs (AO2) take one point of contact each: bodies as property (Offred's 'two-legged wombs'; Nora's forgery prosecuted), language as control (Offred's permitted vocabulary; Torvald's pet names), escape as performance (Offred's narration; Nora's tarantella). AO3: 1980s authoritarianism; 1879 Norwegian marriage law. AO5: weigh feminist and Marxist lenses, then judge which text indicts power more completely - and commit.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Plan an AQA A NEA-style comparative critical essay (feminist lens) on The Handmaid's Tale and The Duchess of Malfi.
Answer: Title: 'Women's bodies, men's titles: property and power in The Handmaid's Tale and The Duchess of Malfi.' Thesis: both expose patriarchal systems that convert women's bodies into assets - Gilead by state law, Malfi by family honour - but Atwood lets the woman narrate resistance while Webster lets the Duchess only perform it. Plan (AO1 7/AO2 6/AO3 6/AO4 3/AO5 3 of 50): bodies as property ('two-legged wombs'; the marriage ring), naming (Offred's patronymic; 'Duchess of Malfi still'), confinement (wardrobe vs tomb), resistance (narration vs composure). AO5: feminist readings weighed against historicist caution; interpretations over time on The Handmaid's Tale. Conclusion: judge which text indicts its system more completely.
Apply a Marxist lens to The Great Gatsby and A Doll's House in a comparative essay (25 marks).
Answer: Thesis: both texts commodify intimacy - Gatsby's love is spent on purchased spectacle, Nora's marriage is a contract she has secretly broken to service. Paragraphs: class as geography (East Egg's inherited capital vs West Egg's aspiration; Torvald's bank-manager respectability), money as language ('her voice is full of money'; the loan), labour and disguise (Gatsby's invented biography; Nora's copying work). AO2: Fitzgerald's symbol cluster (green light, valley of ashes) against Ibsen's props (letterbox, macaroons). AO3: prohibition-era mobility myth; 1879 credit law. AO5: weigh the Marxist reading against romantic readings; judge that both texts critique the commodity but only Ibsen lets its product walk out - commit with evidence.
How should you approach the unseen prose extract in AQA A Paper 2 Section B? Give a worked method.
Answer: Read twice: situation and narrative voice, then method. Fix tense and time; identify the gap between narrator and protagonist. Annotate imagery, form, register, sentence rhythm, and context hints the text supplies. Plan five minutes - thesis plus four method points. Write method-first paragraphs: each names a technique, gives its effect on meaning, quotes briefly. Where the question links the extract to set texts, compare via the method, not the plot. Timing: about 50 of the 150 minutes. Mark-scheme logic: AO2 dominates, AO1 needs a shaped argument, AO3 uses only context the text signals - never import facts the extract cannot support.
Apply a psychoanalytic reading to Blanche (A Streetcar Named Desire) and the Creature (Frankenstein) comparatively.
Answer: Thesis: both texts build doubles - Blanche rehearses selves the South has lost, the Creature is Victor's expelled double - so desire and guilt live in split figures. Paragraphs: the uncanny double (the Creature as Victor's shadow self; Blanche's invented gentility), repression and return (the Varsouviana polka as trauma's return; the Creature's recollected rejections), naming and identity (the Creature's chosen name and 'I ought to be thy Adam'; Blanche's 'Sophia Loren' persona). AO5: weigh the psychoanalytic lens against feminist and historicist readings; judge that the double conceit explains both texts' structures better than their politics - commit with evidence from both.
'Interpretations of A Doll's House have changed more than the play.' Discuss with reference to reception over time.
Answer: Structure by period (new historicism). 1879: scandal - German theatres forced a concessionary ending Ibsen called a 'barbaric outrage'; the door slam read as social explosion. 1891: Shaw's The Quintessence of Ibsenism claims Nora as the herald of individual freedom. Later 20th century: feminist critics reread Nora through economic dependence - marriage law as the villain, not Torvald. Contemporary: readings stress the discussion scene and the unresolved 'most wonderful thing' - liberation as unfinished education. AO5 discipline: weigh each interpretation against textual evidence (props, registers, structure); judge that the play sustains all three readings because Ibsen left the machinery open - the debate, not the door, is the ending.
