Poetry Analysis

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level All Boards (AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC, CCEA) AQA

A-Level English Literature revision: Poetry Analysis. Learning objectives, key points, worked examples and practice questions across AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC and CCEA.

📌 Key Points

Key Fact: Metaphor (direct comparison), simile (like/as), personification (human qualities to non-human)
Key Fact: Imagery: visual, auditory, tactile, olfactory, gustatory; extended metaphors
Key Fact: Sound devices: alliteration, assonance, consonance, onomatopoeia, sibilance
Key Fact: Structure: stanza types, rhyme (ABAB, AABB, free verse), metre (iambic, trochaic), line breaks
Key Fact: Enjambment (run-on lines), caesura (mid-line pause), volta (turn)
Key Fact: Tone: attitude of speaker; Mood: atmosphere created; Voice: persona adopted
Key Fact: Themes: love, death, nature, time, identity, power, conflict

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Analyse poetic language: imagery, metaphor, simile, personification, symbolism
  • Understand form and structure: stanza, rhyme scheme, metre, enjambment, caesura
  • Interpret tone, mood, and voice
  • Compare poems across themes, techniques, and contexts
  • Write critical appreciations with textual evidence

💡 Worked Example

Exam-Style Question

Question: Analyse how the poet uses structure and language to convey the theme of loss in 'When You Are Old' by Yeats

Model Answer:

Three stanzas move from present to past to future. Iambic pentameter creates reflective tone. 'Glad grace' (alliteration) contrasts with 'pilgrim soul' (metaphor). Final stanza 'bending down beside the glowing bars' -- imagery of ageing/regret. ABBA rhyme encloses memory

Q3: Analyse how Wilfred Owen uses form and sound to present death in war in 'Anthem for Doomed Youth'.

Show Model Answer

Answer: The sonnet form is used ironically: the octave replaces elegiac prayers with the 'monstrous anger of the guns' and the 'stuttering rifles' rapid rattle', where alliteration and onomatopoeia mimic machine-gun fire. The sestet turns to private mourning -- 'candles', 'pall' and 'flowers' evoke domestic funeral rites denied to soldiers dying abroad. Sibilance across both stanzas suggests both bullets and sighs, and the octave-sestet contrast between industrialised slaughter and the silence of grief at home embodies Owen's argument that war destroys even the rituals of mourning.

Q4: Compare how Wordsworth in the extract from 'The Prelude' and Shelley in 'Ozymandias' present the power of nature.

Show Model Answer

Answer: Wordsworth presents nature as sublime and morally formative: the 'huge peak, black and huge' that 'upreared its head' personifies nature as a living threat that pursues the speaker into his dreams, reflecting Romantic belief in nature's educative power over the imagination. Shelley presents nature as indifferent and erasing: 'lone and level sands stretch far away' shows desert and time annihilating Ozymandias's empire, with the framed narrative (the traveller's tale) distancing human vanity from the poet's voice. The key difference: Wordsworth's nature elevates and educates the individual, while Shelley's nature annihilates human pretension altogether.

Q5: Explain the significance of the volta in one poem you have studied.

Show Model Answer

Answer: In Shakespeare's Sonnet 130, the octave catalogues apparent insults ('My mistress' eyes are nothing like the sun') that seem to deny Petrarchan idealisation entirely. The volta -- 'And yet, by heaven, I think my love as rare' -- reverses the argument, revealing that the poem celebrates honest love rather than false comparison. The turn is essential to meaning: without it the poem reads as mockery of the beloved rather than subversive praise, demonstrating how structural features carry meaning rather than merely decorating it.
