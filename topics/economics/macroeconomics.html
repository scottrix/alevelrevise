Macroeconomics

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level All Boards (AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC, CCEA) AQA

A-Level Economics revision: Macroeconomics. Learning objectives, key points, worked examples and practice questions across AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC and CCEA.

📌 Key Points

Key Fact: GDP = C+I+G+(X-M); Real = nominal/deflatorx100; Deflator = nominal/realx100
Key Fact: AD: C (wealth, confidence, rates), I (confidence, rates, tech), G (policy), X-M (exchange rate, world income)
Key Fact: SRAS: sticky wages/prices; LRAS: vertical at Yf (classical) or upward (Keynesian); AS shifts: costs, productivity
Key Fact: Inflation: demand-pull (AD>AS), cost-push (AS left); CPI basket vs RPI (housing); costs: menu, shoe-leather, uncertainty
Key Fact: Unemployment: frictional (search), structural (skills mismatch), cyclical (AD deficient), natural = frictional+structural
Key Fact: Fiscal: G↑/T↓ -> AD↑; Multiplier = 1/(1-MPC); Crowding out: G↑->r↑->I↓
Key Fact: Monetary: Bank Rate -> market rates -> AD; QE: buy bonds -> money supply↑; Inflation target 2%
Key Fact: Exchange rate: floating (market), fixed (peg); Appreciation: X↓ M↑ -> AD↓; BOP: current + capital + financial account

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Measure economic activity: GDP (expenditure, income, output), real vs nominal, GDP deflator
  • Analyse aggregate demand (C+I+G+X-M) and aggregate supply (SRAS, LRAS)
  • Understand inflation: causes (demand-pull, cost-push), costs, CPI/RPI
  • Explain unemployment: types (frictional, structural, cyclical, seasonal), natural rate
  • Evaluate fiscal policy: discretionary vs automatic stabilisers, multiplier, crowding out
  • Evaluate monetary policy: interest rates, QE, transmission mechanism, inflation targeting
  • Analyse exchange rates: determination, appreciation/depreciation effects, BOP
  • Understand supply-side policies: interventionist vs market-based, time lags

💡 Worked Example

Exam-Style Question

Question: Using AD/AS, analyse the effect of a rise in oil prices on price level and real output

Model Answer:

Oil price rise -> production costs ↑ -> SRAS shifts left. Result: price level rises (cost-push inflation), real output falls (stagflation). If central bank accommodates (AD↑), output restored but inflation higher. If not, recession

Q1: The government increases infrastructure spending by GBP 50 billion. The marginal propensity to consume (MPC) is 0.8. Calculate the size of the multiplier and the total rise in real GDP, and explain why the actual impact may be smaller.

Show Model Answer

Answer: The multiplier is the ratio of the final change in real GDP to the initial change in an injection. K = 1 / (1 − MPC) = 1 / (1 − 0.8) = 1 / 0.2 = 5. Total rise in real GDP = GBP 50 billion × 5 = GBP 250 billion. The process: the initial GBP 50bn is paid to construction firms and workers, who spend 80% of it (GBP 40bn) on other goods and services; those recipients spend 80% of that (GBP 32bn), and so on in a geometric series that converges to GBP 250bn. Evaluation: the actual impact is likely smaller because the simple formula ignores leakages — some income is spent on imports (marginal propensity to import, MPM), some is taxed away (marginal rate of tax, MPT), so the open-economy multiplier is 1 / (MPW) where MPW = MPS + MPT + MPM; if spending is financed by borrowing, interest rates may rise, crowding out private investment; there may be a time lag before spending feeds through; and if the economy is near full capacity (LRAS vertical), the extra AD causes inflation rather than real output growth. The real-world multiplier for the UK is typically estimated at around 1–1.5, well below 5.

Q2: Using an AD/AS diagram, analyse how a fall in business confidence affects the macroeconomy, and evaluate one policy the government could use to offset the effects.

Show Model Answer

Answer: Investment (I) is a component of AD (AD = C + I + G + (X − M)) and depends heavily on business confidence (Keynes' "animal spirits"). If firms expect weak future demand, they postpone or cancel capital projects, so I falls. On an AD/AS diagram, AD shifts left from AD1 to AD2. With a downward-sloping SRAS, the price level falls and real output falls, creating a negative output gap (actual output below potential) — demand-deficient (cyclical) unemployment rises. The multiplier effect means the fall in income reduces consumption further, potentially deepening the shift. Policy response: the central bank could cut the base rate — this lowers borrowing costs and mortgage repayments, raises the incentive to invest (lower hurdle rates) and may cause currency depreciation (hot money outflows) making exports more competitive, all shifting AD back right. Evaluation: monetary policy may be ineffective in a deep slump (liquidity trap / low confidence) if firms will not borrow at any rate; there are long and variable lags (12–18 months); and the policy conflict is low here since inflation is falling below target in a negative output gap, so rate cuts carry little inflation risk. Fiscal policy (higher G or lower T) is an alternative but risks crowding out and higher public debt. The most effective response depends on the severity of the shock: rate cuts for moderate downturns, but coordinated monetary and fiscal expansion for severe recessions.

Q3: Explain the difference between the classical and Keynesian views of the long-run aggregate supply curve, and assess which view better explains the economy's behaviour after a recession.

Show Model Answer

Answer: The classical LRAS is vertical at the full-employment level of output (Yf): in the long run, wages and prices are fully flexible, so markets clear and output returns to Yf regardless of AD — the economy is self-correcting. The Keynesian LRAS is L-shaped or upward-sloping: at low output levels (deep recession) it is horizontal or flat, because spare capacity means output can rise without inflation; at high output it becomes vertical as capacity constraints bind. Keynesians argue wages are sticky downwards (contracts, minimum wage, morale, union resistance), so a fall in AD causes prolonged demand-deficient unemployment rather than instant wage/price adjustment. Assessment: the Keynesian view better explains behaviour after a recession in the short to medium run — the UK after 2008 and the Eurozone after 2011 saw output gaps persist for years and unemployment stay elevated, which is inconsistent with rapid classical self-correction; monetary policy at the zero lower bound failed to restore full employment quickly, supporting Keynesian emphasis on sticky prices and demand deficiency. However, the classical view has force in the long run: output did eventually recover, and persistent unemployment fell as wages adjusted relatively (real wage flexibility) and capital was reallocated; classical insights also warn that Keynesian stimulus near full capacity causes inflation rather than output gains. Overall, the Keynesian model best explains the medium-run adjustment path, while the classical model better describes very long-run outcomes — most economists accept a hybrid where the speed of self-correction depends on wage/price flexibility.
