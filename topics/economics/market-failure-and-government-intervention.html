Market Failure and Government Intervention

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level All Boards (AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC, CCEA) AQA

A-Level Economics revision: Market Failure and Government Intervention. Learning objectives, key points, worked examples and practice questions across AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC and CCEA.

📌 Key Points

Key Fact: Externalities: divergence private/social costs/benefits; Negative: MSC>MPC (pollution); Positive: MSB>MPB (education)
Key Fact: Pigouvian tax = MEC at optimum; Subsidy = MEB; Tradable permits: cap-and-trade; Regulation: standards/bans
Key Fact: Public goods: non-excludable (can't stop use), non-rival (one's use doesn't reduce other's) -> free rider -> underprovision
Key Fact: Info asymmetry: adverse selection (pre-contract, hidden info), moral hazard (post-contract, hidden action); Solutions: signalling, screening, regulation
Key Fact: Competition: CMA investigates mergers (SLC test), cartels (price-fixing), abuse of dominance; Regulation: price caps (RPI-X), profit caps
Key Fact: Government failure: intervention worsens allocation of resources -> unintended consequences, information gaps, administrative costs, regulatory capture, rent-seeking. Nudges: choice architecture (default enrolment, salience) changes behaviour without banning options or changing prices

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Identify sources of market failure: externalities, public goods, information asymmetry, market power
  • Analyse negative/positive externalities: MSC=MPC+MEC, MSB=MPB+MEB
  • Evaluate policies: Pigouvian taxes, subsidies, tradable permits, regulation, nudges
  • Understand public goods: non-excludable, non-rival; free-rider problem
  • Analyse information asymmetry: adverse selection, moral hazard; solutions
  • Evaluate competition policy: monopolies, mergers, cartels, regulation

💡 Worked Example

Exam-Style Question

Question: A factory pollutes a river. MPC = 20+Q, MEC = 5, MPB = 100-Q. Find market output, social optimum, and tax

Model Answer:

Market: MPC=MPB -> 20+Q=100-Q -> Q=40. Social: MSC=MSB -> 20+Q+5=100-Q -> 2Q=75 -> Q=37.5. Tax = MEC = 5 per unit. At Q=37.5, P=100-37.5=62.5. Tax revenue = 5x37.5=187.5

Q1: Using a diagram, analyse the market failure that arises from the consumption of goods with negative externalities, and evaluate the use of indirect taxes to correct it.

Show Model Answer

Answer: A negative consumption externality occurs when the consumption of a good imposes costs on third parties not reflected in the market price (e.g. cigarettes, sugary drinks, petrol). The marginal social benefit (MSB) is less than the marginal private benefit (MPB): MSB = MPB − MEC. The free market equates MPB = MPC at output Q1 and price P1, but the socially optimal output is where MSB = MSC, at the lower output Q*. Between Q1 and Q*, the MSC exceeds MSB, so each unit consumed creates a deadweight welfare loss (the shaded triangle on the diagram) — society values those units at less than they cost to produce. A Pigouvian indirect tax equal to the marginal external cost (MEC) shifts the supply curve (MPC) up to MSC, raising the price to P* and reducing output to Q*, internalising the externality. Evaluation: the tax corrects the overconsumption and raises revenue that could fund healthcare or subsidies to alternatives; the size of the output reduction depends on the price elasticity of demand — demand for cigarettes is addictive and price inelastic, so a large tax causes only a small fall in output while raising most revenue from consumers (regressive, hitting low-income smokers hardest); the government faces an information gap in measuring the MEC accurately, so the tax may be set too high or low; and indirect taxes may create unintended consequences such as illicit (black-market) trade. Overall, a Pigouvian tax is the most theoretically efficient correction, but in practice its effectiveness depends on elasticity, accurate measurement and enforcement.

Q2: Explain the problem of adverse selection in insurance markets and assess the measures insurers use to reduce it.

Show Model Answer

Answer: Adverse selection is a form of market failure arising from asymmetric information before a contract is agreed: one party (the buyer of insurance) knows more about their own risk than the other (the insurer). High-risk individuals (e.g. smokers, reckless drivers, people with a family history of illness) are more likely to buy insurance because they know they are more likely to claim, while low-risk individuals find the average price too high and stay out of the market. The result is an adverse (unfavourable) selection of customers: the insurance pool is dominated by high-risk clients, so insurers must raise premiums, which drives out even more low-risk customers — a "death spiral" that can cause the market to collapse or underprovide insurance (market failure). Measures to reduce it: screening — insurers gather information through application forms, medical exams, driving records and no-claims bonuses to price policies according to individual risk; excesses/deductibles — making the claimant pay the first part of any loss deters high-risk customers who expect to claim frequently; and experience rating — adjusting premiums over time based on claims history. Evaluation: screening is costly and never perfect (information gaps remain); pricing by risk may make insurance unaffordable for genuinely high-risk groups (e.g. genetic testing raising ethical and equity issues), leading to calls for regulation such as pooled or community rating; and excesses themselves create a new problem — moral hazard is reduced but small claims go unclaimed, reducing the insurance function. Overall, screening and excesses substantially mitigate adverse selection, but government regulation is often needed where fairness and affordability conflict with efficient pricing.

Q3: Evaluate the use of tradable pollution permits as a means of reducing carbon emissions, compared with a carbon tax.

Show Model Answer

Answer: Tradable pollution permits (cap-and-trade, e.g. the EU Emissions Trading Scheme) set a cap on total emissions and issue permits that firms can buy and sell. Firms with low abatement costs sell permits to firms with high abatement costs, so pollution reduction is achieved at minimum total cost — the market finds the least-cost abatement. The cap guarantees the environmental outcome (a certain maximum quantity of emissions), unlike a tax which guarantees price but not quantity. Benefits: cost-effective, incentivises investment in clean technology (firms that reduce emissions can sell permits for profit), revenue can be raised by auctioning permits. Costs/drawbacks: the government faces an information gap in setting the initial cap — too generous and permits are worthless (EU ETS phase 1 saw the permit price collapse to near zero); too tight and firms face excessive costs; high administrative and monitoring costs; permits may be handed out free (grandfathering), giving windfall gains to incumbents and acting as a barrier to entry; and carbon leakage if firms relocate to unregulated countries. Comparison with a carbon tax: a tax is simpler to administer and gives firms price certainty for planning investment, but the government cannot be sure what quantity of emissions reduction it will buy — if demand for carbon is price inelastic, a high tax achieves little; a tax also does not automatically adjust as the economy grows. Evaluation: in theory both are efficient corrections of the negative externality, and either is superior to regulation; the choice depends on which information problem is more binding — quantity uncertainty (favours tax) or price uncertainty (favours permits) — and on political acceptability.
