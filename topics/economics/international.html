International Trade and Development

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA, Edexcel, OCR

A* revision for comparative advantage, protectionism and tariffs, trade blocs, exchange rates, the balance of payments and development strategies for AQA 7136, Edexcel A 9EC0 and OCR H460.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Calculate opportunity-cost ratios, identify comparative advantage and explain the gains from trade and the terms-of-trade window
  • Evaluate comparative advantage theory using its assumptions: constant costs, transport costs, mobility and dynamic advantage
  • Draw the tariff diagram in words, marking consumer loss, producer gain, government revenue and the two deadweight losses
  • Evaluate the arguments for and against protectionism with real examples (US 2025 tariffs, EU EV duties, Smoot–Hawley)
  • Distinguish trade creation from trade diversion and evaluate trade blocs including Brexit and the UK's CPTPP accession
  • Explain floating, fixed and managed exchange rate systems, the Marshall–Lerner condition and the J-curve effect
  • Analyse the balance of payments structure and evaluate development strategies: export-led growth vs import substitution, aid, FDI and debt relief

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Absolute advantage: A country can produce more of a good with the same resources than another country.
Comparative advantage: A country can produce a good at a lower opportunity cost than another country — the basis for specialisation and trade (Ricardo).
Tariff: A tax on imports, raising the domestic price, cutting consumption, raising domestic production and government revenue, and creating deadweight losses.
Trading bloc: A group of countries reducing trade barriers between themselves — free trade area, customs union, common market or monetary union.
Trade creation: Joining a bloc replaces high-cost domestic production with lower-cost production from members — an efficiency gain.
Trade diversion: Joining a bloc replaces lower-cost imports from non-members with higher-cost imports from members — an efficiency loss.
Marshall–Lerner condition: A devaluation improves the current account only if the sum of the price elasticities of demand for exports and imports exceeds 1.
J-curve effect: After a devaluation, the current account initially deteriorates (fixed contracts, inelastic short-run demand) before improving as elasticities rise — tracing a J shape.
Balance of payments: The record of all transactions with the rest of the world: current account (goods, services, income, transfers), capital and financial accounts; the accounts sum to zero.
Human Development Index (HDI): The UNDP index (0–1) combining life expectancy, education (mean and expected years of schooling) and GNI per capita (PPP).

Absolute and comparative advantage, and gains from trade

Absolute advantage: a country can produce more of a good with the same resources. Comparative advantage: a country can produce a good at a lower opportunity cost — the basis of trade (Ricardo). Gains from trade: specialisation according to comparative advantage raises total world output; both countries can consume beyond their production possibility frontier (PPF) through trade at terms of trade between the two opportunity-cost ratios. Worked example (real exam-style calculation): Country A: 10 labour hours produce 1 unit of cloth or 5 of wine; Country B: 6 hours produce 1 cloth or 4 wine. Opportunity costs — A: 1 cloth = 2 wine (10/5), 1 wine = 0.5 cloth; B: 1 cloth = 0.67 wine (6/4), 1 wine = 1.5 cloth. B has the comparative advantage in cloth (lower opportunity cost, 0.67 < 2), A in wine (0.5 < 1.5). A exports wine, B exports cloth; at any terms of trade between 0.5 and 1.5 wine per cloth, both gain. Mark-scheme logic: [AO1] define comparative vs absolute advantage; [AO2] compute opportunity-cost ratios accurately; [AO3] explain specialisation and the terms-of-trade window.

Assumptions and limits (the evaluation engine): the theory assumes constant opportunity costs (linear PPFs) — real PPFs are concave, so complete specialisation is never optimal; no transport costs; perfect factor mobility within countries but immobility between them (structural unemployment from specialisation — the classic cost); no exchange-rate complications; perfect information; and it ignores dynamic comparative advantage — advantage can be created (South Korea moving from textiles to electronics through investment, not endowment). Patterns of trade: the rise of China and emerging economies (China ≈ 15% of world goods exports), commodity exporters' dependence on primary products, and intra-industry trade (similar goods traded both ways within the EU) driven by economies of scale and product differentiation — which the simple theory cannot explain. Evaluation frame: trade raises world output but its distribution within countries creates winners (consumers, exporters) and losers (import-competing workers) — the source of the protectionist backlash (see the next section); and the terms of trade determine how the gains are shared.

Protectionism: tariffs, quotas and the arguments

Protectionism — restricting free trade — uses: tariffs (taxes on imports), quotas (quantity limits), subsidies to domestic producers, voluntary export restraints (VERs), administrative barriers (standards, customs delays), and exchange-rate manipulation.

Diagram description (tariff): partial equilibrium — axes price/quantity; the world price Pw is a horizontal line (the country is a price taker); domestic demand D and supply S. At Pw: domestic demand Qd, domestic supply Qs, imports = Qd − Qs. A tariff shifts the effective import supply up to Pw + tariff: domestic price rises; Qd falls to Qd1, Qs rises to Qs1; imports shrink to Qd1 − Qs1. Welfare: consumer surplus falls (areas A + B + C + D); producers gain A; government gains tariff revenue C (tariff × import volume); B (production distortion) and D (consumption distortion) are deadweight losses — welfare destroyed with no offsetting gain. Evaluation: the size of the DWL depends on elasticities (the more elastic demand and supply, the larger the loss); the tariff is regressive (essentials hit low-income households hardest); retaliation is likely and shrinks export markets; the large-country case — a big economy's tariff can lower the world price, shifting part of the burden to foreign producers (optimal tariff argument) — but only for large economies. Real-world anchors: the US tariff escalation of 2025 (25% on steel and aluminium from March 2025; 10%+ “reciprocal” tariffs from April 2025), the EU's duties on Chinese electric vehicles (up to ~45% combined, from October 2024), and the classic cautionary tale of the Smoot–Hawley tariff (1930), which deepened the Depression through retaliation.

Arguments for protectionism (know both sides): infant industry — temporary protection lets new industries reach the scale to compete (dynamic comparative advantage created); anti-dumping — countering below-cost exports; employment protection — shielding import-competing jobs (politically powerful); revenue — tariffs fund governments with weak tax systems (historically the US's main revenue source); national security — strategic industries (defence, semiconductors, energy); current account correction — reducing imports. Arguments against: DWL and higher prices for consumers; retaliation and trade wars; reduced competition → X-inefficiency in protected firms; regressive incidence; and global inefficiency — production shifts from low-cost to high-cost producers. The A* judgement: the case for protection depends on the infant-industry argument being credible (temporary, exit-credible protection) and on retaliation being avoided — both empirically fragile; the Smoot–Hawley precedent shows the systemic risk.

Trade blocs, trade creation and diversion, and the WTO

Types of trade bloc (increasing integration): a free trade area (tariffs removed between members, external tariffs set nationally — EFTA); a customs union (common external tariff — the EU Customs Union); a common market/single market (free movement of goods, services, capital and labour — the EU's four freedoms); an economic and monetary union (shared currency and monetary policy — the Eurozone, 20 members). Other blocs: USMCA (2020, replacing NAFTA), ASEAN, and CPTPP — the UK acceded and it took effect from December 2024. The WTO sets trade rules, administers dispute settlement (binding panels), and promotes liberalisation (the Doha Round stalled — multilateral liberalisation has given way to regional deals).

Trade creation vs trade diversion (the core analysis, Viner): joining a bloc creates trade when high-cost domestic production is replaced by lower-cost production from a member (efficiency gain); it diverts trade when lower-cost production from a non-member is replaced by higher-cost production from a member (efficiency loss — the external tariff does it). Chain of reasoning: customs union formed → internal tariffs fall → member goods cheaper → imports from members rise (creation); but the common external tariff makes non-member goods relatively dearer → imports shift from efficient non-members to less efficient members (diversion). Net welfare depends on which dominates — determined by the height of the external tariff and cost differences between members and non-members.

Real-world evaluation anchors: Brexit — the OBR judges the UK–EU trade arrangements will reduce long-run productivity by roughly 15% relative to remaining in the EU (trade diversion plus higher frictions: customs checks, rules-of-origin, services barriers); the UK's CPTPP accession (effective December 2024) partially diversifies trade (CPTPP covers ~15% of world GDP); the EU single market's four freedoms deliver scale economies and intra-industry trade, but monetary union without fiscal union produced the 2010–13 Eurozone crises (Greece, Ireland, Portugal). The A* judgement: deeper blocs raise trade among members but the welfare effect depends on trade creation dominating diversion; the EU's single market created trade, while Brexit arrangements diverted it — and the distribution of gains within countries (left-behind regions) drives the politics of trade.

Exchange rates, the balance of payments and external adjustment

Exchange rate systems: floating — the market determines the rate (the pound since 1992; the UK left the ERM on “Black Wednesday”, 16 September 1992, after defending a peg proved unsustainable); fixed — pegged to another currency (the Hong Kong dollar peg; the CFA franc); managed (dirty) float — market-determined with occasional central-bank intervention. Determination in a floating system: demand for the currency (exports, FDI inflows, hot money seeking yields) vs supply (imports, outflows); a rise in UK interest rates attracts hot money, appreciating the currency. Purchasing power parity (PPP): identical goods should cost the same in a common currency in the long run — deviations signal over/undervaluation (the Big Mac index); PPP holds poorly in the short run.

Effects of an appreciation: exports dearer, imports cheaper — (X − M) falls, AD shifts left; inflation falls (import prices); growth slows; the current account worsens if elasticities are adequate. Effects of a depreciation: exports cheaper, imports dearer — AD shifts right, but imported inflation raises input costs, shifting SRAS left: growth with a price rise. The Marshall–Lerner condition: a devaluation improves the current account only if PEDx + PEDm > 1 (demand for exports and imports is elastic overall). The J-curve effect: after devaluation the current account initially deteriorates (contracts fixed, quantities respond slowly — inelastic short-run demand), then recovers and improves as elasticities rise in the long run — tracing a J shape. Diagram description: axes time (horizontal) and current account balance (vertical); the line dips below its starting level, troughs, then rises above it — the J; annotate the Marshall–Lerner condition holding only after the trough. Real-world anchor: sterling fell ~10–15% after the June 2016 referendum; the current account did not improve as hoped (services and investment income effects), and inflation rose ~1–2 points — Marshall–Lerner held weakly.

The balance of payments records all transactions with the rest of the world: the current account (trade in goods, trade in services, primary income — investment income, secondary income — transfers); the capital account (small: capital transfers); and the financial account (FDI, portfolio investment, reserve assets). The accounts must sum to zero: a current account deficit is financed by financial inflows. UK data anchor: the UK runs a persistent current account deficit of roughly 3–4% of GDP (it peaked near 5% in 2016), with a large services surplus (the UK is the world's second-largest services exporter) offset by a chronic goods deficit. Evaluation: a deficit is not automatically bad — if financed by FDI (productive capital) it funds growth; if financed by hot money it is fragile (sudden stops); persistent deficits signal uncompetitiveness or overconsumption. Disequilibrium under fixed rates forces painful internal adjustment (austerity, deflation — the Eurozone periphery 2010–13); floating rates adjust automatically but with J-curve lags and inflation costs.

Growth, development and development strategies

Measuring development: the HDI (UNDP, 0–1) combines life expectancy at birth, education (mean and expected years of schooling) and GNI per capita (PPP) — a broader measure than GDP per capita; the UK scores around 0.94 (very high human development), while the lowest-ranked countries score around 0.4. Inequality: the Gini coefficient (0 = perfect equality, 1 = perfect inequality; UK disposable-income Gini ≈ 0.35) and the Lorenz curve (cumulative income share against cumulative population share; the further the bow from the 45° line, the greater the inequality — the Gini is the area ratio between them). The World Bank classifies countries by GNI per capita: low income ≈ $1,145 or below (2025 threshold); the international poverty line is $2.15/day (2017 PPP), recently rebased to $3.00/day (2021 PPP).

Barriers to development: primary product dependency — volatile commodity prices and declining terms of trade (the Prebisch–Singer hypothesis: primary exporters' terms of trade deteriorate over time, since manufactured goods' incomes rise faster); savings gap (the Harrod–Domar logic — low incomes → low savings → low investment); foreign currency gap; debt — unsustainable burdens (the HIPC initiative and subsequent restructurings — Zambia's 2020 default restructured from 2023, Sri Lanka's 2022 default restructured 2024–25); poor infrastructure and institutions; capital flight; disease and demographics.

Development strategies (the classic essay): import-substitution industrialisation (ISI) — protect infant industries behind tariffs/quotas to replace imports (Latin America, 1950s–70s); export-led growth — promote industries competing in world markets (the East Asian tigers: South Korea, Taiwan; then China). Arguments for export-led: world competition forces efficiency and innovation; economies of scale from a global market; export earnings provide foreign currency for capital goods and debt service; attracts FDI and technology transfer — the tigers grew 8–10% a year for decades while ISI economies stagnated. Arguments for ISI: infant industries need temporary protection to reach competitive scale; reduces dependence on volatile primary exports; preserves foreign exchange and jobs. Other strategies: aid (tied vs untied; the UK's aid target was cut from 0.7% to 0.5% of GNI in 2021 — aid fills the savings gap but risks dependency, distortion and capture); FDI (brings capital, technology and jobs but profits repatriate and enclaves can form); microfinance (small loans to the unbanked — Grameen model; helps marginal enterprise but effects are modest at scale); debt relief (HIPC — frees fiscal space but moral hazard if discipline is absent).

Evaluation (the A* frame): ISI frequently failed — protected industries never became efficient (no competitive pressure), small domestic markets limited scale, and lobbying entrenched rent-seeking; but export-led growth has risks — dependence on world demand (2008 hit hard), suppressed wages and undervalued exchange rates, and the East Asian success also reflected strong state investment in education and infrastructure, not exports alone. Judgement: the evidence favours export-led growth for most developing economies, provided it is supported by human-capital investment, macroeconomic stability and institutions — the East Asian model is a package, not a single lever.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: Country A can produce 1 unit of cloth with 10 labour hours or 1 unit of wine with 5 labour hours. Country B can produce 1 unit of cloth with 6 labour hours or 1 unit of wine with 4 labour hours. Identify each country's comparative advantage and explain how both can gain from trade. (AQA 7136 Paper 3 style, 15 marks)
Mark-scheme logic: Mark logic: [AO1] define comparative vs absolute advantage; [AO2] compute opportunity costs accurately; [AO3] explain specialisation, terms of trade and mutual gains. [AO1] Absolute advantage: B produces both goods with fewer hours (6 < 10 for cloth; 4 < 5 for wine) — but absolute advantage does not determine trade patterns. Comparative advantage rests on opportunity cost. [AO2] Opportunity costs: Country A — 1 cloth costs 2 wine (10/5); 1 wine costs 0.5 cloth. Country B — 1 cloth costs 0.67 wine (6/4); 1 wine costs 1.5 cloth. [AO3] B has the comparative advantage in cloth (0.67 < 2 wine per cloth); A has it in wine (0.5 < 1.5 cloth per wine). A should specialise in wine and export it; B specialises in cloth and exports it. Gains: at any terms of trade between 0.5 and 1.5 wine per cloth (strictly between the two opportunity-cost ratios), both countries can consume combinations beyond their own PPFs — e.g. at 1 wine = 1 cloth, both gain relative to self-sufficiency: A gives up 0.5 cloth per wine domestically but receives 1 cloth per wine by trade; B gives up 1.5 cloth per wine domestically but receives 1. Total world output rises because resources move to their lowest-opportunity-cost uses. Evaluation: the theory assumes constant costs (real PPFs are concave, so full specialisation is never optimal), ignores transport costs and exchange rates, and assumes displaced workers redeploy instantly — specialisation creates structural adjustment costs; and the terms of trade determine how the gains are split, which is why primary exporters (Prebisch–Singer) may gain little.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Explain the Marshall–Lerner condition and the J-curve effect, and assess their importance for a country considering a devaluation.
Answer: Marshall–Lerner: a devaluation improves the current account only if PEDx + PEDm > 1 — exports cheaper in foreign currency and imports dearer in domestic currency raise export revenue and cut import spending only if demand is elastic. J-curve: the current account initially deteriorates (contracts fixed, quantities respond slowly — inelastic short-run demand; the country pays higher import prices with little volume adjustment), then recovers and improves as elasticities rise over time — tracing a J. Evaluation: devaluation risks worsening the BoP first; countries dependent on imported inputs (fuel, materials) face rising import bills regardless; trading partners may retaliate with competitive devaluations, negating the gain; and devaluation is inflationary (import prices rise), eroding the competitiveness gain — sterling after the 2016 referendum fell ~10–15% but the current account barely improved while inflation rose. Judgement: for a country with persistent deficits and elastic export demand, devaluation is powerful without domestic austerity — but only if Marshall–Lerner holds and partners do not retaliate.
Using a diagram, analyse the effects of imposing a tariff on imported goods, and evaluate the likely impact on consumers, producers and government revenue.
Answer: World price Pw (horizontal — price taker); domestic demand Qd, supply Qs; imports = Qd − Qs. A tariff raises the domestic price to Pw + tariff: Qd falls to Qd1, Qs rises to Qs1, imports shrink. Consumer surplus falls (areas A+B+C+D); producers gain A (but via inefficient high-cost production replacing cheaper imports); government gains revenue C (tariff × import volume); B (production distortion) and D (consumption distortion) are deadweight losses. Evaluation: DWL size depends on elasticities (more elastic = larger loss); tariffs are regressive (essentials hit low-income households); retaliation shrinks export markets (Smoot–Hawley 1930 deepened the Depression); the large-country case shifts some burden to foreign producers via a lower world price; but tariffs can be justified on infant-industry or anti-dumping grounds (the US 2025 steel/aluminium tariffs at 25%; EU duties up to ~45% on Chinese EVs, October 2024) and generate revenue for weak tax systems. Judgement: tariffs transfer surplus from consumers to producers and government at a net welfare cost — justification depends on the credibility of the strategic/infant-industry case and on avoiding retaliation.
Assess the view that export-led growth is a more effective development strategy than import-substitution industrialisation.
Answer: Export-led: promote industries competing in world markets (South Korea, Taiwan, China); ISI: protect infant industries behind tariffs/quotas to replace imports (Latin America, 1950s–70s). For export-led: world competition forces efficiency and innovation; economies of scale from a global market; export earnings supply foreign currency for capital goods and debt service; attracts FDI and technology transfer — the tigers grew 8–10% a year for decades while ISI economies stagnated. For ISI: infant industries genuinely need temporary protection to reach competitive scale; reduces dependence on volatile primary exports; preserves foreign exchange and employment. Evaluation: ISI frequently failed — no competitive pressure meant industries never became efficient, small domestic markets limited scale, and lobbying entrenched rent-seeking; but export-led has risks — dependence on world demand (2008 hit hard), suppressed wages and undervalued currencies, and East Asian success also reflected state investment in education and infrastructure, not exports alone. Judgement: evidence favours export-led growth for most developing economies, provided it is a package with human-capital investment, macroeconomic stability and institutions.
Using the data and your knowledge, analyse the effects of sterling's depreciation on the UK economy, and evaluate the effects on the balance of payments.
Answer: Analysis: a depreciation makes exports cheaper and imports dearer. AD shifts right (X rises, M falls — if Marshall–Lerner holds, PEDx + PEDm > 1); but import prices rise — input costs shift SRAS left, so growth comes with a price rise (imported inflation ~1–2 points after the 2016 referendum fall). Exports more competitive supports growth and employment in export sectors. Evaluation on the BoP: the current account improves only after the J-curve trough — short-run contracts and inelastic demand mean it initially worsens; the UK's 2016 experience shows the current account did not improve as hoped (services and investment-income effects dominated), and goods deficits persisted; structural uncompetitiveness (productivity, non-price factors) limits the volume response regardless of price; and if financed by hot money, the currency move can reverse (sudden stop risk). Judgement: a depreciation is expansionary and inflationary, and improves the current account only in the long run if Marshall–Lerner holds — for an import-intensive, services-driven economy like the UK, the volume response is weak and the inflation cost is immediate.
Explain the difference between trade creation and trade diversion, and evaluate whether the UK's post-Brexit trade arrangements have created or diverted trade.
Answer: Trade creation: joining a bloc replaces high-cost domestic production with lower-cost member production — efficiency gain. Trade diversion: the common external tariff shifts imports from efficient non-members to less efficient members — efficiency loss; net welfare depends on which dominates (Viner). Brexit evaluation: leaving the EU single market and customs union introduced customs checks, rules-of-origin requirements and services barriers — frictions with the UK's largest trading partner (the EU takes ~42% of UK exports); the OBR judges the arrangements will cut long-run productivity by roughly 15% relative to EU membership — a diversion-plus-frictions effect; the UK's CPTPP accession (effective December 2024) diversifies towards fast-growing markets but covers only ~15% of world GDP, far less than the EU. Counterpoints: regulatory autonomy allows tailored rules (financial services, AI), and some new trade deals (Australia, New Zealand) create modest trade. Judgement: the net effect so far is diversion — frictions with the dominant partner outweigh gains from new deals; the productivity cost depends on how far UK firms reorient or absorb the frictions.
Evaluate the HDI and the Gini coefficient as measures of development and inequality.
Answer: HDI: combines life expectancy, education (mean and expected years of schooling) and GNI per capita (PPP) — broader than GDP per capita (captures health and schooling, not just output); comparable across countries (UK ≈ 0.94, very high). Limitations: still omits distribution (an average), environmental quality, political freedoms and gender gaps; GNI (PPP) adjustments are imprecise; the three components are equally weighted by assumption. Gini: 0 = perfect equality, 1 = perfect inequality (UK disposable-income Gini ≈ 0.35); captures the whole distribution via the Lorenz curve. Limitations: sensitive to the income measure (market vs disposable income; pre- vs post-housing costs — the UK's Gini rises to ~0.39 after housing costs); says nothing about absolute poverty or where in the distribution inequality lies (top vs bottom). Judgement: the HDI is the better single development measure (multi-dimensional, comparable) but should be supplemented by distribution-corrected measures (IHDI) and poverty lines; the Gini is the standard inequality summary but must specify the income concept — both are complements to GDP per capita, not replacements.
