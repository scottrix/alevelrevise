EC2: Macroeconomic Policy, Inflation and Globalisation

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level All Boards (AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC, CCEA) AQA

Aggregate Demand and Supply (AD/AS), fiscal, monetary, and supply-side policies, Phillips curve, international trade.

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Aggregate Demand (AD): Total spending on domestic output: AD = C + I + G + (X - M).
Monetary Policy: Central bank actions managing interest rates (base rate), quantitative easing, and money supply to meet inflation targets.
Fiscal Policy: Use of government spending (G) and taxation (T) to influence AD; expansionary (G↑/T↓) to boost growth, contractionary (G↓/T↑) to reduce inflation. Distinguish discretionary policy from automatic stabilisers (e.g. progressive tax and welfare payments that dampen the cycle without new decisions).
Inflation: Sustained rise in the general price level. Demand-pull (excess AD, AD shifts right into vertical/LRAS region) vs cost-push (rising costs shift SRAS left, e.g. oil prices, wage claims). Measured by CPI; UK target 2% CPI ±1%. Costs: menu costs, shoe leather costs, redistributed income, reduced international competitiveness, fiscal drag.
Phillips Curve: Short-run Phillips curve (SPRC) shows an inverse trade-off between inflation and unemployment. Long-run Phillips curve (LPRC) is vertical at the natural rate of unemployment (NRU): monetarists argue any reduction in unemployment below NRU is only temporary and leads to accelerating inflation (expectations-augmented Phillips curve).
Supply-Side Policy: Policies shifting LRAS right: education/training (human capital), infrastructure investment, deregulation, tax incentives (lower corporation tax, cutting benefits to improve work incentives), labour market flexibility. Long time lags but improve both growth and the trade-off between inflation and unemployment.
Globalisation: Increasing interdependence of economies through trade in goods/services, capital flows (FDI, hot money), migration and technology diffusion. Drivers: trade liberalisation (WTO), cheaper transport/communications, emergence of MNCs and emerging economies (BRICs). Benefits: growth, lower prices, choice; Costs: structural unemployment, inequality, environmental damage, contagion in financial crises.

🔍 Key Principles & Specification Requirements

  • Macro policy objectives: 2.0% CPI inflation, sustainable GDP growth, low unemployment, balanced current account.
  • Short-run Phillips curve shows inverse trade-off between inflation and unemployment; vertical Long-Run Phillips curve at NRU.
  • Comparative advantage: countries gain from trade by specializing in goods with lowest opportunity cost.

💡 Worked Example Question

Exam-Style Question

Question:

Explain the transmission mechanism of a central bank interest rate hike on aggregate demand.

Model Solution & Mark Scheme:

1. Higher base rate increases commercial borrowing costs and mortgage rates.
2. Consumption (C) falls as saving returns rise and discretionary income drops.
3. Investment (I) falls as hurdle rates for capital projects increase.
4. Currency appreciates (hot money inflows), making exports dearer and imports cheaper (X - M falls).
5. Net result: AD shifts left, dampening economic growth and reducing demand-pull inflationary pressure.

Q3: Using an aggregate demand and aggregate supply diagram, analyse how a rise in global oil prices affects inflation and output, and evaluate how effective monetary policy would be in responding.

Show Model Answer

Answer: Oil is a key input for firms (transport, energy, petrochemicals). A sharp rise in oil prices raises firms' costs of production, shifting the short-run aggregate supply curve (SRAS) to the left. On an AD/AS diagram, with AD unchanged, the new equilibrium is at a higher price level (cost-push inflation) and a lower real output (negative growth), with rising unemployment — a situation of stagflation. The extent of the shift depends on the oil intensity of production and the elasticity of demand for oil. Evaluation: monetary policy faces a conflict here — raising interest rates would reduce AD and contain second-round inflationary pressure (e.g. wage demands chasing higher prices), but it would also deepen the fall in output and unemployment; cutting rates to support growth would risk embedding inflation above the 2% target. The Bank of England's response therefore depends on whether the shock is judged temporary (look through it, keep inflation expectations anchored) or persistent (act). Effectiveness also depends on the credibility of the central bank: if inflation expectations are well anchored, the SRAS shift is less likely to trigger a wage-price spiral; and since monetary policy operates with long and variable lags (12–18 months), it cannot offset the immediate impact of the shock. Supply-side measures (e.g. investment in renewable energy to reduce oil dependence) would be more effective in the long run but take years to have any effect.

Q4: Evaluate the effectiveness of supply-side policies as a means of achieving long-run economic growth.

Show Model Answer

Answer: Supply-side policies aim to increase productive potential by shifting LRAS to the right. Market-based policies include cutting corporation tax (raising expected returns on investment), reducing trade union power, deregulation and welfare reform to improve work incentives; interventionist policies include government spending on education and training, infrastructure (roads, broadband, energy) and support for R&D. In an AD/AS diagram, LRAS (and LRMC) shifts right, raising potential output and lowering the price level, allowing growth without inflation. Effectiveness: interventionist policies have strong evidence — education and infrastructure raise labour productivity, and East Asian growth was built on state-led investment; market-based policies are cheaper for the taxpayer but the benefits are uncertain (e.g. tax cuts may be saved rather than invested, especially in a recession when confidence is low). Evaluation: supply-side policies suffer from long and variable time lags — a generation of schooling is needed before human capital raises productivity — so they cannot solve short-run demand-deficient unemployment; many policies are costly (infrastructure) and create opportunity costs or higher government borrowing; cutting benefits may increase inequality; deregulation can cause negative externalities (e.g. weaker environmental or labour protections). Their effectiveness also depends on complementary demand-side policy: LRAS shifting right only raises actual output if AD is sufficient to absorb the extra capacity. Overall, supply-side policies are the only sustainable route to higher long-run growth, but work best as a long-term programme combined with stable macroeconomic management rather than a quick fix.
