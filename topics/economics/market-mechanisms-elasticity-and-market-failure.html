EC1: Market Mechanisms, Elasticity and Market Failure

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level All Boards (AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC, CCEA) AQA

Price mechanism, PED, YED, XED, market failure, externalities, public goods, government intervention and failure.

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Price Elasticity of Demand (PED): %Δ in Quantity Demanded / %Δ in Price. Measures consumer responsiveness to price changes.
Negative Externality: A cost imposed on a third party external to the market transaction (Social Cost > Private Cost).
Income Elasticity of Demand (YED): %Δ in Quantity Demanded / %Δ in Income. Normal goods: YED > 0 (necessities 0 < YED < 1, luxuries YED > 1). Inferior goods: YED < 0 (e.g. value-brand groceries rise in demand in recession).
Cross Elasticity of Demand (XED): %Δ in Quantity Demanded of Good A / %Δ in Price of Good B. Substitutes: XED > 0 (Coke and Pepsi); Complements: XED < 0 (printers and ink). Higher XED = closer substitute = stronger competitive threat.
Price Elasticity of Supply (PES): %Δ in Quantity Supplied / %Δ in Price. Determinants: spare capacity, stock levels, time period (supply more elastic in long run), mobility of factors of production. Inelastic supply (e.g. housing, agriculture) means price shocks cause large price swings.
Price Mechanism: Adam Smith's "invisible hand" performs three functions: signalling (prices convey information about scarcity), incentive (higher prices encourage production/discourage consumption) and rationing (scarce goods go to those willing and able to pay). Maximum price (price ceiling, set below equilibrium) causes shortage -> rationing by queues or black markets; minimum price (price floor, set above equilibrium, e.g. National Minimum Wage) causes surplus/unemployment.

🔍 Key Principles & Specification Requirements

  • PED: Elastic (|PED| > 1), Inelastic (|PED| < 1), Unitary (|PED| = 1).
  • Public goods characteristics: Non-excludable (free-rider problem) and non-rivalrous in consumption.
  • Government intervention tools: Pigouvian taxes, subsidies, maximum/minimum price controls, tradable pollution permits.

💡 Worked Example Question

Exam-Style Question

Question:

Using a diagrammatic explanation, show why negative production externalities lead to overproduction in a free market.

Model Solution & Mark Scheme:

In a free market, output is determined where Marginal Private Benefit (MPB) = Marginal Private Cost (MPC) at Q_market.
External cost (MEC) means Marginal Social Cost (MSC = MPC + MEC) lies above MPC.
The social optimum occurs where MSB = MSC at Q_optimum.
Because Q_market > Q_optimum, the market overproduces, generating a deadweight welfare loss triangle.

Q3: A coffee shop raises the price of a latte by 10% and finds that the quantity demanded falls by 4%. Calculate the price elasticity of demand and assess the impact on the firm's revenue, and on the wider market if all coffee shops did the same.

Show Model Answer

Answer: PED = %Δ in quantity demanded / %Δ in price = (−4%) / (+10%) = −0.4. Since |−0.4| < 1, demand is price inelastic: the percentage fall in quantity is smaller than the percentage rise in price. Revenue impact: Total Revenue = P × Q. Price rises 10%, quantity falls 4%, so TR changes by (1.10 × 0.96) − 1 = +5.6%. When demand is inelastic, a price rise increases total revenue — this is why the firm profits from the rise. Wider market: if all coffee shops raise prices simultaneously, substitutes (buying coffee from supermarkets, home-brewed coffee) become relatively cheaper, so market demand is more elastic than a single firm's demand — some customers leave the market entirely. Evaluation: PED is a point estimate that may vary over time (consumers respond more in the long run as habits change and alternatives are found); it assumes ceteris paribus — if incomes were also rising, some of the observed fall in quantity would be masked; and elasticity varies along a straight-line demand curve, so a further 10% rise might yield a different response. The firm should use the inelastic finding to raise prices further, but must beware of competitor reactions and long-run substitution out of the market.

Q4: Using a diagram, analyse the effects of a maximum price (price ceiling) set below the equilibrium in the rental housing market, and evaluate whether rent controls are an effective way of improving access to housing.

Show Model Answer

Answer: A maximum price is a legally imposed ceiling set below the equilibrium price (a maximum set above equilibrium would have no effect since the market clears below it anyway). On the diagram, the price line Pmax lies below equilibrium (Pe). At Pmax, quantity demanded (Qd) exceeds quantity supplied (Qs): a shortage (excess demand) of Qd − Qs arises. Landlords supply fewer properties (some convert to other uses or leave the market as returns fall), while more tenants want to rent at the artificially low price. The price mechanism's rationing function is disabled: allocation is by queues, waiting lists, landlord discrimination or black-market side payments (key money), and a deadweight welfare loss arises from the under-supply. Evaluation: the size of the shortage depends on the elasticities of supply and demand — housing supply is very price inelastic in the short run, so the immediate fall in supply is small, but in the long run landlords exit and investment in new rental property falls, worsening the shortage over time; evidence from cities with strict rent control (e.g. San Francisco, Berlin) shows reduced rental supply and deterioration in property quality; shortages hit the poorest hardest as landlords select tenants. However, rent controls do transfer surplus from landlords to sitting tenants, providing security and affordability for those who hold a tenancy. Overall, most economists judge rent controls ineffective: subsidies to housebuilding or housing vouchers increase supply and improve access without distorting the price mechanism, whereas rent caps suppress the very supply response needed to solve the shortage.
