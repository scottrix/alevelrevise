Market Failure and Government Intervention

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA, Edexcel, OCR

A* revision for externalities, merit and demerit goods, public goods, information gaps, Pigouvian taxes, tradable permits and government failure for AQA 7136, Edexcel A 9EC0 and OCR H460.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Draw and interpret externality diagrams, identifying Q1, Q*, the welfare loss and which curve shifts
  • Distinguish merit from demerit goods and evaluate interventions for each
  • Explain the free-rider problem for public and quasi-public goods and evaluate state provision
  • Analyse adverse selection and moral hazard, and assess signalling, screening and regulatory remedies
  • Evaluate Pigouvian taxes, subsidies, tradable permits, regulation and price controls using elasticity, information and cost criteria
  • Identify the five causes of government failure and apply them to real interventions
  • Construct balanced judgements weighing the size of market failure against the risk of government failure

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Market failure: When the price mechanism allocates resources inefficiently — MSB does not equal MSC at the market output.
Externality: A cost or benefit imposed on a third party outside a market transaction, causing a divergence between private and social costs or benefits.
Merit good: A good that is under-consumed in the free market because of positive externalities and information gaps (e.g. education, vaccination).
Demerit good: A good that is over-consumed because of negative externalities and imperfect information about long-run costs (e.g. cigarettes).
Public good: A good that is non-excludable and non-rival in consumption (e.g. national defence), so markets underprovide it.
Free-rider problem: When non-excludable users cannot be charged, individuals let others pay, so voluntary provision collapses.
Adverse selection: Pre-contract asymmetric information: higher-risk parties are more likely to enter transactions, so average pricing drives out low-risk customers.
Moral hazard: Post-contract hidden action: once insured or bailed out, agents take more risks because part of the cost falls on others.
Pigouvian tax: An indirect tax set equal to the marginal external cost, internalising a negative externality.
Government failure: When intervention worsens resource allocation compared with the free market, via information gaps, administrative costs, regulatory capture, unintended consequences or rent-seeking.

Externalities, merit and demerit goods

Market failure occurs when the price mechanism allocates resources inefficiently — marginal social benefit does not equal marginal social cost at the market output. Externalities are costs or benefits imposed on third parties outside the transaction, causing a divergence between private and social costs/benefits. A negative production externality (pollution) means MSC > MPC; a positive consumption externality (education, vaccination) means MSB > MPB. Formally: MSC = MPC + MEC and MSB = MPB + MEB.

Diagram description (negative production externality): axes price/quantity; MPB = demand (downward-sloping); MPC = supply (upward-sloping); MSC drawn above MPC, vertically separated by the constant marginal external cost. Free market: MPC = MPB at Q1 (overproduction). Social optimum: MSC = MSB at Q* < Q1. Between Q* and Q1 each unit costs society more than it is worth — the deadweight welfare loss is the triangle between MSC and MSB over that gap. For a positive externality the logic mirrors: MSB above MPB, free market underproduces at Q1 < Q*, DWL triangle over Q1–Q*.

Merit goods (education, healthcare, vaccination) are goods whose consumption generates positive externalities and/or whose benefits are underestimated by consumers due to information gaps — under-consumed in the free market. Demerit goods (cigarettes, gambling) are over-consumed: negative externalities plus imperfect information (consumers underestimate long-run costs). Worked example (real exam-style calculation): a factory pollutes a river with MPC = 20 + Q, MEC = 5, MPB = 100 − Q. Market: MPC = MPB → 20 + Q = 100 − Q → Q = 40. Social optimum: MSC = MSB → 20 + Q + 5 = 100 − Q → Q = 37.5. The Pigouvian tax equals MEC = £5 per unit; at Q = 37.5 price = 100 − 37.5 = 62.5 and tax revenue = 5 × 37.5 = £187.5. Exam technique: always name the externality, identify which curve shifts, mark both Q1 and Q*, and shade the DWL — the diagram carries half the available marks in AQA Paper 1 Section B.

Public goods and the free-rider problem

A public good has two defining characteristics: non-excludable — it is impossible (or prohibitively costly) to prevent people from consuming it once provided; and non-rival — one person's consumption does not reduce the amount available to others. Examples: national defence, street lighting, flood defences, clean air, the lighthouse (the classic textbook case). Quasi-public goods are partially excludable or partially rival: roads (congestion makes them rival; tolls make them excludable), museums, broadcast TV (encryption), beaches.

The free-rider problem: because non-excludable users cannot be charged, each individual has an incentive to let others pay — everyone reasons this way, so voluntary markets underprovide or fail to provide the good entirely. Chain of reasoning: non-excludable → free riders cannot be excluded → no revenue for private providers → profit-maximising firms will not supply → the good is underprovided relative to the social optimum → total surplus is lost (a DWL equal to the forgone benefits). This is a complete market failure: no market price exists to ration provision.

Solution: state provision financed by taxation (defence, street lighting) — the government estimates the socially optimal quantity and taxes beneficiaries. Evaluation: the government faces an information gap in valuing public goods — cost–benefit analysis of flood defences or new roads is uncertain, and political incentives (visible projects before elections) distort choice; taxation itself creates deadweight losses; and quasi-public goods can sometimes be charged for (road pricing, subscription TV), which partially restores a market. The complete vs partial distinction (pure public good vs quasi-public good) is the discriminator between high marks: clean air is pure; motorways are quasi and can be priced.

Information gaps: adverse selection and moral hazard

Asymmetric information — one party to a transaction knows more than the other — causes two classic failures. Adverse selection (pre-contract, hidden information): high-risk individuals are more likely to buy insurance because they know they will claim, while low-risk people find the average premium too high and stay out; the insurance pool becomes dominated by high-risk clients, premiums rise, more low-risk customers leave — a “death spiral” that can collapse the market (Akerlof's “market for lemons” in used cars). Moral hazard (post-contract, hidden action): once insured, people take more risks or take less care, because part of the cost falls on the insurer — e.g. careless driving after buying comprehensive cover, or banks taking excessive risks expecting bailouts.

Market solutions: signalling — the informed party reveals quality (warranties, qualifications, no-claims bonuses, brand reputation); screening — the uninformed party gathers information (application forms, medical exams, driving records); excesses/deductibles — the claimant pays the first part of any loss, deterring high-risk customers and reducing moral hazard; experience rating — premiums adjusted over time to claims history. Government solutions: mandatory disclosure (nutrition labels, financial services regulation), compulsory insurance (motor), and regulation of professional standards (FCA conduct rules).

Evaluation: screening is costly and never perfect — information gaps persist; risk-based pricing can make insurance unaffordable for genuinely high-risk groups (genetic testing raises equity and ethical issues, hence the UK moratorium on genetic test results in life insurance); and excesses create their own problem — moral hazard falls but small claims go unclaimed, weakening the insurance function. A well-structured answer defines both forms of information failure, gives one market and one government remedy for each, then evaluates whether the remedy restores efficiency or merely shifts the failure elsewhere.

Government intervention: taxes, subsidies, permits, regulation and price controls

Indirect (Pigouvian) taxes equal to the marginal external cost shift MPC up to MSC, internalising the externality: price rises to P*, output falls to Q*, and the DWL is eliminated. Diagram description: supply shifts left (up) by the tax per unit; the new intersection with demand marks Q*; the vertical gap between old and new supply equals the tax. Real-world: the Soft Drinks Industry Levy (2018; 18p/24p per litre, raised in 2025) cut sugar content as firms reformulated; the UK carbon price floor (£18/tonne since 2015) plus EU ETS linkage prices carbon in power generation. Evaluation: the output effect depends on PED — cigarette demand is addictive and inelastic, so a large tax cuts output little while raising revenue mostly from consumers (regressive, hitting low-income smokers hardest); government faces an information gap in measuring MEC; and illicit trade is an unintended consequence (illegal tobacco is estimated at a double-digit share of the UK market).

Subsidies for positive externalities (education, vaccination, renewable energy) shift supply right, raising output towards Q*; evaluation: cost to the taxpayer, opportunity cost, and deadweight loss if the subsidy over-corrects. Tradable pollution permits (cap-and-trade): a cap on total emissions; firms with low abatement costs sell permits to firms with high abatement costs, so reduction is achieved at minimum total cost; the cap guarantees the environmental outcome, unlike a tax which guarantees price but not quantity. Real-world: the EU Emissions Trading Scheme (permit prices peaked above €80 in 2023); phase 1 saw prices collapse to near zero because the cap was set too generously — the information-gap failure. Regulation (bans, standards, quotas): simple and certain, but blunt — no incentive to abate beyond the minimum standard, high enforcement costs.

Price controls: a maximum price (ceiling) set below equilibrium causes shortage → rationing by queues, waiting lists or black markets (e.g. rent controls — evidence from San Francisco and Berlin shows reduced rental supply and deteriorating quality); a minimum price (floor) set above equilibrium causes surplus — e.g. the failed alcohol minimum unit pricing proposal in the UK was resisted before Scotland introduced it in 2018, and EU agricultural price floors created butter mountains. Nudges (behavioural economics): choice architecture — default pension enrolment, salience, framing — changes behaviour without banning options or changing prices; cheap but effects are modest and can be gamed. State provision and information provision (labels, public campaigns) complete the toolkit: name the policy, show the diagram, then evaluate with elasticity, information gaps, administrative cost and unintended consequences.

Government failure

Government failure occurs when intervention worsens the allocation of resources compared with the free market — net welfare falls. The five recognised causes (AQA 4.1.6.3, Edexcel 1.4.2): information gaps — governments cannot measure MEC, MEB or optimal output accurately, so taxes/subsidies/caps are set too high or too low (EU ETS phase 1 permits collapsing to near zero); administrative and compliance costs — enforcement exceeds the welfare gain (costly regulation of small firms); regulatory capture — the regulator comes to serve the industry it regulates (revolving-door appointments, industry lobbying shaping rules); unintended consequences — black markets, rent controls reducing housing supply, buffer-stock agencies running out of funds; and rent-seeking — resources diverted to lobbying for protection or subsidies rather than productive activity (grandfathered permits giving windfall gains to incumbents).

Real-world examples for evaluation: rent controls in Berlin and San Francisco reduced rental supply and worsened shortages — intervention disabled the price mechanism's rationing and incentive functions; the EU Common Agricultural Policy's price floors created overproduction (“butter mountains”, “wine lakes”) at huge budgetary cost; biofuel mandates (US corn ethanol) raised food prices while delivering questionable emissions gains; and the UK 2022 energy price guarantee capped bills but muted the incentive to cut consumption, adding to the fiscal cost of the shock.

The evaluation frame examiners reward: (1) identify the market failure and its size — is intervention justified at all?; (2) assess the chosen instrument against alternatives (a tax vs regulation vs permits vs nudge) using elasticity, information, administrative cost and political acceptability; (3) consider government failure — can the government actually implement it well?; (4) conclude with a judgement that weighs the size of the market failure against the risk of government failure. The classic A* line: intervention should correct the failure without creating larger ones — the comparison is not “market vs perfect government” but “imperfect market vs imperfect government”.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: A factory pollutes a river. Marginal private cost MPC = 20 + Q, marginal external cost MEC = 5, marginal private benefit MPB = 100 − Q. Find the market output, the social optimum, the corrective tax, and explain why the market fails. (AQA 7136 Paper 1 style, 15 marks)
Mark-scheme logic: Mark logic: [AO1] define the externality and conditions; [AO2] calculate; [AO3/AO4] explain the failure and the correction. Market equilibrium: MPC = MPB → 20 + Q = 100 − Q → 2Q = 80 → Q = 40, price P = 100 − 40 = 60. Social optimum: MSC = MSB → (20 + Q + 5) = 100 − Q → 2Q = 75 → Q = 37.5. Why the market fails: the factory's private costs exclude the £5-per-unit cost imposed on river users (fisheries, water abstracters, recreation), so MSC > MPC; output Q = 40 exceeds the optimum Q = 37.5, and over that gap MSC exceeds MSB, so each extra unit destroys surplus — a deadweight welfare loss (roughly ½ × 5 × 2.5 = £6.25 per period on these figures). Correction: a Pigouvian tax of £5 per unit (equal to MEC) shifts MPC up to MSC, raising the price to £62.50 and cutting output to 37.5 — the externality is internalised; tax revenue = 5 × 37.5 = £187.50 per period, which could fund river clean-up. Evaluation: the tax works only if the government measures MEC accurately (information gap); if polluters can relocate or the demand for the firm's output is inelastic the output cut will be smaller; monitoring emissions has administrative costs; and the alternative of regulation (an emission standard at 37.5) is certain but gives no incentive to abate further.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Using a diagram, analyse the market failure arising from the consumption of goods with negative externalities, and evaluate the use of indirect taxes to correct it.
Answer: Negative consumption externality (cigarettes, sugary drinks, petrol): MSB < MPB (MSB = MPB − MEC). Free market: MPB = MPC at Q1, P1; optimum: MSB = MSC at lower Q*. Between Q* and Q1, MSC > MSB — deadweight welfare loss (shaded triangle): society values those units at less than they cost. A Pigouvian tax equal to MEC shifts MPC up to MSC, raising price to P* and cutting output to Q*. Evaluation: output effect depends on PED — cigarette demand is addictive/inelastic, so a large tax cuts output little while raising revenue mostly from consumers (regressive against low-income smokers); government faces an information gap measuring MEC so the tax may be mis-set; unintended consequences such as illicit tobacco trade; but revenue can fund healthcare or subsidies to alternatives. Judgement: the Pigouvian tax is the most theoretically efficient correction, but effectiveness depends on elasticity, measurement and enforcement.
Explain the problem of adverse selection in insurance markets and assess the measures insurers use to reduce it.
Answer: Adverse selection: pre-contract asymmetric information — buyers know their own risk better than insurers. High-risk individuals (smokers, reckless drivers) are more likely to buy; low-risk people find the average premium too high and exit. The pool becomes dominated by high-risk clients, premiums rise, more low-risk customers leave — a death spiral that can collapse the market (market failure). Measures: screening (application forms, medicals, driving records); no-claims bonuses; excesses/deductibles (claimant pays first part of loss — deters frequent claimers); experience rating (premiums track claims history). Evaluation: screening is costly and imperfect — information gaps remain; risk pricing may make cover unaffordable for genuinely high-risk groups (genetic testing raises ethical/equity issues, hence the UK moratorium on genetic results in life insurance); excesses reduce moral hazard but small claims go unclaimed. Judgement: screening and excesses substantially mitigate adverse selection, but regulation (community rating, compulsory pools) is needed where affordability conflicts with efficient pricing.
Evaluate the use of tradable pollution permits as a means of reducing carbon emissions, compared with a carbon tax.
Answer: Cap-and-trade (e.g. EU ETS): cap total emissions, issue tradeable permits; low-abatement-cost firms sell to high-abatement-cost firms — reduction at minimum total cost; the cap guarantees the environmental quantity, unlike a tax which guarantees price not quantity. Benefits: cost-effective, incentivises clean-tech investment (permits sold for profit), auction revenue. Drawbacks: information gap in setting the cap — too generous and permits are worthless (EU ETS phase 1 price collapsed to ~zero); too tight and firms face excessive costs; high monitoring costs; grandfathering gives windfall gains to incumbents (barrier to entry); carbon leakage if firms relocate. Tax comparison: simpler, price certainty for investment planning, but quantity uncertain — with inelastic carbon demand a high tax buys little reduction, and it does not auto-adjust as the economy grows. Judgement: both are efficient corrections superior to regulation; the choice depends on which information problem binds — quantity uncertainty favours the tax, price uncertainty favours permits — plus political acceptability and administrative capacity.
Explain why clean air is a public good and why markets underprovide it, and evaluate one policy to protect it.
Answer: Clean air is non-excludable (cannot prevent breathing it) and non-rival (one person's use does not diminish another's) — a pure public good. Chain: non-excludable → free riders cannot be charged → no private revenue → firms will not supply → underprovision; moreover, clean air is degraded by negative externalities (pollution), so markets both underprotect and over-pollute it. Policy: a Pigouvian pollution tax or tradable permits (e.g. UK carbon price floor £18/tonne since 2015; EU ETS prices above €80 in 2023) price the externality; or direct regulation (Clean Air Acts, ULEZ charging zones). Evaluation: measurement of the external cost is uncertain; ULEZ shows distributional effects (regressive on poorer drivers) and political backlash; regulation is certain but blunt. Judgement: markets cannot price clean air at all, so some intervention is inevitable; the choice of instrument turns on measurement quality and enforceability.
Explain why natural monopolies are regulated, and evaluate price-cap regulation (RPI−X style).
Answer: A natural monopoly exists when economies of scale mean one firm can serve the whole market at lower AC than two or more (e.g. water networks, rail track). Duplication is wasteful, but an unregulated monopolist sets P > MC, creating DWL and redistributing surplus from consumers. Regulators (Ofwat, Ofgem, CAA) set price caps linked to inflation minus an efficiency factor (RPI−X; now CPIH−X), designed to pass predicted efficiency gains to consumers while allowing the firm a return on capital. Evaluation: caps simulate competitive pressure (X gains force cost cuts); but the regulator faces an information gap — if X is set too tight, underinvestment follows (UK water underinvestment and leakage controversies); too loose and consumers overpay; and the company can game the regulator (asset revaluation). Rate-of-return or profit caps are weaker incentives. Judgement: regulation improves on unregulated monopoly, but quality depends on regulator information and credibility — competition for the market (franchise auctions) can help where competition in the market cannot.
Using a real intervention, evaluate the concept of government failure.
Answer: Example: rent controls (Berlin, San Francisco). Aim: make housing affordable by capping rents below equilibrium. Failure mechanism: the price mechanism's incentive function is disabled — landlords convert properties to other uses, exit the market and cut maintenance, so long-run supply falls and the shortage worsens; allocation shifts to landlord selection, key money and waiting lists; DWL from under-supply. Evidence: both cities show reduced rental supply and deteriorating quality after strict controls. General causes illustrated: unintended consequences; information gaps (the government cannot set the cap at the right level); administrative/enforcement costs; and rent-seeking (incumbent tenants lobbying to keep controls). Evaluation: intervention is not always failure — controls do transfer surplus to sitting tenants and provide security; the comparison is imperfect market vs imperfect government; better alternatives (housebuilding subsidies, housing vouchers) raise supply without disabling the price mechanism. Judgement: government failure is the net welfare loss after weighing intended gains against unintended costs — and it argues for instruments that work with price signals, not against them.
