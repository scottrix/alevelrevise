Microeconomics and Markets

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level All Boards (AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC, CCEA) AQA

A-Level Economics revision: Microeconomics and Markets. Learning objectives, key points, worked examples and practice questions across AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC and CCEA.

📌 Key Points

Key Fact: Demand: P↓->Qd↑ (law); Determinants: income, tastes, related goods, expectations, #buyers
Key Fact: Supply: P↑->Qs↑; Determinants: input prices, technology, expectations, #sellers, taxes/subsidies
Key Fact: Equilibrium: Qd=Qs; Shortage: P<P* -> Qd>Qs; Surplus: P>P* -> Qs>Qd
Key Fact: PED = %DeltaQd/%DeltaP; elastic (>1), inelastic (<1), unitary (=1); factors: substitutes, necessity, time, %income
Key Fact: YED: normal (>0), inferior (<0); XED: substitutes (>0), complements (<0); PES: time, spare capacity
Key Fact: Surplus: CS = area under D above P; PS = area above S below P; Deadweight loss from intervention
Key Fact: Market structures: PC (many, homogeneous, free entry); Monopoly (one, barriers); MC (many, differentiated); Oligopoly (few, interdependence)
Key Fact: Labour: D = MRP; S = workers; Wage = MRP = MRC in PC; Monopsony: MRC > S; Unions: collective bargaining

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Analyse demand and supply: shifts vs movements, determinants
  • Determine market equilibrium and disequilibrium (excess demand/supply)
  • Calculate and interpret elasticities: PED, YED, XED, PES
  • Understand consumer and producer surplus, allocative efficiency
  • Evaluate market structures: perfect competition, monopoly, monopolistic competition, oligopoly
  • Analyse labour markets: derived demand, MRP, wage determination, trade unions
  • Evaluate government intervention: max/min prices, taxes, subsidies, regulation

💡 Worked Example

Exam-Style Question

Question: A monopolist faces demand P = 100 - 2Q and has MC = 20. Find profit-maximising output, price, and profit

Model Answer:

MR = 100 - 4Q. Set MR = MC: 100 - 4Q = 20 -> Q = 20. P = 100 - 2(20) = 60. Profit = (P - ATC)Q. ATC = TC/Q. If TC = 20Q + 100, ATC = 20 + 5 = 25. Profit = (60-25)x20 = 700

Q1: A firm knows that the price elasticity of demand for its product is −0.5. It raises price by 10%. Calculate the change in quantity demanded and assess the effect on the firm's total revenue and on consumer surplus.

Show Model Answer

Answer: PED = %ΔQd / %ΔP, so %ΔQd = PED × %ΔP = (−0.5) × (+10%) = −5%. Quantity demanded falls by 5%. Since |PED| = 0.5 < 1, demand is price inelastic. Total revenue = P × Q: the 10% price rise outweighs the 5% quantity fall, so total revenue rises (by approximately (1.10 × 0.95) − 1 = +4.5%). This illustrates the general rule: when demand is inelastic, a price rise raises total revenue; when demand is elastic, a price rise lowers it. Consumer surplus falls: consumers who continue buying pay a higher price (loss equal to the rectangle of extra price × remaining quantity), and some consumers stop buying altogether (loss of their surplus on those units). Evaluation: PED is measured at a point and is not constant along a linear demand curve — the same firm raising price again may face a more elastic response as price moves up the curve; if the product has few substitutes (e.g. a necessity or addictive good), inelasticity persists, but if rivals do not follow the price rise, customers switch, making demand more elastic than measured; and the ceteris paribus assumption may fail if incomes or tastes change simultaneously. The firm maximises revenue by raising price while |PED| < 1, stopping when |PED| = 1 (unitary elasticity, revenue-maximising point).

Q2: Using a diagram, compare the price and output decisions of a profit-maximising monopolist with those of a perfectly competitive industry, and evaluate the welfare implications.

Show Model Answer

Answer: In perfect competition, each firm is a price taker facing a horizontal demand curve; profit maximisation occurs where P = MC, and in long-run equilibrium P = MC = minimum AC, so firms earn only normal profit. Industry output is where the industry MC (supply) curve cuts demand. A monopolist faces the whole downward-sloping market demand curve, so marginal revenue (MR) lies below demand (MR has twice the slope of a linear demand curve). Profit maximisation occurs where MR = MC; output Qm is then sold at price Pm read off the demand curve, with Pm > MC. Compared with perfect competition, the monopolist restricts output (Qm < Qpc) and charges a higher price (Pm > Ppc), earning supernormal profit sustained in the long run by barriers to entry. Welfare implications: allocative inefficiency because P > MC — society values extra units above their marginal cost, creating a deadweight welfare loss (the triangle between demand and MC over the output gap Qm to Qpc); consumer surplus falls and part is transferred to the monopolist as profit. Evaluation: the comparison is not entirely one-sided — supernormal profits fund R&D and innovation, and dynamic efficiency may be higher under monopoly (Schumpeter); economies of scale mean a large monopolist's AC (and hence price) can be lower than that of many small competitive firms, so consumers may face lower prices than the perfect competition model predicts; price discrimination can increase output closer to the competitive level; and the model assumes identical cost curves, which is rarely true. Overall, monopoly causes static allocative inefficiency (DWL), but the possibility of economies of scale and dynamic efficiency means real-world monopolies may not always be against the consumer interest — hence competition authorities examine each case on its merits.

Q3: Using the monopsony model, analyse how the imposition of a National Minimum Wage set above the market wage could raise both wages and employment, and evaluate this outcome.

Show Model Answer

Answer: A monopsony is a market with a single buyer of labour (e.g. a large employer in a small town, or the NHS for certain specialists). The monopsonist faces the upward-sloping labour supply curve (S = ACL), so to hire more workers it must raise the wage for all workers, meaning the marginal cost of labour (MCL) lies above the supply curve. Unregulated, the monopsonist hires where MCL = MRP (the derived demand for labour), giving employment Q1 and wage W1 (read off the supply curve). Both employment and wage are below the competitive outcome (Wc, Qc where S = MRP) — the monopsonist exploits its buyer power. If a National Minimum Wage (NMW) is set at Wc (above W1 but below the MCL at Q1), the supply curve becomes horizontal (perfectly elastic) at Wc up to Qc: the monopsonist can now hire more workers at a constant wage without raising wages for existing staff. The profit-maximising condition changes: the horizontal NMW line cuts MRP at a higher employment level Qc. So wages rise from W1 to Wc AND employment rises from Q1 to Qc — the minimum wage corrects the monopsony distortion. Evaluation: this benign outcome holds only if the monopsony model accurately describes the labour market — in competitive labour markets (many employers), a minimum wage set above equilibrium causes classic unemployment (supply > demand); the risk depends on where the NMW is set relative to the market wage: set too high and job losses occur (particularly among young and low-skilled workers whose MRP is low); empirical evidence (e.g. UK Low Pay Commission studies, Card and Krueger) suggests moderate minimum wages have had little negative employment effect, consistent with some monopsony power; and firms may respond by cutting hours, benefits or training instead of jobs. Overall, if labour markets have monopsony characteristics, a moderate NMW can raise both wages and employment, but the level at which it is set is critical and varies by region and sector.
