Socio-cultural Issues in Sport

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC/Eduqas

A-level PE socio-cultural revision: pre-industrial to post-industrial sport, rationalisation and codification, globalisation, commercialisation and the media (golden triangle), ethics and deviance (drugs, match fixing), violence, discrimination and routes to sporting excellence for AQA 7582, OCR H555 and Edexcel 9PE0.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Describe the characteristics of pre-industrial popular recreations and contrast them with rationalised post-industrial sport, including the role of the public schools (AQA 3.6; OCR 3.1; Edexcel Topic 5)
  • Explain rationalisation and codification: governing bodies, leagues, respectability and the spread of sport through universities, the army and the railways (AQA 3.6; OCR 3.1)
  • Evaluate globalisation, commercialisation and the media, including the golden triangle and the effects on performers, sport and spectators (AQA 3.6; OCR 3.1)
  • Explain ethics and deviance: sportsmanship, gamesmanship, contract to win, drug use and the role of WADA, and match fixing (AQA 3.6; OCR 3.1)
  • Explain causes and control of player and crowd violence, and forms of discrimination (sexism, racism, disability) with initiatives (AQA 3.6; OCR 3.1)
  • Outline routes to sporting excellence: talent identification, the performance pyramid, and the roles of UK Sport, TASS and national governing bodies (AQA 3.6; OCR 3.1)

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Popular recreation: Pre-industrial (pre-1800) village sports and pastimes: natural/simple, local, occasional (holy days and festivals), severe (violent), ritualistic and wagering-based, played largely by the rural lower classes.
Rationalisation: The transformation of sport from occasional, violent, local popular recreations into organised, codified, regular, national competition under governing bodies and written rules in post-industrial Britain.
Golden triangle: The interdependent relationship between sport, sponsorship and the media: sport provides content and profile, media pay for broadcasting rights, sponsors buy exposure - each side profits from the other two.
Commercialisation: The treatment of sport as a business commodity: generating income through sponsorship, advertising, broadcasting rights, gate receipts and merchandising, with success increasingly measured by financial return.
Globalisation: The worldwide spread of sport through shared codified rules, international governing bodies and events, global media coverage and performer migration, making sport a global cultural commodity.
Gamesmanship: Winning by whatever means - bending the rules without breaking them (time-wasting, sledging, professional fouls); contrasted with sportsmanship (playing fairly and honourably) and the contract to win (all-out commitment to victory).
WADA: The World Anti-Doping Agency (founded 1999): writes the World Anti-Doping Code, maintains the prohibited list, coordinates testing and sanctions offenders in partnership with national anti-doping organisations like UKAD.
Positive discrimination: Policies or targets that deliberately favour under-represented groups (women, ethnic minorities, disabled people) in participation, coaching and leadership to correct historical imbalance.

📌 Key Points

Popular recreations: natural/simple, local, occasional, severe, ritual, wagering; caused by poor transport, illiteracy, rural life
Post-industrial sport: urbanisation initially damaged sport, then railways, Factory Acts (Saturday half-day) and middle-class leadership enabled rational recreation
Public schools: muscular Christianity (playing sport builds character), codification, the melting pot, spread via universities, church, army and railways (old boys)
Golden triangle: sport - sponsorship - media interdependence; positives (money, profile, participation) and negatives (scheduling, advertising control, win-at-all-costs)
Deviance: sportsmanship vs gamesmanship vs contract to win; drugs explained by pressure/money, controlled by WADA/UKAD testing and the prohibited list
Routes to excellence: grassroots - club - county - regional - national; UK Sport funds elite (lottery), TASS supports dual-career athletes, NGBs run talent identification

Pre-industrial and post-industrial Britain: popular recreations to rational recreation (AQA 3.6; OCR 3.1; Edexcel Topic 5)

Pre-industrial Britain (before ~1800): sport took the form of popular recreations - village games and pastimes whose characteristics are a guaranteed 6-mark list: (1) natural/simple - played with natural materials (animal bladders, sticks) and without equipment; (2) local - confined to individual villages with differing rules; (3) occasional - tied to holy days, festivals and wakes rather than regular fixtures; (4) severe - violent and rough (mob football, animal baiting, cudgelling); (5) ritual - linked to religious and agricultural calendar customs; (6) wagering - heavy betting surrounded contests; plus courtly/popular divide: the upper classes pursued exclusive pursuits (real tennis, hunting, fencing) while peasants played village games. Causes of the characteristics (the exam rewards the explanation): limited transport and communications (games stayed local, rules never unified), illiteracy (rules were unwritten, passed by word of mouth), harsh rural poverty and long agricultural hours (sport only on holy days), and class separation (two-tier sport). Classic examples to cite: mob football (village versus village, hundreds of players, minimal rules, damage to property - repeatedly banned by authorities) and mob/bare-knuckle prize fighting with wagering.

Post-industrial Britain (1800-1900): the industrial revolution first damaged traditional sport - urbanisation moved workers into crowded towns with no space or fields; long factory hours (12-hour days, seven-day weeks) destroyed free time; child labour and low wages removed energy and money; the loss of village life killed local traditions; lack of space and squalor limited play. The urban lower classes initially had neither the time, money, space nor health to participate - the standard explain point. Then, through the century, conditions improved and sport was transformed: the Factory Acts and the Saturday half-day (from the 1840s-50s) created regular free time; rising wages and cheap excursions gave money and access; the railways made fixtures between towns possible; middle-class employers and the church encouraged respectability; urban parks and provisions created space.

The public schools were the engine of rationalisation and are a guaranteed extended-response topic. Stage the development: (1) bullying and brutality (early 1800s - fagging, mob rule, uncontrolled games); (2) control by trust (headmasters like Thomas Arnold at Rugby used older boys and team games to control energy - games became regulated); (3) athleticism - muscular Christianity: the belief that playing team games developed manly Christian character (courage, leadership, fairness) - playing hard and fair; (4) codification: each school wrote rules for its own version (Rugby football, Eton's Field Game), and the universities then became the melting pot where old boys from different schools agreed common rules - leading to national governing bodies such as the FA (1863), separating Association football from Rugby; (5) spread: old boys carried codified games into the universities, church (church teams), the army (regimental sport), industry (works teams) and abroad via the empire, plus the railways moving teams - the standard explain sequence for how modern sport spread nationally and internationally.

Rational recreation replaced popular recreation with sport whose characteristics mirror and invert the old list: regionally/nationally organised; regular league and cup fixtures; codified under governing bodies with written rules; exclusive (amateur ideals excluding professionals); controlled/disciplined (referees, sanctions); respectable. The amateur-professional divide matters for the class dimension: the middle and upper classes played as gentlemen amateurs (sport for character), the working classes became paid professionals (sport as livelihood); professionalism was initially banned from amateur clubs (a discrimination point that later reversed). The exam frequently sets a popular-versus-rational comparison table - prepare characteristics side by side with the industrial causes behind each change.

The rationalisation timeline in detail - the dates and bodies that earn precision marks: the Football Association (1863) codified Association football and separated it from handling games; the Rugby Football Union (1871) codified rugby, and the 1895 northern breakaway (the Northern Union, later rugby league) split the professional working-class north from the amateur south over payments for 'broken time' - the clearest class dimension in codification; the Amateur Athletic Club (1866) defined an amateur as one who had never competed for money nor for a prize open to professionals - explicitly excluding manual workers, the gentleman-amateur ideal in law; the LTA (1888) codified lawn tennis. Supporting infrastructure: railway excursion trains carried teams and spectators to cup ties (the FA Cup, from 1871-2, was the first national competition); the Factory Acts (the 1847 Ten Hours Act and the Saturday half-day from the 1850s) created regular leisure time; public parks and public baths legislation provided urban space and swimming provision; church movements (temperance societies, church lads' brigades) promoted sport as respectable recreation, spreading muscular Christianity; works teams (factory-employer clubs, several later becoming league clubs) gave the working classes organised sport.

Women's exclusion in Victorian sport is the discrimination link-back: Victorian medical myths claimed exercise harmed female health; restrictive dress (corsetry, full skirts) excluded physical activity; women were barred from clubs, competitions and the professions; acceptable activities were limited to croquet, lawn tennis and archery. The exam links this to modern sexism: the historical exclusion explains today's participation, prize-money and leadership gaps - quote the continuity for the synthesis mark. Amateurism versus professionalism completed the class story: amateurs (middle and upper class) valued taking part and character; professionals (working class) played for a livelihood; amateur bodies banned professionals (the Olympic amateur code persisted until 1980s reform) - the reversal of that ban is the bridge to modern commercialisation: once professionals were admitted, sport became a career, and commercial money followed - a synthesis point that links the two sections of this topic.

The 'weekend' and leisure time is the structural change behind rational recreation: the Factory Acts and union pressure created the Saturday half-day (from the 1850s), giving the working classes a regular afternoon of free time - the direct precondition for Saturday league fixtures (the Football League, 1888, built its whole structure on it) and for spectator crowds. Transport detail: railways first moved teams (inter-town fixtures), then spectators (cheap excursion trains to cup ties), then trophies and news (results printed nationally, feeding support and rivalry) - cite all three movements for the transport mark. The church and army as spreaders: church lads' brigades and temperance societies promoted sport as respectable, alcohol-free recreation (muscular Christianity applied to the urban working class); regimental sport spread games through the army (garrisons playing codified sport at home and across the empire); public schools supplied the old boys who ran the new clubs and governing bodies - the full spread network: universities, church, army, industry, railways, empire. Female exclusion detail: lawn tennis and croquet were tolerated as 'suitable' because they required little strength, dress modification or public display; competitive and contact sport was barred - the origin of the participation gap the modern initiatives (This Girl Can, coverage growth) address; quote the continuity for the synthesis mark.

Exam logic: for popular recreation questions, list characteristics with examples (mob football) and explain the causes (transport, illiteracy, class); for public school questions, stage the sequence (brutality, control, athleticism/muscular Christianity, codification, melting pot, spread) and name Arnold and the FA; for comparison questions, match each rational characteristic to the industrial change that produced it.

Globalisation, commercialisation and the media (AQA 3.6; OCR 3.1)

Globalisation is the worldwide spread and interconnection of sport: codified rules adopted globally, international governing bodies (FIFA, IOC, World Athletics) regulating worldwide competition, global events (Olympic Games, football World Cup), global media coverage and the migration of performers, coaches and capital across borders. Effects the exam rewards: shared global sporting culture (the same sports, stars and brands everywhere); worldwide talent markets (players move to the best leagues - the Premier League recruits globally); growth of sport in new regions (women's football, T20 cricket); but also negative evaluations: cultural homogenisation (minority national sports squeezed out), the talent drain from developing nations to wealthy leagues, and the dominance of a few sports, leagues and sponsors. Link globalisation to the history: codification and the empire's spread of sport in the 19th century was the first globalisation wave; satellite television and digital streaming made the second - a synthesis point that separates A* answers.

Commercialisation is sport run as business: income from sponsorship, advertising, broadcasting rights, gate receipts and merchandising. The golden triangle is the guaranteed diagram-and-explain item: sport (content, performers, profile), media (pays for rights, broadcasts to audiences) and sponsorship (buys exposure to those audiences) - a mutually dependent relationship where each increases the value of the other two. Explain the mechanism: media pay billions for rights because advertisers pay for the audience; sponsors pay clubs and performers for the exposure media deliver; sport reinvests in facilities, wages and technology. Quote realistic figures or examples where possible (Premier League broadcasting deals, shirt sponsorship, the Olympics' TOP sponsorship programme) - precision earns marks.

The media - types: television, radio, press, internet, streaming and social media (performers build personal brands directly). Positive effects on sport: massive income and investment (facilities, player wages, grass-roots funding via the Football Foundation model); raised profile and participation (visibility drives uptake - women's sport coverage growth); improved officiating and fairness through technology (VAR, TMO, Hawk-Eye, photo finishes); rule changes for excitement (T20 cricket, tie-breaks); global audience growth. Negative effects: scheduling changes for TV (kick-off times moved for broadcast windows - inconvenience to spectators and performers); breaks in play for advertising (commercialised formats); advertiser control (naming rights, kit obligations); loss of tradition (motorsport street circuits, franchise moves); win-at-all-costs pressure flowing from the financial stakes (linking to deviance); and unequal distribution (media money concentrates on few sports, starving minority and disability sport - the equity evaluation).

Effects on performers: dramatically higher incomes (prize money, wages, personal sponsorship); fame and profile through social media; but pressure and scrutiny (24/7 coverage, performance anxiety - link to sport psychology), privacy loss, and being traded/controlled by media value. Effects on spectators: wider access (global broadcast, streaming, highlights); improved viewing technology (multiple angles, statistics); but rising costs (subscription channels - Sky/TNT - versus free-to-air tradition; the listed events rule protecting events like the FA Cup final and Olympics for free TV is a good depth point), ticket price inflation from commercial demand, and kick-off times moved for television. Effects on the sport itself: financial dependence on media money (club finances tied to broadcast deals - a fragility evaluation: if rights income falls, wage bills collapse); rule and format changes; commercialisation of previously amateur events.

The listed events rule is the depth point on spectator rights: UK regulation protects certain events (the FA Cup final, the Olympics, the Grand National, the Wimbledon finals) for free-to-air television, preventing them being locked behind paywalls - quote it as the counterweight to full commercialisation. Social media deserves its own evaluation: performers build personal brands and direct income streams (bypassing traditional media); clubs and sports reach younger audiences; but 24/7 scrutiny brings online abuse, pressure to maintain the brand, and distraction from performance - the exam rewards both sides. Globalisation examples to cite: performers migrating to the best leagues (worldwide recruitment into the Premier League); overseas ownership of clubs; global transfer markets and wage inflation; the talent drain from developing nations (athletes and footballers leaving systems that invested in them); worldwide growth of women's football and franchise formats (T20 cricket) - evidence that globalisation spreads opportunity and dilutes tradition in the same movement.

The golden triangle applied - the worked example that scores: the Premier League sells broadcasting rights (media pay billions for exclusive coverage); broadcasters sell the audience to advertisers; sponsors pay clubs for shirt and stadium naming rights to reach that audience; the club reinvests in players and facilities (raising the product's quality, raising rights value again) - the self-reinforcing loop of the triangle. The Olympic TOP programme applies the same loop globally: worldwide sponsors (category-exclusive) fund the Games and the governing body, buying global exposure across broadcast windows. Media highlight culture: coverage now privileges spectacular, high-scoring, fast formats - a selection pressure on sport itself (rule changes for excitement: T20 cricket, shootouts, shot clocks) - the media shaping the sport, not just reporting it, is the deepest evaluation point. Paralympic coverage growth is the positive counter-example: Channel 4's coverage since 2012 transformed visibility and participation in disability sport - evidence that media exposure changes participation when it is deliberately targeted; contrast with minority sports still starved of coverage (the equity evaluation in one sentence).

Exam logic: for commercialisation questions, draw or describe the golden triangle, then evaluate: positives (investment, profile, participation, technology) against negatives (scheduling, control, inequity, win-at-all-costs), and finish with a judgement (commercialisation has transformed sport's quality and reach, but its benefits are unevenly distributed and its pressures feed deviance). For media questions, separate the effects on sport, performers and spectators - the mark schemes allocate marks across the three, so structuring the answer that way guarantees full coverage.

Ethics and deviance, violence, discrimination and routes to excellence (AQA 3.6; OCR 3.1)

Ethics in sport: sportsmanship - playing fairly, observing the rules and courtesy in victory and defeat; gamesmanship - winning by bending the rules without breaking them (time-wasting, sledging, professional fouls, distracting opponents); the contract to win - the modern expectation that performers give all-out commitment to victory, which critics argue has eroded sportsmanship. Deviance is behaviour outside accepted norms - on a spectrum from acceptable assertiveness to cheating. The exam frames ethics questions on this spectrum: identify where the behaviour sits (sportsmanship, gamesmanship, cheating), explain the cause (pressure to win, financial stakes, role models) and evaluate (some gamesmanship is tolerated by officials and crowds - a realism point).

Drugs and doping: reasons performers dope (a guaranteed explain list): the win-at-all-costs ethos and pressure from coaches, nation and self; enormous financial rewards for success; belief that rivals dope (arms-race logic); weak perceived risk of detection; pressure to recover from injury. Types to name: anabolic steroids (muscle mass, strength - health risks: liver damage, aggression), blood doping and EPO (raise red cell count and oxygen carriage - endurance; risks: thickened blood, clots), stimulants (alertness), beta blockers (steady fine-motor skills in precision sports), peptide hormones and gene doping as modern frontiers. WADA (World Anti-Doping Agency, 1999) writes the World Anti-Doping Code, maintains the prohibited list, coordinates testing (random, targeted, in and out of competition; the athlete biological passport) and sanctions (bans, stripped titles) via national bodies - in the UK, UKAD. Evaluation: testing has limits (new substances outpace detection; unequal testing resources between nations), but the biological passport and athlete whereabouts rules have tightened the net - a balanced judgement earns the top band. The legalisation debate is a classic evaluation: for legalisation (levelling the field under medical supervision, ending hypocrisy over permitted enhancements) against (health risks, unfairness to clean athletes, undermines the meaning of sport) - know both sides.

Match fixing: fixing outcomes or spot-fixing individual events (no-balls, bookings) for betting syndicates or results advantages; causes: betting markets (in-play betting created new fixing opportunities), low-paid performers in weakly governed leagues, weak regulation and detection; consequences: lifetime bans, criminal prosecution (criminal law has been applied to fixing), erosion of trust in results - name a case type (cricket spot-fixing, tennis betting investigations) for the application mark. Control: stricter regulation, betting-integrity monitoring, education, severe sanctions.

Violence: player violence - causes (a guaranteed explain list): instinct theory (Lorenz) - innate aggressive drive released in sport; frustration-aggression hypothesis (Dollard/Berkowitz) - blocked goals (losing, poor refereeing) create frustration that erupts; social learning (Bandura) - copying aggressive role models reinforced for violence (cite the Bobo doll study); plus situational causes - importance of the result, rivalries, crowd provocation, officials' weakness, arousal levels (link to sport psychology - the same dominant-response logic). Crowd violence/hooliganism: historical peak in British football in the 1970s-80s; causes: ritualised and territorial behaviour, group dynamics (deindividuation in crowds), alcohol, segregation failures, media hype of rivalries; control measures: all-seater stadia (post-Taylor Report after Hillsborough), segregation, alcohol bans, CCTV and banning orders, policing, and ticket pricing. Control of player violence: codes of conduct, sanctions (cards, sin-bin, retrospective video punishment), officials' authority, technology (VAR/TMO), coach modelling of assertive play, and arousal management - link psychology and sociology for the synthesis mark.

Discrimination: sexism - historical exclusion of women (Victorian medical myths about female frailty), persisting gaps in participation, prize money (pay gaps in many sports), coaching and leadership representation, and media coverage (women's sport historically under-broadcast - though coverage has grown sharply); initiatives: This Girl Can (Sport England), equal prize money at the Grand Slams in tennis, growth of women's leagues. Racism - abuse in stadia and online, stereotyping by position, and under-representation in coaching and boardrooms (the Rooney Rule-style initiatives); initiatives: Kick It Out, Show Racism the Red Card, sanction regimes. Disability - access barriers (facilities, transport, cost), segregation of para-sport from mainstream, coaching provision; initiatives: inclusive fitness equipment, the Paralympic movement's profile effect, disability NGB programmes. Positive discrimination (quotas and targets) is the evaluation battleground: for (corrects embedded imbalance, speeds change) against (merit concerns, tokenism) - a balanced conclusion earns the top band.

Routes to sporting excellence: the performance pyramid - grass-roots/foundation (school, primary competition), participation (club, community), performance (county, regional, academy), excellence (national, international). Talent identification (TID): NGB-run scouting and testing (e.g. football academies from age 8; the EIS/UK Sport power2perform-style profiling), school-club links and the School Games. Key organisations and funding (a guaranteed name-and-role list): UK Sport - distributes lottery funding to elite sport on a no-compromise medal model; Sport England - grass-roots participation; English Institute of Sport (EIS) - sports science and medicine support to elite performers; TASS (Talented Athlete Scholarship Scheme) - supports dual-career athletes (education plus training); national governing bodies (NGBs) - run talent pathways, academies and coaching structures. Exam logic: name the organisation and its role in one line each; for evaluation, note the medal-focus critique (funding concentrated on medal-chance sports starves others) and the dual-career balance point - that pairing reliably completes a 6-mark route-to-excellence answer.

Crowd control history in detail: the peak of British football hooliganism (1970s-80s) - ritualised, territorial clashes, group dynamics and deindividuation (anonymity in the crowd reduces personal responsibility - link to social loafing's cousin concept), alcohol, and media hype of rivalries; the Hillsborough disaster (1989) and the Taylor Report produced all-seater stadia, segregation, alcohol bans, CCTV and banning orders - the standard explain-and-control sequence; modern digital abuse extends the issue (online racial abuse of performers - link to discrimination). Discrimination initiatives expanded: This Girl Can (Sport England's participation campaign for women - normalising female sport at every level); Kick It Out and Show Racism the Red Card (education and reporting in football); women's sport coverage growth (dedicated broadcast deals) raising the profile-participation cycle; the Paralympic Games' profile effect (visibility of disability sport driving participation and provision); inclusive facilities programmes. Talent identification in detail: NGB-run academies (football category systems from young ages), performance profiling (testing against event-specific benchmarks), the English Institute of Sport providing science and medicine to funded athletes, TASS supporting dual-career athletes at university, and the School Games feeding the pyramid. Evaluation depth: the medal-focused funding model (UK Sport's no-compromise approach) delivers medals but concentrates money on a few sports - the equity critique that reliably completes a route-to-excellence answer; dual-career support keeps talented performers in education and the system.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: Evaluate the impact of commercialisation and the media on modern sport. (20 marks, OCR H555 style extended response)
Mark-scheme logic: Mark-scheme logic - AO1: knowledge of commercialisation, the golden triangle and media types; AO2: application to named sports/events; AO3: evaluation weighing benefits against costs with a justified conclusion. AO1/AO2: Commercialisation treats sport as a business - income from sponsorship, advertising, broadcasting rights, gate receipts and merchandising. The golden triangle shows the interdependence of sport, sponsorship and the media: media pay for rights because sponsors pay for the audience; sport reinvests the money in facilities, wages and grass-roots funding. Apply with examples: Premier League broadcast deals fund academy and community programmes; the Olympics' sponsorship programme (TOP) funds the Games; VAR and Hawk-Eye improve fairness; women's sport coverage has grown participation. AO3 positives: massive investment, raised profile and participation, global audiences, technological improvement of officiating and viewing, performer incomes. AO3 negatives: scheduling moved for TV and breaks in play for advertising; advertiser control (naming rights, kit rules); media money concentrated on few sports, starving minority and disability sport; club finances fragile - dependent on rights income; win-at-all-costs pressure from the stakes feeding deviance (doping, match fixing); spectator costs rising (subscription channels, ticket inflation). Conclusion: commercialisation and the media have transformed sport's quality, reach and fairness, but benefits are unevenly distributed and financial pressure feeds ethical problems - the strongest answers judge that the gains outweigh the costs only where governing bodies regulate the balance (listed events, integrity monitoring, equity funding).

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Describe the characteristics of pre-industrial popular recreations and explain how they contrast with rationalised post-industrial sport. (6 marks)
Answer: Popular recreations (3 marks): natural/simple (natural materials, no equipment), local (village-confined), occasional (holy days and festivals), severe (violent - mob football, baiting), ritual and wagering-based; caused by limited transport and communications, illiteracy, rural poverty and class separation. Contrast with rational sport (3 marks): regionally/nationally organised under codified written rules (governing bodies such as the FA, 1863), regular league fixtures, controlled by referees and sanctions, respectable middle-class amateur ideals - enabled by railways, the Saturday half-day, Factory Acts and rising wages.
Explain the role of the public schools in the codification and spread of sport in 19th-century Britain. (6 marks)
Answer: Development staged (1 mark per stage): early brutality was replaced by control through trust, with headmasters like Thomas Arnold at Rugby using team games to channel energy; athleticism and muscular Christianity promoted the belief that playing sport built manly Christian character (courage, leadership, fairness); each school codified its own rules (Rugby football, Eton's Field Game); the universities acted as the melting pot where old boys from different schools agreed common rules, producing national governing bodies (the FA, 1863, separating Association and Rugby football); old boys then spread codified games through universities, the church, the army, industry and abroad via the empire, with the railways enabling regular inter-town fixtures.
Explain why performers use drugs in sport and the role of WADA in controlling doping. (8 marks)
Answer: Reasons (1 mark each, four required): win-at-all-costs ethos and pressure from coaches, nation and self; large financial rewards for success; belief that rivals dope (arms-race logic); low perceived risk of detection; pressure to return from injury. Types credited: anabolic steroids, blood doping/EPO, stimulants, beta blockers. WADA role (1 mark each, four required): founded 1999 to coordinate world anti-doping; writes the World Anti-Doping Code; maintains the prohibited list; coordinates testing (random, targeted, out-of-competition, athlete biological passport) and sanctions (bans, stripped titles) with national bodies such as UKAD. Evaluation credit: detection lags new substances and testing resources are unequal between nations, but whereabouts rules and the biological passport have tightened enforcement.
Explain the causes of player violence in sport and how it can be controlled. (6 marks)
Answer: Causes (1 mark each, four required): instinct theory (Lorenz) - an innate aggressive drive released in competition; frustration-aggression hypothesis (Dollard, revised by Berkowitz) - blocked goals (losing, poor refereeing) create frustration that erupts; social learning theory (Bandura) - performers copy aggressive models reinforced for violence (Bobo doll study); situational factors - importance of the result, rivalries, crowd provocation, weak officiating, high arousal producing the dominant response. Control (1 mark each, two required): codes of conduct and sanctions (cards, sin-bin, retrospective video punishment); officials' authority plus technology (VAR, TMO); coach reinforcement of assertive rather than aggressive play; arousal management strategies.
Outline the route from talent identification to national sporting excellence, naming the key organisations and their roles. (6 marks)
Answer: Pyramid (1-2 marks): grass-roots/foundation (school, primary competition) - participation (club, community) - performance (county, regional, academy) - excellence (national, international); talent identification is run by NGBs through scouting, academies (e.g. football academies from a young age), school-club links and the School Games. Organisations (1 mark each, four required): UK Sport - distributes lottery funding to elite sport (no-compromise medal model); Sport England - funds grass-roots participation; English Institute of Sport - sports science and medicine support for elite performers; TASS - scholarships supporting dual-career athletes balancing education and training; NGBs - talent pathways, academies and coaching. Evaluation credit: the medal-focused funding model concentrates money on medal-chance sports, starving others; dual-career support is essential to keep talented athletes in the system.
