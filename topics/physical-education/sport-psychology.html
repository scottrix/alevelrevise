Sport Psychology

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC/Eduqas

A-level PE sport psychology revision: personality and attitudes, motivation and achievement theory, arousal (drive, inverted U, catastrophe), anxiety, aggression, social facilitation, group dynamics (Steiner, Ringelmann), goal setting, attribution (Weiner) and self-efficacy (Bandura) for AQA 7582, OCR H555 and Edexcel 9PE0.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Compare trait and interactionist theories of personality and apply the triadic model of attitudes, including changing attitudes via cognitive dissonance (AQA 3.5; OCR 2.2; Edexcel Topic 4)
  • Explain intrinsic and extrinsic motivation and achievement motivation theory (Atkinson and McClelland: NACH/NAF) and achievement goal theory (Nicholls) (AQA 3.5; OCR 2.2)
  • Evaluate arousal theories - drive theory (Hull), inverted U (Yerkes-Dodson), catastrophe model (Fazey and Hardy) and peak flow - and apply anxiety management techniques (AQA 3.5; OCR 2.2)
  • Distinguish trait and state anxiety, their measurement, and explain theories and control of aggression (AQA 3.5; OCR 2.2)
  • Explain social facilitation (Zajonc, evaluation apprehension) and group dynamics: Steiner's model, the Ringelmann effect and social loafing (AQA 3.5; OCR 2.2)
  • Apply goal setting (outcome/performance/process, SMART), Weiner's attribution theory with retraining, and Bandura's four sources of self-efficacy (AQA 3.5; OCR 2.2)

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Interactionist theory: Lewin's formula B = f(P, E): behaviour is a function of the personality (traits) and the environment (situation); predicts behaviour better than trait theory alone because traits are expressed differently in different sporting situations.
Cognitive dissonance: Festinger's technique for changing attitudes: creating a conflict (dissonance) between the components of the triadic model so the performer changes beliefs or behaviour to restore consistency.
Achievement motivation: Atkinson and McClelland's theory: the drive to succeed (NACH) competes with the fear of failure (NAF); high NACH performers seek challenges with moderate uncertainty, low NAF-dependent performers avoid risk.
Inverted U hypothesis: Yerkes and Dodson's arousal-performance relationship: performance improves with arousal up to an optimal point, then deteriorates; the optimum is lower for complex/fine skills, higher for simple/gross skills.
Catastrophe model: Fazey and Hardy's model: when cognitive anxiety (worry) is high, further increases in physiological arousal cause a sudden, catastrophic collapse in performance; recovery requires arousal to fall well below the original level, not just a small reduction.
Social loafing: The loss of individual effort in a group when contributions cannot be identified; it reduces actual productivity below potential and is countered by identifying individual contributions, roles and goals.
Attribution retraining: Changing a performer's reasons for failure from internal, stable causes (lack of ability) to internal, unstable, controllable ones (effort, strategy), maintaining expectations of future success and preventing learned helplessness.
Self-efficacy: Bandura's term for a performer's confidence in their ability to succeed in a specific situation; built from four sources - performance accomplishments, vicarious experiences, verbal persuasion and physiological/emotional states.

📌 Key Points

Personality: trait theory (stable, innate) vs interactionist (Lewin B = f(P,E)); Hollander's layers - psychological core, typical responses, role-related behaviour
Attitudes: triadic model (cognitive, affective, behavioural); changed by cognitive dissonance (Festinger) and persuasive communication (source, message, situation, recipient)
Arousal: drive theory (linear, dominant response), inverted U (optimal point, lower for complex skills), catastrophe (Fazey and Hardy - high cognitive anxiety plus arousal = sudden collapse), peak flow (complete absorption)
Anxiety: trait (stable, Spielberger/SCAT) vs state (situational); cognitive vs somatic; measured by questionnaires, observation, physiological measures
Groups: Steiner - actual = potential - losses (faulty processes: motivation, coordination); Ringelmann effect and social loafing countered by identifiable contributions and roles
Attribution (Weiner): locus (internal/external), stability (stable/unstable), control; retrain failure to internal unstable controllable (effort); Bandura self-efficacy: performance accomplishments, vicarious experiences, verbal persuasion, physiological states

Personality, attitudes and motivation (AQA 3.5; OCR 2.2; Edexcel Topic 4)

Personality is the characteristic pattern of behaviour, thoughts and feelings that makes an individual unique. Trait theory holds that personality consists of stable, innate characteristics shown consistently across situations: introverts (quiet, prefer individual sports, low optimal arousal - benefit from calm environments) and extroverts (sociable, arousal-seeking, prefer team and high-arousal situations - benefit from loud environments). Trait theory is simple but cannot explain why behaviour changes between situations - a guaranteed evaluation point. The interactionist approach (Lewin): behaviour is a function of personality and environment - B = f(P, E) - traits are stable but expressed differently depending on the situation; a calm netball captain may become aggressive in a derby. Hollander's personality structure earns depth: three layers - the psychological core (true values, deepest), typical responses (usual behaviour) and role-related behaviour (surface, situation-dependent). Measuring personality: questionnaires and observation; evaluation: questionnaires can be faked and behaviour is not fully predictable - the exam rewards saying that personality alone is a weak predictor of sporting behaviour.

Attitudes are evaluations of people, objects and situations expressed in three components - the triadic model: cognitive (beliefs and knowledge - swimming is good for fitness), affective (feelings and emotions - enjoying swimming) and behavioural (actions - swimming twice a week). Attitudes form through socialisation (parents, peers, coaches) and conditioning (association with positive experiences). Changing attitudes: (1) cognitive dissonance (Festinger) - deliberately creating a conflict between components (a swimmer who believes they dislike training but keeps winning races must change one component to restore consistency, typically their beliefs), pushing the performer to re-evaluate; (2) persuasive communication - changing attitudes by the message itself, effective when the source is credible and significant (elite coach), the message is clear, relevant and balanced, the situation is comfortable, and the recipient is ready to change (low resistance). Evaluation: attitudes are hard to change if all three components are strong and consistent; dissonance works best when components are already in tension - a standard evaluation point.

Motivation is the internal drive and external pressures that direct and sustain behaviour. Intrinsic motivation (from within - enjoyment, satisfaction, mastery, pride) is more durable and linked to long-term participation; extrinsic motivation (from outside - rewards, prizes, praise, avoidance of punishment) works quickly but can undermine intrinsic motivation if overused - the overjustification effect: tangible rewards (money) can replace enjoyment as the reason for playing, and when the reward stops, participation stops - the guaranteed evaluation of reward schemes. Best practice: use extrinsic rewards initially, then shift to intrinsic satisfaction, making rewards informational (feedback on competence) rather than controlling.

Achievement motivation theory (Atkinson and McClelland): motivation to achieve results is the interaction between the need to achieve (NACH) and the need to avoid failure (NAF), modified by the probability of success and the incentive value of success. High-NACH performers approach achievement situations, seek challenges with moderate risk (uncertain outcomes raise incentive value) and persist after failure; high-NAF performers avoid risk, choose easy tasks (guaranteed success) or very hard ones (no blame for failure), and give up after failure. Developing achievement motivation: attribute success internally (ability, effort), set challenging but reachable goals, provide reinforcement for approach behaviour - links directly to attribution retraining. Achievement goal theory (Nicholls) adds orientation: task-oriented performers judge success against personal mastery (more resilient, sustained motivation) versus ego-oriented performers judging success against others (vulnerable when outperformed); promoting task orientation (mastery climate) sustains motivation - a top-band application. Exam logic: for motivation questions, define intrinsic/extrinsic, give the overjustification evaluation, then link NACH/NAF behaviour to the performer described in the stem.

Self-determination theory (Deci and Ryan) is the optional depth that separates A* answers: intrinsic motivation is sustained by three needs - autonomy (choice in how to train), competence (feeling effective - link to self-efficacy) and relatedness (connection to others - link to cohesion); coaching that satisfies the three needs builds durable motivation, while controlling coaching (rewards, punishment, pressure) undermines it - the theoretical basis for the informational-rewards strategy.

Attitude change worked example: a junior swimmer believes training is pointless (cognitive) but keeps winning (behavioural success). The coach creates cognitive dissonance by pairing training with enjoyable game-based sets and pointing out the race wins that followed training weeks - the conflict between belief and evidence forces the swimmer to change the belief to restore consistency. Persuasive communication factors applied: the source (a respected, elite coach or performer - credibility), the message (clear, relevant, balanced - not one-sided lectures), the situation (comfortable, low-pressure), the recipient (ready to change - low resistance; a strongly attached attitude resists change). Attitude measurement: questionnaires (quick, scalable, but can be faked) and observation of behaviour (real but subjective and time-consuming) - cite both when asked how attitudes are measured. Exam logic: for attitude questions, name the three components, give the change technique (dissonance or persuasion), then apply to the named performer with one evaluation point (strong, consistent attitudes resist change).

Personality applied to sport choice - the trait evidence the exam expects: introverts (lower optimal arousal) prefer individual, fine-skill, low-arousal sports (long-distance running, archery, golf) where concentration dominates and arousal stays controlled; extroverts (higher optimal arousal, arousal-seeking) prefer team, gross-skill, high-arousal environments (rugby, football) where excitement is part of the appeal. The correlation is a tendency, not a rule - repeat the caution (interactionist correction) for the evaluation mark. Measuring personality: questionnaires (e.g. EPI-style trait inventories, POMS mood profiling) - quick and scalable but self-report can be faked; observation of behaviour - real but subjective and time-consuming; neither fully predicts performance, so the exam's evaluation is that personality is a weak single predictor - behaviour is better predicted by the interaction of traits and situation.

Motivation applied to task choice - the NACH/NAF behaviour the stem expects: a high-NACH performer takes the risky pass, attempts the difficult trick, seeks competition, and persists after failure (approach behaviour); a high-NAF performer takes the safe option, avoids the spotlight, picks easy tasks (or impossible ones where failure carries no blame) and withdraws after setbacks (avoidance behaviour). The coach develops achievement motivation by attributing success internally (ability and effort - link to Weiner), setting challenging-but-reachable goals (moderate probability of success raises incentive value), reinforcing approach behaviour, and reframing failure as information (attribution retraining) - the synthesis across motivation, attribution and goal setting that full-mark answers chain together. Attitude formation: through socialisation (parents, peers, coaches, media role models), conditioning (association of the activity with positive experiences) and familiarity (repeated exposure breeds liking) - cite socialisation as the primary route, then link to the change techniques (dissonance, persuasion) for the full attitude answer.

Arousal, anxiety, aggression and social facilitation (AQA 3.5; OCR 2.2)

Arousal is the physiological and psychological readiness (activation) of the performer, ranging from deep sleep to high excitement. Four theories, evaluated in pairs: Drive theory (Hull): performance = habit strength x drive - as arousal increases, performance increases in a linear relationship, but the dominant response is produced: well-learned (expert) dominant responses are correct, so arousal helps experts; novice dominant responses are wrong, so arousal harms novices. Evaluation: predicts a straight line that real performance data do not support (performance declines past a point), but the expert-novice distinction is genuinely useful. Inverted U hypothesis (Yerkes and Dodson): performance rises with arousal to an optimal point, then declines gradually - too little arousal (under-activated, unmotivated) and too much (over-aroused, tense) both impair performance. The optimum is lower for complex, fine or novel skills (archery, golf putting) and higher for simple, gross or well-learned skills (weightlifting, sprint start) - the required application. Evaluation: better than drive theory (captures the decline), but treats arousal as one-dimensional and cannot explain sudden collapse - which is where the catastrophe model wins.

Catastrophe model (Fazey and Hardy): performance depends on the interaction of physiological arousal and cognitive anxiety (worry). When cognitive anxiety is low, the inverted U holds; when cognitive anxiety is high, rising physiological arousal produces a sudden, catastrophic drop in performance (choking) rather than a gradual decline. Recovery is asymmetric: arousal must fall well below the original optimal point before performance recovers - a subtle but examinable detail. Peak flow (Csikszentmihalyi's flow state): the ideal state of complete absorption - total focus, effortless automatic performance, sense of control, intrinsic reward - reached when the challenge of the task matches the skill of the performer with optimal arousal. Controlling arousal: somatic (physiological) techniques - progressive muscle relaxation (tense then release muscle groups), breathing control (slow, deep diaphragmatic breathing), biofeedback (monitoring heart rate/sweat to learn control), warm-up routines; cognitive (mental) techniques - mental rehearsal/imagery (visualising successful performance), positive self-talk, goal setting, mindfulness/meditation, attention control (focusing on relevant cues). Match the technique to the type: somatic techniques lower physiological arousal, cognitive techniques lower cognitive anxiety - the exam links them to the catastrophe model's two variables.

Anxiety is a negative emotional state with feelings of worry and apprehension. Distinguish the pair precisely: trait anxiety - a stable, inherited disposition to perceive situations as threatening (measured by the SCAT questionnaire); state anxiety - the temporary, situation-specific level of anxiety right now (measured by the STAI/CSAI-2 questionnaires, which separate cognitive anxiety - worry/negative thoughts - from somatic anxiety - physiological symptoms like raised heart rate and butterflies). Measurement (a guaranteed 3-mark list): (1) questionnaires/self-report (SCAT, STAI, CSAI-2 - quick but can be faked); (2) observation of behaviour (visible signs - quick, subjective); (3) physiological measures (heart rate, galvanic skin response, sweat, blood pressure - objective but expensive and the equipment itself can raise anxiety). High trait anxiety performers show more state anxiety in competition - link the two for the application mark.

Aggression is deliberate behaviour outside the rules with the intent to harm; contrast with assertive behaviour - forceful, goal-directed play within the rules with no intent to harm (a hard but fair tackle is assertive; an elbow after the whistle is aggressive). Types: hostile aggression (deliberate harm, anger-driven, e.g. retaliation) and instrumental aggression (harm used as a means to a goal - a late tackle to stop a scorer, harm accepted though not the aim). Theories: instinct theory (Lorenz/Freud) - aggression is innate, cathartic when released (weakly supported - sport does not reliably reduce aggression); social learning theory (Bandura) - aggression is learned by observing and copying models reinforced for it (the Bobo doll study - children copied observed aggression; cite for the study mark); frustration-aggression hypothesis (Dollard, revised by Berkowitz) - blocked goals create frustration, which can produce aggression (but frustration does not always cause aggression - the revision point). Controlling aggression (a standard list): punishment and sanctions (sin-bin, fines, sending off), codes of conduct and contracts, coach reinforcement of assertive not aggressive play, stress and arousal management, removing performers before frustration escalates, and channelling aggression into assertive effort.

Social facilitation is the effect of the presence of others on performance: Zajonc - the presence of others raises arousal and produces the dominant response: helping simple, well-learned skills, hindering complex, novel skills - the same expert/novice logic as drive theory, and the guaranteed application. Triplett's study (cyclists rode faster with co-actors than alone) is the classic evidence citation. Types of presence: co-actors (others performing the same task), passive audience (watching, not judging), evaluative audience - evaluation apprehension (Cottrell): the presence of others who judge performance raises arousal further and impairs novices most; proximity effect (the closer the audience, the greater the effect). Coping strategies: train with an audience present (habituation), use selective attention to block out the crowd, imagery and positive self-talk, ensure skills are overlearned so the dominant response is correct, and gradually introduce competition pressure - link the strategies back to the dominant-response logic for full marks.

Measuring arousal and anxiety - the same toolkit applies to both (arousal is neutral activation, anxiety its negative form): self-report questionnaires (CSAI-2 for competitive state anxiety, separating cognitive and somatic components; STAI for state/trait; SCAT for trait) - quick and cheap but can be faked; observation of behaviour (visible signs - pacing, agitation; quick but subjective); physiological measures (heart rate, galvanic skin response, blood pressure, cortisol) - objective but expensive, and the apparatus itself can raise anxiety (a reactivity evaluation point).

Matching arousal strategies to the skill - the applied table the exam links to the inverted U:

Evaluation apprehension depth (Cottrell): an audience that merely watches raises arousal modestly; an audience that judges (selectors, examiners, scouts) raises it far more - explaining why trials and assessments are the worst-case scenario for novices; the proximity effect (the closer and larger the audience, the greater the effect) and the type of audience (supportive versus hostile) complete the picture. Strategies to combat social inhibition: habituation training (practising with audiences present until the presence stops raising arousal), selective attention (focusing on task cues, blocking the crowd), imagery and positive self-talk, ensuring skills are overlearned (dominant response correct), and gradual exposure to competition pressure. Exam logic: for social facilitation questions, state Zajonc's dominant-response logic, name the audience types (co-actors, passive, evaluative), then match strategies to the performer - habituation and overlearning for novices, selective attention for experts facing hostile crowds.

Peak flow characteristics (Csikszentmihalyi) - the detail that completes arousal answers: the flow state combines total absorption in the task, effortless automatic performance, a strong sense of control, distorted time perception, and intrinsic reward - reached when the challenge of the task matches the skill of the performer with optimal arousal and no distraction. The practical implication: flow is engineered by matching challenge to skill (goals slightly above current ability), removing distractions (attention control), and rehearsed routines - link it to goal setting and selective attention for the synthesis mark. Aggression distinctions applied: a two-footed lunge after the whistle is hostile aggression (intent to harm, anger-driven); a late but ball-winning tackle in a cup final is instrumental aggression (harm as a means, the goal the aim); a ferocious but legal 50-50 challenge is assertive behaviour (forceful, within the rules, no intent to harm). The channelled aggression concept completes it: performers with aggressive tendencies (high trait arousal, extroverts) can have their energy channelled into assertive, legal intensity by coaching - the constructive use of aggression that separates good coaching from suppression.

Group dynamics, goal setting, attribution and self-efficacy (AQA 3.5; OCR 2.2)

Groups and teams: a group is two or more people with a shared goal and interaction; a team adds shared identity, interdependence and collective accountability - a relay squad is a group, a netball seven is a team. Steiner's model explains why teams often underperform: actual productivity = potential productivity - losses due to faulty processes. Potential productivity is the best possible output from the group's resources and the task demands; faulty processes are (1) motivation losses - reduced individual effort (social loafing) and (2) coordination losses - poor interaction, timing and communication (a relay changeover dropped, a defence failing to mark). Application logic: identify whether the losses in the question stem are motivational (effort) or coordination (interaction) - the mark scheme distinguishes them. The Ringelmann effect: as group size increases, individual productivity decreases (Ringelmann's rope-pulling study: individuals pulled less hard in larger groups) - coordination losses grow with size. Social loafing: the loss of individual motivation when contributions cannot be identified (a footballer walking in defence because no one will notice). Strategies to counter losses (a guaranteed list): make individual contributions identifiable (statistics, individual roles), set individual and group goals, assign clear roles and responsibilities, build cohesion, reward individual effort, use video analysis to highlight individual input, and select committed performers.

Cohesion is the tendency of a group to stick together and remain united in pursuing goals: task cohesion (unity toward the task - most predictive of team performance) versus social cohesion (unity of relationships - matters for sustained participation). Carron's factors affecting cohesion: personal (individual motivation, satisfaction), team (shared goals, success, ability), leadership (coach style, decision-making), environmental (group size, time together, facilities). Building cohesion (top-band application): team goal setting, role clarification, social activities, reducing group size where possible, uniform and identity. Cohesion relates to performance positively, especially task cohesion in interactive sports - a discrimination point (co-active sports like swimming need less social cohesion).

Goal setting directs and sustains motivation. Types: outcome goals (the result - winning the league; motivating but not fully controllable - opponents determine them), performance goals (standards relative to own previous performance - 800 m personal best, 90% first-serve; fully controllable, best for building confidence), process goals (the technique/actions - keep the elbow high in the serve; most useful for correcting technique and maintaining focus). SMART principles: goals must be Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Realistic and Time-bound; set short-term goals (laddering toward the long-term goal) with regular review. Functions: directs attention to relevant cues, mobilises effort, builds persistence, develops new strategies, and raises confidence through measured success - match the goal type to the performer's need for the application mark.

Attribution theory (Weiner) explains how performers explain success and failure, along three dimensions: locus (internal - within the performer, e.g. ability/effort; external - outside, e.g. task difficulty/luck), stability (stable - unlikely to change; unstable - changeable) and control (controllable or not by the performer). The four classic attributions: ability (internal, stable, uncontrollable), effort (internal, unstable, controllable), task difficulty (external, stable, uncontrollable) and luck (external, unstable, uncontrollable). Effects on motivation: attributing success to internal, stable causes (ability) sustains confidence and expectation of future success; attributing failure to internal, stable causes (lack of ability) destroys motivation and produces learned helplessness (Seligman) - the belief that failure is inevitable, so effort is withdrawn. Attribution retraining (the guaranteed application): the coach changes failure attributions to internal, unstable, controllable causes - effort and strategy - so the performer retains the expectation that change is possible, maintains motivation, and moves from learned helplessness to a mastery orientation (persisting after setbacks). Self-serving bias (attributing success internally, failure externally) protects confidence short-term - an optional depth point.

Self-efficacy (Bandura): a performer's confidence in their ability to succeed in a specific situation - distinct from general self-confidence; it predicts the tasks chosen, effort, persistence and resilience. Four sources, in order of influence (a guaranteed 4-mark list): (1) performance accomplishments - the most powerful source: actual success builds efficacy (structured success experiences, achievable goals - link to goal setting); (2) vicarious experiences - observing a similar other succeed (watching a comparable player succeed raises the belief that one can too - link to social learning/ARMD); (3) verbal persuasion - encouragement from significant others (credible coaches, positive self-talk); (4) physiological and emotional states - interpreting arousal and feelings positively (butterflies read as readiness rather than fear - link to anxiety management). High self-efficacy performers attempt harder challenges and persist; low self-efficacy performers avoid risk and give up - Vealey's sport confidence adds the trait (trait sport confidence) versus state (competitive orientation) distinction for depth. Exam logic: for attribution and self-efficacy questions, name the theorist, give the dimensions/sources precisely, then apply to the performer in the stem with a strategy the coach uses.

The Ringelmann study in detail: individuals pulling a rope alone pulled at 100% effort, but as group size grew the individual contribution fell (to roughly half in groups of eight) - Ringelmann attributed this to coordination losses (pulling is not perfectly synchronised) and motivation losses (effort disperses). This is the classic evidence citation for both the Ringelmann effect and social loafing - quote it when a study is required. Steiner applied to task types: interactive tasks (netball, football - team members depend on each other) suffer most from coordination losses (poor timing, communication, tactics - a dropped relay changeover, a failed defensive line) so the remedies are structural: roles, drills, cohesion, communication systems; co-active tasks (rowing eight, relay, cycling team pursuit - individuals perform alongside each other) suffer most from motivation losses (social loafing - effort disperses because contributions blur), so the remedies are motivational: identifiable contributions, individual goals, individual statistics. The exam rewards matching the loss type to the task type - the most common lost mark is prescribing cohesion drills for a co-active sport.

Goal setting worked example: a 400 m runner targeting the county final. Outcome goal: reach the county final (motivating, but not fully controllable - opponents determine it). Performance goal: reduce the personal best by 1.5 seconds within 8 weeks (fully controllable, builds confidence). Process goal: keep the arm action tall and relaxed through the final 100 m (technique focus, corrects the mechanism). All three must be SMART - specific, measurable, achievable, realistic, time-bound - and laddered: short-term process goals feed the mid-term performance goal feeds the long-term outcome goal, with regular review to adjust. Learned helplessness versus mastery orientation (Seligman): learned helplessness is the belief that failure is inevitable (attributed to internal, stable, uncontrollable causes - 'I am not good enough'), so effort is withdrawn; a mastery orientation persists after setbacks (failure attributed to internal, unstable, controllable causes - effort and strategy). Self-serving bias - attributing success internally (ability) and failure externally (luck, officials) - protects confidence short-term but blocks learning: the coach should retrain both success and failure attributions honestly. Exam logic: for attribution questions, dimensions first, then the motivational consequence, then retraining as the application; for group questions, Steiner first, then the loss type matched to the task, then strategies matched to the loss.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: A footballer converts penalties reliably in training but his performance collapses suddenly in a cup shoot-out. Explain the catastrophe model of arousal and evaluate strategies he could use to prevent the collapse. (10 marks, AQA Paper 2 style)
Mark-scheme logic: Mark-scheme logic - AO1: accurate description of the catastrophe model and strategies; AO2: application to the shoot-out scenario; AO3: evaluation with strengths, limitations and a conclusion. AO1: Fazey and Hardy's catastrophe model holds that performance depends on the interaction of physiological arousal and cognitive anxiety; when cognitive anxiety (worry) is low, the inverted U relationship holds, but when cognitive anxiety is high, further increases in physiological arousal cause a sudden, catastrophic drop in performance rather than a gradual decline; recovery requires arousal to fall well below the original optimal point. AO2: the shoot-out raises cognitive anxiety (high stakes, importance of outcome) and physiological arousal (crowd, fatigue) - crossing the threshold produces the sudden collapse, explaining why reliable training performance fails in competition. AO3 strategies: cognitive techniques lower cognitive anxiety - imagery/mental rehearsal of successful penalties, positive self-talk, outcome-independent process goals (strike technique), mindfulness; somatic techniques lower physiological arousal - progressive muscle relaxation, slow breathing, biofeedback, a consistent pre-shot routine. Evaluation: cognitive techniques target the model's key variable (cognitive anxiety) so are most relevant; routines and overlearned technique make the dominant response correct (link to drive theory - expertise protects against arousal); overconfidence is a risk if arousal falls too low (inverted U left side); conclusion - combining cognitive anxiety management with a rehearsed routine gives the best protection because it addresses both variables of the model.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Explain the drive theory and the inverted U hypothesis of arousal, including one limitation of each. (5 marks)
Answer: Drive theory (Hull) (2 marks): performance = habit strength x drive; as arousal rises, performance rises linearly and the dominant response is produced - correct for experts with well-learned skills, incorrect for novices. Limitation (1 mark): real performance data show a decline beyond an optimum, so a straight-line prediction is inaccurate. Inverted U hypothesis (Yerkes and Dodson) (2 marks): performance improves with arousal up to an optimal point then gradually declines; the optimum is lower for complex/fine/novel skills and higher for simple/gross/well-learned skills. Limitation (1 mark): treats arousal as one-dimensional and cannot explain sudden catastrophic collapse (high cognitive anxiety) - hence the catastrophe model.
Distinguish trait and state anxiety and identify three ways anxiety can be measured. (5 marks)
Answer: Trait anxiety (1-2 marks): a stable, inherited disposition to perceive situations as threatening; consistent across situations (measured by SCAT). State anxiety (1-2 marks): the temporary, situation-specific emotional response right now, varying between occasions (measured by STAI/CSAI-2); high-trait performers show more state anxiety in competition. Measurement (1 mark for three): self-report questionnaires (SCAT, STAI, CSAI-2 - separates cognitive and somatic anxiety, but can be faked); observation of behaviour (visible signs; quick but subjective); physiological measures (heart rate, galvanic skin response; objective but costly and the apparatus itself can raise anxiety).
Explain Steiner's model, the Ringelmann effect and social loafing, and give three strategies a coach uses to reduce losses in a team. (8 marks)
Answer: Steiner's model (2-3 marks): actual productivity = potential productivity - losses due to faulty processes; potential productivity is the group's best possible output given resources and task demands; faulty processes are motivation losses (reduced effort) and coordination losses (poor interaction, timing, communication - e.g. a dropped relay changeover). Ringelmann effect (1-2 marks): as group size increases, individual productivity decreases (rope-pulling study) because coordination losses grow. Social loafing (1-2 marks): individual motivation falls when contributions cannot be identified - a footballer not tracking back because no one will notice. Strategies (1 mark for three): make individual contributions identifiable (statistics, video analysis, individual roles); set individual and group goals; assign clear roles and responsibilities; build task cohesion; reward individual effort; select committed performers.
Explain Weiner's attribution dimensions and how a coach should retrain attributions after a losing run. (6 marks)
Answer: Dimensions (2-3 marks): locus - internal (within the performer: ability, effort) or external (task difficulty, luck); stability - stable (unlikely to change) or unstable (changeable); control - controllable or uncontrollable by the performer. The four classic attributions: ability (internal stable), effort (internal unstable controllable), task difficulty (external stable), luck (external unstable). Effects (1-2 marks): attributing failure to internal stable causes (lack of ability) produces learned helplessness (Seligman) - failure feels inevitable and effort is withdrawn; attributing success to internal stable causes (ability) sustains confidence. Retraining (1-2 marks): the coach changes failure attributions to internal, unstable, controllable causes - effort and strategy - so the performer retains the expectation that change is possible, maintaining motivation and promoting a mastery orientation that persists after setbacks.
Explain Bandura's four sources of self-efficacy with a sporting example of each. (6 marks)
Answer: Self-efficacy is confidence in the ability to succeed in a specific situation. Four sources (1-1.5 marks each): performance accomplishments - the most powerful; actual successful experiences build belief, e.g. structured achievable goals that the performer meets in training; vicarious experiences - observing a similar other succeed, e.g. a junior watching a comparable-size player win a scrimmage raises the belief that they can too; verbal persuasion - encouragement from significant others, e.g. a credible coach's specific praise plus positive self-talk; physiological and emotional states - interpreting arousal positively, e.g. reading pre-match butterflies as readiness rather than fear. Application: high self-efficacy performers choose harder challenges, persist longer and recover from failure faster; low self-efficacy performers avoid risk and give up - so the coach deliberately engineers success experiences first.
