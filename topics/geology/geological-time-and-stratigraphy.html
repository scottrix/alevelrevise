Geological Time and Stratigraphy

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level WJEC/Eduqas

Relative and radiometric dating, fossils and correlation, stratigraphic principles and the geological time scale - Eduqas A-Level Geology core (time and change; past life and past climates) with exam-style questions and mark schemes.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Apply the principles of superposition, original horizontality, lateral continuity, cross-cutting relationships, inclusions and faunal succession to sequence events on maps and sections
  • Recognise and classify unconformity types and describe the events each implies
  • Use radiometric systems (U-Pb, K-Ar, Rb-Sr, C-14) to calculate ages from half-lives and state the assumptions and limitations of each
  • Explain the conditions for fossil preservation and evaluate index fossils and biozones for correlation
  • Sequence the divisions of the geological time scale, quoting key events and mass extinctions, and apply them synoptically to British geology

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Principle of superposition: In an undeformed sedimentary or volcanic sequence, each bed is younger than the one beneath it.
Principle of cross-cutting relationships: A rock unit, fault or intrusion is younger than any rock it cuts; an inclusion is older than the host rock that contains it.
Faunal succession: Fossil organisms succeed one another in a definite, non-repeating vertical order, so fossil assemblages can be used to order and correlate strata.
Unconformity: A buried erosion surface separating younger rocks above from older rocks below, representing a gap in the geological record (types: angular, disconformity, nonconformity, paraconformity).
Half-life: The time for half of the atoms of a radioactive parent isotope to decay to its daughter product; constant for each isotope regardless of temperature or pressure.
Radiometric dating: Absolute dating by measuring parent:daughter isotope ratios in a mineral or rock and applying the decay equation age = (1/lambda) x ln(1 + D/P).
Index fossil: A fossil species ideal for correlation: abundant, geographically widespread, easily identified, and with a short vertical (stratigraphical) range due to rapid evolution.
Biozone: A body of rock defined by its fossil content, e.g. a range zone (total range of one species), assemblage zone (association of species) or acme zone (peak abundance).
Correlation: Establishing equivalence in age between rock units in different areas, using lithostratigraphic, biostratigraphic, magnetostratigraphic, chemostratigraphic or tephrochronological evidence.
Mass extinction: A geologically short interval of globally elevated extinction; the Big Five are end-Ordovician, Late Devonian, end-Permian, end-Triassic and end-Cretaceous (K-Pg).

📌 Key Points

Radiometric half-lives: U-238 4.5 Ga; U-235 704 Ma; K-40 1.25 Ga; Rb-87 48.8 Ga; C-14 5730 yr (useful limit about 50-60 ka).
Age of Earth about 4.567 Ga (from meteorite and oldest zircon U-Pb ages); oldest rocks about 4.0 Ga (Acasta gneiss).
Requirements for a valid radiometric age: closed system, known/corrected initial daughter, half-life appropriate to the age range.
The Precambrian is about 88% of geological time; Phanerozoic divisions: Palaeozoic, Mesozoic, Cenozoic.
Big Five extinctions: end-Ordovician (~445 Ma), Late Devonian (~372 Ma), end-Permian (252 Ma, largest), end-Triassic (~201 Ma), K-Pg (66 Ma, iridium anomaly, Chicxulub).

Relative dating and stratigraphic principles

Event-ordering questions on maps and sections are marked by the principle quoted for each relationship. Know all six and apply them explicitly: superposition (youngest at top in undeformed sequences), original horizontality (sediments deposit on near-horizontal surfaces; tilted or folded beds show later deformation), lateral continuity (a bed once extended until it thinned out or was truncated - allows displaced sequences to be matched across faults), cross-cutting relationships (faults, intrusions and veins are younger than the rocks they cut), inclusions (fragments in a host are older than the host - e.g. xenoliths in granite, clasts in conglomerate) and faunal succession (fossils order the strata). Always write the reasoning: state the principle as well as the event order.

Unconformities represent gaps in the record (time missing = erosion and/or non-deposition) and must be classified:

TypeRecognitionImplication
AngularOlder beds tilted/folded, younger beds lie across them at an angleDeformation + uplift + erosion + subsidence + renewed deposition
DisconformityBeds parallel either side but an erosion surface (relief, palaeosol, basal conglomerate) separates themUplift/erosion or non-deposition without deformation
NonconformitySediments rest on igneous/metamorphic basementLong erosion unroofed deep-seated rocks
ParaconformityParallel beds, no visible relief - gap proved only by fossils/agesNon-deposition or subtle erosion

On sections, sequence the full event history: deposition, deformation, erosion (unconformity), renewed deposition, intrusion, faulting - in that logical order, with each principle named. The way-up structures from sedimentology (graded bedding, cross-bedding) also matter here: inverted limbs of folds are identified by way-up evidence, which changes the whole event sequence.

Event-ordering drill - a sequence question wants exactly this structure. Given: mudstone, overlain by sandstone, cut by a dyke, intruded by granite with xenoliths of both sediments, all unconformably overlain by conglomerate: (1) mudstone deposited; (2) sandstone deposited (superposition); (3) dyke intruded (cross-cutting - younger than both sediments); (4) granite intruded (contains inclusions of both sediments AND the dyke - younger than all three); (5) uplift and erosion; (6) conglomerate deposited (unconformity - youngest). Note the inclusion logic: the dyke fragments appear in the granite, so the granite post-dates the dyke even though both cut the sediments - the classic discriminating mark.

Absolute dating and radiometric methods

Absolute (numerical) ages come from radiometric dating: radioactive parent isotopes decay to stable daughters at a fixed half-life, so the measured parent:daughter ratio gives the age. Learn the examinable systems and their applications:

SystemHalf-lifeUse
U-238 to Pb-2064.47 GaOldest rocks, Earth age (4.567 Ga from meteorites); zircon U-Pb is the gold standard
U-235 to Pb-207704 MaOld Palaeozoic rocks; concordant U-Pb pairs cross-check
K-40 to Ar-401.25 GaIgneous rocks containing K-feldspar, mica, hornblende; volcanic ash (tephra) horizons
Rb-87 to Sr-8748.8 GaWhole-rock dating of granites; isochron method corrects initial daughter Sr
C-14 to N-145730 yrOrganic remains (peat, wood, shell); useful to about 50-60 ka - Quaternary only

Half-life calculations are routine: after n half-lives, parent remaining = (1/2)^n. Example: a rock with 12.5% of its original K-40 has lost 87.5% = 3 half-lives, so age = 3 x 1.25 Ga = 3.75 Ga. Reverse problems (given age, find fraction remaining) are equally common - practise both.

Assumptions and limitations earn the evaluation marks: the system must have remained closed (no gain/loss of parent or daughter - argon especially is lost on reheating, so K-Ar dates the last cooling, not crystallisation); the initial daughter content must be zero or corrected (the Rb-Sr isochron solves this by plotting 87Sr/86Sr against 87Rb/86Sr for several co-magmatic whole-rock samples: slope = e^(lambda t) - 1, intercept = initial ratio); the half-life must suit the age range (C-14 useless beyond about 50-60 ka; Rb-Sr poor for young rocks). Metamorphism can reset or partially reset systems - a granite giving a K-Ar mica age younger than its U-Pb zircon age records cooling after metamorphism, not a contradiction. Non-radiometric absolute methods for the Quaternary: dendrochronology (tree rings, calibrates C-14), varves (annual glacial lake couplets), ice cores.

Choosing the right system is the applied skill: zircon survives metamorphism and gives U-Pb crystallisation ages - the system of choice for ancient rocks; K-Ar on mica/hornblende dates cooling through about 300-350 C, so it dates metamorphism or the last heating event; Rb-Sr whole-rock suits granites but needs an isochron; C-14 suits only young organic material (peat, shell) to about 50-60 ka. Quote precision: modern U-Pb gives about +/-0.1% (about 1 Ma at 1 Ga); K-Ar on young volcanics to about +/-1-2%. Errors and interpretation matter: a K-Ar age younger than a U-Pb age on the same granite records slow cooling after intrusion or later metamorphism - not a failed analysis. Link to the Quaternary: dendrochronology (tree-ring counts to about 14 ka) and varves calibrate the C-14 scale; ice cores count annual layers to about 100 ka.

Fossils, evolution and correlation

Fossilisation is exceptional: it requires rapid burial and usually hard parts, commonly in low-oxygen (anoxic) sediment. Modes of preservation: unaltered shells/teeth, permineralisation (pore spaces filled - petrified wood), replacement (original material dissolved and replaced - pyritised, silicified, calcitised ammonites), carbonisation (volatiles distilled leaving black film - graptolites, fish, plants), moulds and casts (external/internal impressions filled to form natural casts). Life assemblages (in life position, e.g. crinoids rooted, oysters in clusters) vs death assemblages (transported, sorted, broken) are examined in Component 1 - use orientation, breakage and size sorting as evidence.

Index fossils must be: abundant, widespread geographically, easy to identify, and short-ranging (rapid evolution). The classic British macrofossil index groups by era: graptolites (Ordovician-Silurian), trilobites (Palaeozoic), brachiopods and corals (Palaeozoic), ammonites (Mesozoic, with lineage zones), belemnites (Mesozoic). Microfossils serve hydrocarbon exploration: foraminifera, conodonts, pollen and spores - recovered from borehole cuttings. Biozones formalise correlation: range zones, assemblage zones, acme zones.

Correlation uses multiple independent methods - know their reliability ranking: lithostratigraphic correlation (matching rock type) is least reliable alone (same facies can repeat in time - facies is diachronous); biostratigraphic (fossil zones) is the backbone of the Phanerozoic; magnetostratigraphic (reversal chrons matched to the global polarity time scale); chemostratigraphic (d18O, d13C, Sr isotope curves); tephrochronology (instantaneous volcanic ash markers, e.g. the Vedde Ash). Exam questions ask you to correlate boreholes or field sections and justify the method - always prefer fossil/isotope evidence to lithology and say why.

Evolution: know gradualism versus punctuated equilibrium (long stasis punctuated by rapid speciation, often after extinctions - fits the fossil record's abrupt appearances), adaptive radiation after extinctions (mammals after K-Pg), and the Cambrian explosion (about 541 Ma; sudden appearance of most animal phyla, preceded by the Ediacaran biota). The Big Five mass extinctions with approximate dates and causes: end-Ordovician (445 Ma, glaciation), Late Devonian (372 Ma, ocean anoxia), end-Permian (252 Ma, the largest - about 96% of marine species; Siberian Traps volcanism, ocean anoxia, climate change), end-Triassic (201 Ma, CAMP volcanism), K-Pg (66 Ma - iridium anomaly, shocked quartz, Chicxulub impact crater; ammonites, belemnites, non-avian dinosaurs lost). Mass extinctions mark era boundaries and are used as correlation events.

Fossil identification is examined in Component 1 with specimens - know the diagnostic morphology of the classic index groups: graptolites (colonial, carbonised films - rhabdosome of parallel stipes with thecae; Ordovician-Silurian); trilobites (three-lobed arthropods, head/middle/tail shields, compound eyes; Palaeozoic); ammonites (planispirally coiled, chambered shells, complex suture lines where septa meet the shell wall; sexual dimorphism; Mesozoic); belemnites (bullet-shaped internal guards; Jurassic-Cretaceous); brachiopods (two-valved but unequal, symmetry plane through both valves; Palaeozoic-dominated). Microfossils serve industry: foraminifera and conodonts (tooth-like elements of an extinct chordate - Palaeozoic index fossils, used in borehole cuttings for hydrocarbon exploration); pollen and spores for Quaternary climate reconstruction (the Llyn Gwernan pollen diagram in the SAMs). Pair the fossil with its era and environment every time.

The geological time scale and British Earth history

The scale is hierarchical: eons (Hadean, Archaean, Proterozoic, Phanerozoic), eras within the Phanerozoic (Palaeozoic 541-252 Ma, Mesozoic 252-66 Ma, Cenozoic 66 Ma-present), periods (Cambrian, Ordovician, Silurian, Devonian, Carboniferous, Permian, Triassic, Jurassic, Cretaceous, Palaeogene, Neogene, Quaternary) and epochs (e.g. Pleistocene 2.58 Ma-11.7 ka, Holocene 11.7 ka-present). The Precambrian occupies about 88% of geological time - a standard one-mark fact. Boundaries are chosen at globally recognisable events (extinctions, reversals, isotopic excursions) and fixed by GSSPs (golden spikes).

Apply the scale synoptically to British geological evolution - the map-and-time combination the Eduqas themes examine:

  • Precambrian: Lewisian gneisses (NW Scotland, about 3.0-1.7 Ga), Torridonian sandstones, Dalradian sediments metamorphosed in the Grampian event.
  • Paleozoic: Iapetus Ocean closes - Caledonian orogeny (Ordovician-Silurian into Devonian, about 490-390 Ma) sutures Scotland to England (Iapetus suture), produces Dalradian/Southern Uplands deformation and the Devonian Old Red Sandstone continent; marine Carboniferous limestones and Coal Measures swamps; Variscan (Hercynian/Armorican) orogeny (about 380-290 Ma) folds SW England, intrudes Cornish granites (tin), forms the Hercynian unconformity.
  • Mesozoic: desert Triassic, marine Jurassic (ammonite-rich, Portland Stone, Kimmeridge Clay source rock), Chalk Cretaceous - the opening Atlantic and North Sea rifting control facies.
  • Cenozoic: Alpine orogeny folds and inverts southern England; Paleogene volcanism builds the Hebridean volcanoes and intrudes the Tertiary dyke swarms; Quaternary (2.58 Ma) glacial/interglacial cycles shaped the landscape - tills, moraines, periglacial deposits.

Epoch-level Quaternary detail (the Eduqas optional theme): Pleistocene glacial stages and interglacials paced by Milankovitch cycles (eccentricity about 100 ka, obliquity 41 ka, precession about 21 ka), evidenced by oxygen-isotope curves from deep-sea cores; Holocene post-glacial warming and sea-level rise. Exam technique: when a question names a period, attach the fossils, rocks and events - e.g. Jurassic = ammonites, marine mudrocks/limestones, Kimmeridge Clay. Cross-reference to the mapwork topic for how these events appear on maps (unconformities, orogenic fold trends).

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: A limestone overlies a slate. The slate is folded with slaty cleavage; the limestone is horizontal and contains abundant fossils. Describe the events implied, naming the type of unconformity, and explain how the limestone could be dated absolutely. [7]
Mark-scheme logic: Mark-scheme logic (AO1 + AO2; expect 5 marks events + 2 dating): Events (1 each, in order): (1) deposition of the original sediment (later slate) - fine-grained, argillaceous; (2) deformation - folding, with slaty cleavage formed by regional metamorphism perpendicular to compression; (3) uplift and erosion to a planar surface; (4) renewed subsidence and deposition of limestone on the erosion surface; (5) the contact is an angular unconformity - tilted/folded beds below, horizontal beds above. Name each principle: superposition (limestone younger), cross-cutting (cleavage older than the limestone it is truncated by). Dating (1 each): the limestone itself contains fossils - date bracketing by biostratigraphy, but for absolute dating (1) date the metamorphic micas in the slate by K-Ar/Ar-Ar (gives the age of cleavage formation, a maximum age for the unconformity) (1) or date an overlying volcanic ash/conglomerate clast by U-Pb/K-Ar (minimum age); the true age lies between. State assumptions: closed system, suitable half-life.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

State the principle of faunal succession and explain why ammonites are good index fossils. [4]
Answer: Principle (1): fossil organisms succeed one another in a definite, non-repeating vertical order, so assemblages order and correlate strata. Ammonites (1 each): abundant in Mesozoic marine rocks (1); rapidly evolving so each species/lineage has a short vertical range (1); widespread in marine facies and easy to identify from coiled, chambered shells with suture lines - enabling worldwide zonal correlation.
A granite contains 3.0% of its original K-40. Calculate its age in Ma (half-life 1.25 Ga) and state two assumptions of radiometric dating. [5]
Answer: Parent remaining 3% = (1/2)^n, so n = about 5.06 half-lives (1); age = 5.06 x 1.25 Ga = about 6.3 Ga (6300 Ma) (2 for correct working + answer). Note for evaluation: an age exceeding Earth age (4.567 Ga) would flag a disturbed system. Assumptions (1 each): closed system - no loss/gain of parent or daughter since crystallisation (1); initial daughter content zero or corrected (isochron) (1); half-life known and constant (1).
Distinguish between an angular unconformity and a disconformity, and state what each implies about geological history. [4]
Answer: Angular (1 each): beds below are tilted/folded at an angle to those above (1); implies deposition, deformation, uplift, erosion, subsidence and renewed deposition - orogeny before the break (1). Disconformity: beds parallel on both sides (1); erosion surface/relief or palaeosol with basal conglomerate; implies uplift/erosion or non-deposition without deformation (1).
Explain how the Rb-Sr isochron method overcomes the problem of initial daughter isotope content. [4]
Answer: Whole-rock samples from one co-magmatic body share the same initial 87Sr/86Sr ratio but different Rb/Sr (1). Plotting 87Sr/86Sr against 87Rb/86Sr gives a straight line - the isochron (1). The slope = e^(lambda t) - 1 gives the age; the intercept gives the initial 87Sr/86Sr, which is subtracted (1). Valid only if samples remained closed systems and were co-magmatic (1).
Name the era and period boundaries marked by the end-Permian and K-Pg events, and give one line of evidence for the K-Pg impact. [4]
Answer: End-Permian (1): boundary Palaeozoic-Mesozoic, about 252 Ma, largest mass extinction (about 96% marine species). K-Pg (1): boundary Mesozoic-Cenozoic, 66 Ma. Evidence (1 each): iridium-rich clay layer worldwide (1); shocked quartz, Chicxulub impact structure (Mexico), fern spike and abrupt ammonite/dinosaur loss (1).
