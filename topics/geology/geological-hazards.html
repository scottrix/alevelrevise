Geological Hazards

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level WJEC/Eduqas

Earthquakes, volcanoes, mass movements and tsunami - causes, prediction limits and mitigation with named case examples - Eduqas A-Level Geology geohazards theme revised with exam-style questions and full mark schemes.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain the elastic rebound theory, describe P, S and surface waves, and distinguish magnitude (Richter, moment magnitude) from intensity (Mercalli)
  • Relate eruption style to magma composition, viscosity and gas content, and classify volcano types with named examples
  • Assess slope stability in terms of shear stress versus shear strength, and classify mass movement types with their triggers
  • Explain tsunami generation, propagation and run-up, and evaluate warning systems
  • Evaluate why earthquake prediction has limited success but volcano forecasting succeeds, and compare mitigation strategies using case examples

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Focus (hypocentre): The point within the Earth where an earthquake rupture begins; the epicentre is the point on the surface directly above it.
Elastic rebound: The mechanism of earthquakes: elastic strain accumulates across a locked fault until it exceeds friction, rupture occurs and the rock rebounds, releasing energy as seismic waves.
Moment magnitude (Mw): Magnitude scale based on the seismic moment (rigidity x slip x ruptured area); logarithmic and accurate for large earthquakes, unlike the Richter (local) scale which saturates.
Mercalli intensity: A I-XII scale of earthquake effects observed at a location (damage, perception); intensity varies with distance, ground conditions and building quality, unlike magnitude.
Liquefaction: Loss of shear strength in water-saturated unconsolidated sediment during shaking, when grains temporarily float in pore water; buildings sink and sand volcanoes erupt.
Pyroclastic flow: A gravity-driven, incandescent density current of hot gas, ash and pumice moving at up to 200 km/h from a collapse of an eruption column or dome - the deadliest volcanic hazard.
Lahar: A volcanic mudflow/debris flow of ash and water, triggered by rainfall on fresh ash or melting snow/ice; can travel far beyond the volcano.
Volcanic Explosivity Index (VEI): A 0-8 logarithmic scale of eruption size based on ejecta volume and column height; each unit is about ten times more erupted volume.
Factor of safety: On a slope, the ratio of shear strength to shear stress; values above 1 are stable, below 1 the slope fails. Reduced by pore-water pressure, undercutting and loading.
Tsunami: A series of long-period sea waves generated by sudden vertical displacement of the sea floor (large shallow earthquakes, submarine landslides, volcanic collapse); wave speed c = sqrt(gd).

📌 Key Points

Wave properties: P fastest (push-pull, through liquids), S slower (shear, solids only), Love and Rayleigh surface waves slowest and most destructive of buildings.
Magnitude vs intensity: Mw measures energy at source (logarithmic); Mercalli I-XII measures effects that vary with distance and ground.
Eruption style follows viscosity: basaltic (low viscosity) = effusive fissure/Hawaiian; andesitic-rhyolitic (high viscosity, gas-rich) = plinian with pyroclastic flows.
Slump (rotational) vs slide (translational planar) vs flow: water pore pressure is the common trigger; factor of safety < 1 = failure.
Case pairs: Mt St Helens 1980 (lateral blast, VEI 5) vs Pinatubo 1991 (successful forecast); Indian Ocean 2004 (no warning system) vs Tohoku 2011 (Mw 9.0, warning but extreme run-up).

Earthquakes: causes, waves and measurement

Elastic rebound: strain accumulates across a locked fault segment until friction is overcome; rupture propagates and the stored elastic energy radiates as seismic waves from the focus. Depth classes: shallow (<70 km, most destructive), intermediate and deep (to 700 km, Benioff zones). Body waves: P (compressional, 6-8 km/s, through any material), S (shear, 3.5-4.5 km/s, solids only, more destructive); surface waves (Love - horizontal shearing; Rayleigh - rolling) travel slowest but shake buildings hardest. Liquefaction in saturated unconsolidated sediment amplifies damage - a standard secondary-hazard point alongside landslides, fires and tsunami.

Magnitude vs intensity - the classic discriminating question: moment magnitude (Mw) measures the seismic moment at source and is logarithmic (each unit = about 32 times more energy); the Richter (local) scale saturates above about M7. Mercalli intensity (I-XII) is observed damage at a location - it varies with distance, ground conditions and building quality, so one earthquake produces a range of intensities but one magnitude. Examiners want both scales contrasted, not confused.

Prediction and mitigation: earthquakes cannot be reliably predicted in time or size - the Eduqas mark scheme credits exactly this evaluation. Some success in location (known active faults, seismic gaps, palaeoseismology); monitoring uses background seismicity rates (increases or decreases as micro-fractures open and water influxes, with pore pressure rising to failure), P/S wave velocity anomalies through the fault zone, radon emission and ground tilt - but anomalies give false alarms and missed events. Mitigation is engineering-led: building codes (cross-bracing, reinforced frames, base isolation on rubber/steel bearings to absorb energy, low centre of gravity, symmetric design), safety cut-off valves on gas/water and reinforced tanks (preventing fire and flooding), early-warning systems (P-wave detection gives seconds before S-wave arrival - Japan), and land-use zoning away from fault traces. Case examples: Christchurch 2011 (M6.3, shallow focus directly under the city, liquefaction) versus Kobe 1995 (elevated highway collapse) - compare damage against building quality.

Locating an epicentre is a routine calculation: three or more seismograph stations measure the S-P arrival time difference; the distance to the focus comes from travel-time curves (S-P interval scales with distance); circles of those radii intersect at the epicentre. Depth classes matter for hazard: shallow (<70 km) causes most damage; deep Benioff-zone earthquakes cause less surface shaking. Aftershocks follow main ruptures for months (Christchurch 2011 followed the September 2010 Darfield event) and finish damaged buildings - a standard secondary-hazard point. Global distribution maps are examinable: belts of shallow foci along ridges and transforms, inclined Benioff zones under arcs, and diffuse continental zones (Himalaya, Mediterranean) - link each belt to its boundary type.

Volcanoes: eruption styles, types and forecasting

Eruption style is controlled by magma composition, viscosity and gas content - the core exam relationship: low-viscosity, low-gas basaltic magma degasses easily, giving effusive Hawaiian (shield volcanoes, lava fountains) and fissure eruptions (Laki 1783, Iceland - flood basalts, famine); higher-viscosity, gas-rich andesitic-rhyolitic magma retains pressure until catastrophic Vulcanian and Plinian eruptions (Vesuvius AD 79) with eruption columns and pyroclastic flows (Mont Pelee 1902 - peléan domes). Classify volcano types with the relationship: shield (basaltic, gentle slopes, non-explosive), stratovolcano/composite (alternating lava and pyroclastic layers, andesitic, steep, explosive), cinder/scoria cone (monogenetic), caldera (collapse after evacuation of a magma chamber - Yellowstone, Santorini), lava domes (acid, endogenous growth, collapse to pyroclastic flows).

Hazards ranked by deadliness: pyroclastic flows and surges (St Helens 1980), lahars (Nevado del Ruiz 1985 - 23,000 deaths in Armero), ash fall (Eyjafjallajokull 2010 - European airspace closure; respiratory and roof-collapse effects), lava flows (slow, property not life), jokulhlaups (glacial outburst floods where eruptions melt ice), toxic gas (CO2 limnic eruption, Lake Nyos 1986 - about 1,700 deaths; SO2 vog). Explosivity is ranked by VEI (0-8, logarithmic ejecta volume): St Helens VEI 5, Pinatubo VEI 6, Toba VEI 8.

Forecasting volcanoes works - contrast with earthquakes: eruptions are preceded by weeks-months of seismicity (harmonic tremor as magma moves), ground deformation (tiltmeters, EDM, GPS, InSAR - inflation of the edifice), gas monitoring (rising SO2 as magma nears surface) and thermal anomalies. Pinatubo 1991: monitoring identified the pre-eruption inflation and tremor, about 60,000 people evacuated, deaths limited to a few hundred; 20 Mt of SO2 caused global cooling of about 0.5 C for a year. St Helens 1980: flank bulge and earthquake swarm monitored, access zones reduced deaths to 57, but the lateral blast (from flank collapse) exceeded the designated zones. Vesuvius/Naples: forecasting is good but evacuation of up to a million people is the limiting factor - the classic exposure/vulnerability point for A* answers.

Eruption column dynamics explain pyroclastic flow formation: gas-rich magma fragments as it exits the vent; the column rises buoyantly while convecting, but if the eruption rate falls or the column density exceeds air, it collapses - pyroclastic density currents race down the flanks at up to 200 km/h. Plinian eruptions (Vesuvius AD 79 - Pliny the Younger described the pine-tree column; Herculaneum and Pompeii buried by ash and pyroclastic surges) are the textbook case. Temperatures: basaltic lava about 1,000-1,200 C (fluid, Pahoehoe and Aa flows); rhyolitic lava about 700-900 C (viscous domes). Link viscosity to silica: polymerised silica tetrahedra in acid melts resist flow - the chemistry-physics link examiners reward.

Mass movements and tsunami

Slope stability is a balance: shear strength (friction + cohesion of the material) against shear stress (weight of material on the slope). Factor of safety = shear strength / shear stress; failure below 1. Pore-water pressure from heavy rainfall is the common trigger (water reduces effective normal stress), alongside stream undercutting, earthquake shaking, and loading (building, tip waste). Lithology matters: bedding dipping out of the slope planes failure surfaces (Vaiont 1963); clays lose strength when remoulded; jointed chalk fails as blocks.

Classify mass movements by material and speed: falls (rockfall from cliffs - jointed limestone), slides - rotational slump (curved slip surface in clay/regolith, back-tilted blocks) and translational slide (planar surface - bedding, joint), flows (mudflow, debris flow, earthflow - saturated), and complex movements (rotational slump mobilising into a flow). UK case: Aberfan 1966 (coal-tip waste slid on saturated ground onto a school - 144 deaths; tipping practice and drainage) and coastal Folkestone Warren slumps. Vaiont 1963 (Italy): a 270 million m3 reservoir slope failed after rising water pore pressures, displacing the reservoir and overtopping the dam - about 2,000 deaths; the classic case of geological understanding (bedding dipping into the valley, clay layers) ignored. Mitigation: drainage (cut-off drains, adits - most effective and cheapest), slope reprofiling (reducing angle), pinning/bolting of jointed rock, retaining walls, grouting, toe loading, and hazard zoning/planning based on geological survey.

Tsunami: generated by sudden vertical displacement of the sea floor - large shallow earthquakes (Mw > 7 at <70 km depth on subduction megathrusts), submarine landslides and volcanic flank collapse. Propagation: a shallow-water wave with speed c = sqrt(gd) - about 200 m/s in 4 km deep ocean, slowing to tens of m/s onshore; wavelength hundreds of km, amplitude small at sea (<1 m) but the wave shoals and amplifies at the coast, with run-up to tens of metres. Case pair: Indian Ocean 2004 (Sumatra-Andaman Mw 9.1 - about 230,000 deaths; no regional warning system existed) versus Tohoku 2011 (Mw 9.0 megathrust - run-up to about 40 m, seawalls overtopped, Fukushima Daiichi meltdown; the Pacific warning system gave warnings but the magnitude and run-up exceeded design). Evaluation points: warnings work for distant tsunamis (hours of travel time) but near-field tsunamis arrive in minutes - hence natural warnings (ground shaking, sea withdrawal) and vertical evacuation are needed.

Landslide hazard mapping underpins mitigation: geological surveys map slopes by lithology, dip of bedding relative to slope, drainage and historic movement (BGS landslide databases); hazard zoning then restricts building on unstable ground. The Hong Kong landslides (Sau Mau Ping 1972 and 1976 - fill slopes failed in rainstorms, about 150 deaths) drove a systematic slope-safety programme: cut slopes engineered with drainage and soil nails, registration of every slope - the textbook success of mitigation after failure. Tsunami distance warning: distant tsunamis give hours of travel time (Pacific Tsunami Warning Centre from 1949), so the 2004 Indian Ocean disaster was principally a failure of warning infrastructure (no Indian Ocean system then) - one was installed afterwards; near-field tsunamis (Tohoku, minutes) need natural warnings and vertical evacuation.

Risk, prediction limits and mitigation strategy

Synoptic framing for the extended-response questions: risk = hazard x exposure x vulnerability (mitigated by capacity/resilience). Examiners reward candidates who separate what CAN be done (engineering, planning, warning systems) from what CANNOT (reliable earthquake prediction in time and size). The Eduqas mark scheme for earthquake prediction credits: limited success in predicting size/timing; some success in location; background seismicity rate anomalies; seismic gaps; P and S velocity changes through the fault zone; dilatancy models (micro-fractures open, water influx, pore pressure rises to failure); duration of anomaly related to magnitude. Quote these to show depth - then evaluate: anomalies give false positives and missed events, so prediction cannot yet drive evacuation policy.

Comparing case examples is the highest-tariff skill - practise three-way comparisons on: (1) prediction success: Pinatubo (evacuation before eruption) vs St Helens (zones, but exceeded by lateral blast) vs earthquakes (no prediction; Kobe 1995 sudden rupture); (2) mitigation effectiveness: building codes and base isolation (Christchurch 2011 vs Kobe 1995 highway collapse), lahar warning sirens (Ruiz failed - Armero), tsunami walls and education (Tohoku - overtopped but evacuation saved many lives); (3) exposure and vulnerability: Naples (good forecasting, impossible evacuation), Iceland (small exposed population, excellent monitoring). Structure extended answers: define the hazard mechanism, give the case with named facts (dates, magnitudes, VEI, deaths), then evaluate mitigation against prediction limits - a coherent line of reasoning with evidence is what QER marking rewards.

Case example quick facts to memorise

St Helens 18 May 1980, VEI 5, 57 deaths, lateral blast. Pinatubo 15 June 1991, VEI 6, 20 Mt SO2, about 0.5 C global cooling, 60,000 evacuated. Nevado del Ruiz 1985, lahar, 23,000 deaths. Lake Nyos 1986, CO2, about 1,700 deaths. Laki 1783, fissure, famine. Indian Ocean 2004, Mw 9.1, about 230,000 deaths. Tohoku 2011, Mw 9.0, run-up 40 m. Christchurch 2011, M6.3, liquefaction.

Hazard perception and capacity complete the risk equation: populations that have recently experienced a hazard (Japan, Chile) support strict building codes; hazard fatigue and short memories undermine compliance (Armero 1985 - warnings issued but not acted on). Economic development increases capacity to mitigate but also concentrates exposure (coastal megacities). For the QER, balance: engineering (codes, base isolation), planning (zoning on geological evidence), warning systems (volcanic monitoring; tsunami buoys; earthquake early warning) and education/drills - then judge which combination the named cases used, and what the residual risk was. This evaluative structure is what levels-based marking rewards above description.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: Compare the effectiveness of mitigation strategies for earthquakes and for volcanic eruptions, using named case examples. [8] (QER)
Mark-scheme logic: Mark-scheme logic (AO1 + AO2 + AO3 evaluation; levels-based QER marking): Earthquake mitigation (2-3 marks): cannot be predicted reliably, so mitigation is engineering-led: building codes with cross-braced reinforced frames (1); base isolation on rubber/steel bearings absorbs shaking energy (1); safety cut-off valves and reinforced tanks reduce fire/flooding; early warning from P-wave detection gives seconds (Japan) (1). Case: Christchurch 2011 M6.3 shallow under the city - liquefaction, but modern buildings survived; Kobe 1995 - elevated highway collapse showing older infrastructure vulnerability. Volcanic mitigation (2-3 marks): eruptions CAN be forecast - seismicity (harmonic tremor), deformation (tiltmeters, GPS, InSAR), gas monitoring (SO2), thermal (1). Pinatubo 1991: monitoring drove evacuation of about 60,000, limiting deaths (1); hazard zoning and lahar sirens. But exposure limits mitigation: Vesuvius/Naples - evacuation of a million people impractical. Evaluation (2-3 marks for a coherent line of reasoning): volcanic mitigation is more effective because precursor warning exists and evacuation is possible when population is small (Iceland, Pinatubo); earthquake mitigation relies on continuous engineering resilience because prediction fails; effectiveness depends on exposure x vulnerability and the capacity to act on warnings - deaths in Armero (Ruiz 1985) show warnings without response capacity fail. Named examples with dates/figures required throughout.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Distinguish between magnitude and intensity of an earthquake. [3]
Answer: Magnitude (1): measures energy released at source - logarithmic scales (Richter/local ML; moment magnitude Mw based on seismic moment, accurate for large quakes). One earthquake has ONE magnitude (1). Intensity (1): observed effects at a location on the Mercalli I-XII scale - varies with distance from epicentre, ground conditions (soft sediment amplifies) and building quality; one earthquake gives a range of intensities.
Explain why basaltic volcanism is usually effusive but andesitic volcanism is explosive. [6]
Answer: Basaltic (1 each): low silica so low viscosity (1); gas escapes easily, so pressure does not build (1); effusive Hawaiian/fissure eruptions, shield volcanoes, lava flows. Andesitic (1 each): higher silica (plus dissolved water) so high viscosity (1); gas retained until pressure is catastrophic - Vulcanian/Plinian eruptions with eruption columns and pyroclastic flows (1); stratovolcanoes, e.g. Vesuvius. Credit explicit viscosity-gas reasoning and named examples.
Explain how a tsunami is generated and why it grows in height as it approaches the coast. [6]
Answer: Generation (1 each): sudden vertical displacement of the sea floor by a large shallow earthquake (Mw > 7 at < 70 km depth on a megathrust), submarine landslide or volcanic collapse (1); the displaced water column propagates as a series of long-period waves, speed c = sqrt(gd), about 200 m/s in 4 km ocean depth (1). Growth at the coast (1 each): the wave slows in shallow water (c decreases as d decreases) (1); energy conserved, so amplitude increases - the wave shoals and stacks up (1); run-up to tens of metres depending on coastal shape; trough arrives first in places (sea withdrawal as a natural warning).
Assess the factors that reduced the death toll at Pinatubo (1991) compared with Nevado del Ruiz (1985). [6]
Answer: Pinatubo (1 each): intense precursor monitoring - seismicity/harmonic tremor, ground inflation (tiltmeters, GPS), rising SO2 (1); warnings heeded, about 60,000 evacuated before the 15 June eruption (1); local capacity and access zones. Ruiz (1 each): lahar generated by minor eruption melting summit ice (1); warning sirens existed but were not activated/heard and Armero's population was not evacuated (1); failure of response capacity, not prediction - 23,000 deaths. Evaluation (1): death toll difference reflects response capacity and warning dissemination, showing risk = hazard x exposure x vulnerability.
Explain how pore-water pressure influences slope failure, with reference to one named mass movement. [4]
Answer: Factor of safety = shear strength / shear stress; failure below 1 (1). Pore-water pressure from heavy rainfall or reservoir filling reduces effective normal stress between grains, lowering shear strength (1); saturated material also adds weight, increasing shear stress (1). Named case (1): Vaiont 1963 - rising reservoir levels raised pore pressures in strata dipping into the valley; 270 million m3 slid, overtopping the dam (about 2,000 deaths). Accept Aberfan (saturated coal-tip waste) or Folkestone Warren.
