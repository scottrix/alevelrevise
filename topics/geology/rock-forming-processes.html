Rock Forming Processes

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level WJEC/Eduqas

Igneous, sedimentary and metamorphic processes, rock classification, textures and the rock cycle - Eduqas A-Level Geology core (elements, minerals, rocks; rock forming processes; rock cycle) revised for exam performance.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Apply Bowen's reaction series and fractional crystallisation to explain the origin of the main igneous rock compositions
  • Classify igneous, sedimentary and metamorphic rocks from composition, grain size and texture, justifying each identification
  • Sequence the sedimentary processes of weathering, erosion, transport, deposition and lithification, and infer depositional environments from sedimentary structures and facies
  • Distinguish contact, dynamic and regional metamorphism, quoting index minerals, facies and fabrics for each
  • Use mineral stability series (Bowen, Goldich) to explain mineral occurrence and susceptibility to weathering
  • Synthesise the rock cycle by linking each rock type to its plate tectonic setting

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Bowen's reaction series: The order in which minerals crystallise from a cooling basic melt: discontinuous ferromagnesian series (olivine-pyroxene-amphibole-biotite), continuous plagioclase series (calcic to sodic), then late residual minerals (orthoclase, muscovite, quartz).
Fractional crystallisation: The separation of early-formed crystals (e.g. by crystal settling or filter pressing) from the remaining melt, so the residual liquid evolves to a more silica-rich composition.
Lithification (diagenesis): The conversion of sediment to sedimentary rock by compaction, dewatering and cementation by calcite, silica or iron oxides.
Facies: The total character of a rock reflecting its formative environment (sedimentary facies, e.g. fluvial, aeolian, reef) or its pressure-temperature conditions (metamorphic facies, e.g. greenschist, amphibolite).
Graded bedding: A bed in which grain size decreases upwards, formed by rapid deposition from a decelerating density (turbidity) current; indicates the way-up of the bed.
Oolite: A limestone composed of ooids - concentrically coated carbonate grains precipitated in warm, shallow, agitated water.
Slaty cleavage: A low-grade regional metamorphic fabric of closely spaced parallel planes formed by alignment of platy minerals perpendicular to the maximum compressive stress.
Index mineral: A mineral whose presence brackets a range of metamorphic grade (chlorite, biotite, garnet, staurolite, kyanite, sillimanite in the Barrovian regional sequence).
Porphyroblast: A large metamorphic crystal (e.g. garnet, staurolite) grown within a finer matrix during metamorphism - the metamorphic analogue of a phenocryst.
Porphyritic texture: An igneous texture of large phenocrysts in a finer groundmass, recording two stages of cooling (slow at depth, then rapid on eruption or emplacement).

📌 Key Points

Silica classification used by Eduqas: ultrabasic <45%, basic 45-52%, intermediate 52-66%, acid >66% SiO2 (synonyms ultramafic/mafic/intermediate/felsic).
Two-stage cooling gives porphyritic texture; dykes have fine chilled margins, sills bake the country rock above and below.
Way-up indicators: graded bedding, cross-bedding, ripple marks, mudcracks, sole structures.
Barrovian sequence: slate-phyllite-schist-gneiss with chlorite, biotite, garnet, staurolite, kyanite, sillimanite index minerals.
Goldich stability series is the inverse of Bowen's: olivine weathers first, quartz survives longest.

Igneous processes and rock classification (Eduqas core: rock forming processes)

Magma originates by partial melting of mantle peridotite (decompression at ridges and hotspots; flux melting by water from slabs at destructive margins) and of lower continental crust. Evolution of composition is examined through Bowen's reaction series: as a basic melt cools, olivine, then pyroxene, amphibole and biotite form by reaction with the melt (discontinuous series) while plagioclase changes continuously from calcic (anorthite) to sodic (albite); the late residual melt crystallises orthoclase, muscovite and quartz. Fractional crystallisation - crystal settling of dense early minerals, filter pressing, or sidewall accretion - removes early crystals from contact with the melt so the residual liquid becomes progressively more silica-rich, explaining suites from gabbro to granite in one pluton and layered basic intrusions (e.g. the Skaergaard intrusion, Greenland). Assimilation of country rock and magma mixing add further variety.

Classification is by silica content and texture, and examiners require justification of both. Eduqas uses ultrabasic (under 45% SiO2), basic (45-52%), intermediate (52-66%), acid (over 66%). Grain size reflects cooling rate: coarse-grained (phaneritic) plutonic rocks cool slowly at depth (granite, diorite, gabbro, peridotite); fine-grained (aphanitic) volcanic rocks cool rapidly at the surface (rhyolite, andesite, basalt). Textures carry the interpretation marks: porphyritic (two-stage cooling), vesicular (gas escape - basalt, pumice), glassy (obsidian, chilled too fast for crystals), pyroclastic (fragmental - tuff, agglomerate from explosive eruption), plus flow banding and amygdaloidal (vesicles later infilled). For minor intrusions know dykes (discordant, fine chilled margins), sills (concordant, baked country rock above and below, e.g. the Whin Sill) and the field evidence distinguishing a sill from a lava flow (baked contacts on both surfaces, no weathered top, feeder relationships).

Tie composition to tectonic setting - the synoptic link examiners test: tholeiitic basalts (low K) dominate ridges; alkali basalts (higher K, Na) characterise hotspots and continental rifts; andesites mark subduction zones; rhyolites come from crustal melting above subducting slabs or fractionated continental magma. Crystal settling is directly observable: layered basic intrusions show graded modal layers and cumulate textures (e.g. the Bushveld Complex) - the same deposits exploited for chromite and platinum (see the Geological Resources topic). Magma stoping (blocks of country rock sinking into the magma) explains xenoliths and further contamination; chilled marginal facies mark the original magma composition before fractionation.

Sedimentary processes, structures and facies

Revise the process chain in order and attach products to each stage: weathering (physical - freeze-thaw, exfoliation, salt weathering; chemical - hydrolysis of feldspars to clay, carbonation of limestone, oxidation; biological - root wedging, chelation) produces regolith and detritus; erosion detaches and removes it; transport by traction (bedload rolling), saltation (bouncing sand), suspension (silt and clay) and solution sorts the load - wind and water sort better than glaciers, so sorting and rounding record transport history and textural maturity; deposition occurs when energy falls below threshold; lithification by compaction, dewatering and cementation (calcite, silica, iron oxide cements) converts sediment to rock.

Classification of clastics by grain size (Wentworth): rudaceous (over 2 mm - conglomerate rounded, breccia angular), arenaceous (2-0.063 mm - sandstone: quartz sandstone, arkose, greywacke), argillaceous (under 0.063 mm - mudstone, shale with fissility from compaction-aligned micas). Carbonates: classify by texture - micrite (lime mud), oolite, calcirudite/shell gravel, bioclastic limestone, chalk (coccoliths), reef limestone (coral, in situ framework). Chemical sediments: evaporites (halite, gypsum - precipitated by evaporation in restricted basins) and chert (silica from radiolaria/sponges). Note the carbonate compensation depth: below about 4-5 km carbonate dissolves faster than it is supplied, so deep-ocean sediments are siliceous or clay - a routine AO2 point.

Sedimentary structures are the way-up and environment toolkit: graded bedding (turbidity currents; youngs upwards), cross-bedding (dune/ripple migration in rivers, deltas, deserts; tops truncated), ripple marks (asymmetric current ripples, symmetric wave ripples), mudcracks (desiccation - exposure), flute and load casts on sole surfaces. Facies analysis links assemblage to environment: red beds + cross-bedding = desert or river; graded greywackes + sole marks = deep marine turbidites; coal + rootlets + seat earth = swamp; oolite + marine fossils = warm shallow shelf. Exam questions give a description or specimen and expect a justified environment with evidence.

Extend the facies analysis to basin-scale patterns: facies belts parallel the shoreline (shoreface sandstone - offshore mudstone - deep-water turbidite), so lateral facies changes record the depth gradient; transgression (sea-level rise) shifts facies belts landward (deepening-upward sequence), regression the reverse. Carbonate settings build a Darwin-style reef sequence: fringing reef, then barrier reef, then atoll as the volcano subsides. Evaporite cycles (restricted basins: Zechstein salt) show repeated carbonate-sulfate-halite bands as salinity rises. Red beds record oxidising terrestrial palaeoclimates - another palaeoclimate pointer examined in the Quaternary theme. Exam questions may supply a log or map of facies belts: infer the environment from the assemblage, then state whether sea level rose or fell from the vertical trend.

Chemical classification details that earn marks: silica content controls mineralogy - basic rocks are dominated by calcic plagioclase and pyroxene/olivine (no quartz); acid rocks by sodic plagioclase, alkali feldspar, mica and quartz (no olivine); intermediate rocks by zoned plagioclase, hornblende and biotite. Colour index (percentage of dark minerals) tracks silica inversely - a hand-specimen clue for classification. Volcanic equivalents of ultrabasic rocks (komatiites) are almost entirely Archaean, recording hotter Precambrian mantle. When justifying an identification, quote TWO observed features plus the inference: e.g. visible quartz + alkali feldspar = granite (acid, plutonic); vesicles + fine grain + pyroxene = basalt (basic, volcanic). Rote naming without features scores half.

Metamorphic processes, grades and rocks

Metamorphism changes rocks in the solid state under heat, pressure/stress and chemically active fluids (water carrying ions in solution). Learn the three types with diagnostic products:

  • Contact (thermal) metamorphism: baked margin (aureole) around an intrusion; high temperature, low pressure, no foliation. Products: hornfels, spotted slate, marble (from limestone), quartzite (from sandstone); aureole zones narrow outwards from the contact.
  • Dynamic (dislocation) metamorphism: intense directed stress along fault zones; products cataclasite (fragmented) and mylonite (sheared, fine, banded).
  • Regional metamorphism: orogenic belts, combined heat and stress. Barrovian sequence in pelitic rocks: slate (chlorite) - phyllite (biotite; silky sheen) - schist (garnet, staurolite, kyanite; visible micas) - gneiss (banding; migmatite at highest grade). Index minerals track increasing grade; isograds can be mapped across an orogen (e.g. the Dalradian of Scotland).

Fabrics: foliation records the compressive stress direction - slaty cleavage (aligned chlorite/mica, perpendicular to compression, commonly oblique to bedding), schistosity (coarser aligned micas), gneissose banding (segregation of light and dark minerals). Distinguish cleavage from bedding in the field (cleavage cuts across bedding) and use this in fold analysis. Lineation and stretched pebbles or fossils record the strain direction. Reactions are prograde with dehydration (clay + quartz gives mica + water); retrograde alteration follows if fluids return during cooling. Metamorphic facies (zeolite, greenschist, amphibolite, granulite, hornfels, blueschist) classify P-T fields - glaucophane-bearing blueschist identifies the high-pressure, low-temperature conditions of subduction zones, a classic applied point.

Thermal and regional metamorphism sit on different P-T paths, and examiners test the discrimination: contact metamorphism is high-T/low-P (hornfels facies; no directed stress, hence no foliation); regional metamorphism combines both (Barrovian facies series). Real aureoles are zoned - the Comrie aureole (Scotland) shows spotted slates grading outwards from hornfels at the contact; Skiddaw shows knotted micaschists around the granite. Impure limestones give skarns (calc-silicate minerals - diopside, wollastonite) where hot fluids react with the carbonate; pure limestone gives marble. Burial metamorphism (low grade, no stress) affects thick sedimentary or volcanic piles - zeolite minerals in deeply buried basalt. Quote these when justifying a metamorphic rock identification: mineral + fabric + inferred P-T conditions.

The rock cycle and mineral stability

Synoptic skill: place every rock in the cycle and its tectonic setting. Igneous rocks form from melt at ridges (basalt), hotspots (flood basalts), subduction zones (andesite, granite) and within plates (minor intrusions). Uplift exposes them to weathering and erosion, producing sediments deposited in environments from desert to deep sea; burial lithifies them into sedimentary rocks. Burial plus heat and stress, or contact with intrusions, produces metamorphic rocks; further heating causes partial melting and the cycle restarts. Exam questions ask you to construct or complete such pathways, so practise writing gabbro-to-migmatite and sandstone-to-gneiss chains with the process named at each arrow.

Mineral stability unites the topic. The Goldich stability series for weathering is essentially the inverse of Bowen's series: minerals crystallising at highest temperature (olivine, calcic plagioclase) are least stable at surface conditions and weather first, while quartz survives to form mature sandstones - explaining why beach sands are quartz-rich and why basic igneous rocks weather to clay faster than granites. The same logic explains residual deposits (bauxite from intense leaching). In metamorphism, stability fields (index minerals, facies) map P-T conditions; in igneous systems, Bowen's series maps crystallisation order. Explaining why each series runs as it does - mineral structures in equilibrium with their environment - earns the evaluation marks.

Specimen-identification routine for Component 1

Work in order: (1) crystalline or clastic? (2) grain size and alignment (equigranular, porphyritic, vesicular, foliated, fossiliferous)? (3) composition tests (hardness, acid reaction on calcite, streak). Name the rock, then justify with two observed features and state the process and setting. Answers justified from observations score; rote names alone do not.

Synthesis drill: the complete cycle on one diagram

Draw the cycle with three boxes (igneous, sedimentary, metamorphic) and label every arrow with a process AND a tectonic setting: melt generation (decompression at ridges; flux melting at slabs), cooling/crystallisation, uplift, weathering/erosion, transport, deposition, lithification (burial), burial metamorphism, contact metamorphism (intrusions), regional metamorphism (orogeny), partial melting. Examiners award the process label, not just the arrow - a bare diagram of boxes scores little.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: Describe and explain how fractional crystallisation can produce a range of igneous rock compositions from a single basic magma. [6]
Mark-scheme logic: Mark-scheme logic (AO1 + AO2; QER coherence expected): Describe (1 each): early-formed ferromagnesian crystals (olivine, pyroxene) and calcic plagioclase settle out of the melt (crystal settling) or are trapped by filter pressing/sidewall crystallisation (1); the residual liquid, now isolated, is progressively enriched in silica and volatiles (1); successive liquids crystallise as progressively more acid rocks - gabbro, diorite, granite in one intrusion (1). Explain (1 each): Bowen's reaction series - minerals crystallise in fixed order and react with the melt; early crystals are dense and sink, physically removing their components from the liquid (1); removal of Mg, Fe and Ca leaves the melt enriched in Na, K and SiO2, so later minerals are orthoclase, muscovite, quartz (1). Applied credit (1): cite evidence - layered intrusions such as Skaergaard, or compositional zoning of a pluton (basic margins, acid core). Full marks require linked description-explanation, not a list.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Explain the differences between a sill and a lava flow, as seen in the field. [4]
Answer: Sill (1 each): concordant sheet intruded within country rock (1); baked margins above AND below (1); no weathered upper surface or soil horizon; may feed dykes, no flow top. Lava flow: erupted at surface, baked base only (1); weathered top surface with soil/palaeokarst; vesicular or scoriaceous top, may show columnar joints and flow structures. Credit any two contrasts with evidence.
A limestone contains ooids, shell fragments and no clay. State the rock name and the depositional environment, with evidence. [3]
Answer: Oolitic limestone (oolite) (1). Environment: warm, shallow, agitated marine shelf (1). Evidence (1): ooids precipitate as concentric coatings in shallow warm water moved by currents/waves; broken shells show current reworking; absence of clay indicates low terrigenous input, clear water.
Explain how the index minerals of the Barrovian sequence are used to map metamorphic grade across an orogenic belt. [6]
Answer: Sequence and minerals (1 each): chlorite, biotite, garnet, staurolite, kyanite, sillimanite in pelitic rocks (need the order). Each mineral first appears at a specific P-T threshold (1). Lines joining first appearances = isograds (1). Mapping isograds across the belt shows increasing grade towards the core/heat source, e.g. garnet zone flanking staurolite and kyanite zones (1). Highest grade (gneiss/migmatite) in the core where temperatures were greatest (1). Application: gradient of metamorphism indicates the thermal structure of the orogeny.
Explain why graded bedding is a way-up indicator, and name two other way-up structures. [3]
Answer: Graded bedding: deposition from a decelerating density current drops the coarsest grains first, so grain size decreases upwards - the top of the bed is the youngest, coarsest-at-base shows which way was up (1). Others (any 2 of 1 each): cross-bedding (tops truncated), ripple marks (crests point up), mudcracks (open upwards), flute casts (bulge into overlying bed).
Account for the fact that beach sands are commonly almost pure quartz. [4]
Answer: Quartz is the end-product of Bowen's residual crystallisation and is very stable at surface conditions (Goldich series) (1). Less stable minerals (olivine, pyroxene, feldspar) weather chemically during transport (hydrolysis to clay, oxidation) (1). Repeated cycles of erosion, transport and deposition (textural maturity) remove the clays and unstable grains (1); quartz survives as sand-sized, well-rounded, well-sorted grains (1).
