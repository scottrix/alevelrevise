Earth Structure and Plate Tectonics

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level WJEC/Eduqas

Seismic evidence for Earth's layered structure, plate boundary processes, palaeomagnetism and seafloor spreading - core Eduqas A-Level Geology (Earth structure and global tectonics) revised with exam-style questions and mark-scheme logic.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Describe the compositional layers of the Earth (crust, mantle, core) and the mechanical layers (lithosphere, asthenosphere), quoting depths, densities and compositions
  • Explain how P-wave and S-wave behaviour, travel-time curves and shadow zones provide evidence for the state of the core and the position of the Moho and Gutenberg discontinuities
  • Compare the processes and products at divergent, convergent (both ocean-ocean and ocean-continent) and conservative plate boundaries
  • Explain the palaeomagnetic evidence, including magnetic anomaly stripes and apparent polar wander curves, that supports seafloor spreading
  • Calculate spreading rates from magnetic anomaly distances and crustal thickness from seismic refraction data
  • Evaluate slab pull, ridge push and mantle convection as driving mechanisms, and sequence the stages of the Wilson cycle

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Lithosphere: The rigid outer shell of the Earth comprising the crust and the uppermost mantle, roughly 100 km thick (thicker beneath cratons), broken into the tectonic plates.
Asthenosphere: The mechanically weak, partially molten zone in the upper mantle beneath the lithosphere (roughly 100-350 km depth) on which the plates move.
Mohorovicic discontinuity (Moho): The seismic velocity boundary between crust and mantle, where P-wave velocities jump from about 6-7 km/s to about 8 km/s; about 7 km deep beneath oceans, 30-70 km beneath continents.
Gutenberg discontinuity: The boundary between the mantle and the liquid outer core at about 2890 km depth, marked by the disappearance of S waves and a sharp drop in P-wave velocity.
Shadow zone: The belt 105-140 degrees from an earthquake focus where direct P waves are refracted away by the core and S waves are absent, because S waves cannot travel through liquid.
Benioff zone: An inclined zone of earthquake foci, dipping from the trench down to about 700 km, tracing the descending oceanic slab at a destructive margin.
Palaeomagnetism: The record of the Earth's past magnetic field preserved as aligned magnetic minerals (e.g. magnetite) cooling through their Curie point in igneous rocks.
Magnetic reversal (chron): A period during which the Earth's magnetic field polarity was reversed relative to the present; reversals are irregular in timing, the last (Brunhes-Matuyama) at about 780,000 years ago.
Seafloor spreading: The generation of new oceanic lithosphere at mid-ocean ridges by upwelling partial melting, producing symmetrical magnetic anomaly stripes that record reversals as the plates diverge.
Slab pull: The gravitational force exerted by the dense, cold subducting slab on the rest of the plate; widely regarded as the dominant plate-driving mechanism.

📌 Key Points

Oceanic crust: about 7 km thick, basaltic/gabbroic, density about 3.0 g/cm3; continental crust: 30-70 km, granitic, about 2.7 g/cm3; mantle peridotite about 3.3 g/cm3; core iron-nickel 10-13 g/cm3.
S-wave shadow beyond 105 degrees plus P-wave shadow 105-140 degrees = liquid outer core. Moho and Gutenberg located from velocity discontinuities.
Vine-Matthews-Morley: symmetrical normal/reversed magnetic stripes parallel to ridges prove ocean floor is created at the ridge and destroyed at trenches.
Age, sediment thickness and water depth all increase away from the ridge axis.
Slab pull dominates; ridge push and basal shear are secondary. Hotspot chains (Hawaii-Emperor) record plate motion over a fixed plume.

Layered Earth and the seismic evidence (Eduqas core: Earth structure and global tectonics)

Exams expect two parallel classifications of the interior. Compositional layers: the crust (oceanic about 7 km thick, basaltic and gabbroic, density about 3.0 g/cm3; continental 30-70 km, granitic in composition, about 2.7 g/cm3), the mantle (peridotite, about 3.3 g/cm3, extending to the Gutenberg discontinuity at 2890 km) and the core (iron-nickel, 10-13 g/cm3; liquid outer core 2890-5150 km, solid inner core 5150-6371 km, separated by the Lehmann discontinuity). Mechanical layers: the rigid lithosphere (crust plus uppermost mantle, about 100 km) rides on the weak asthenosphere, which is why plates can move.

The evidence is seismic and you must be able to link each observation to an inference:

  • P waves (primary, compressional) travel fastest (6-8 km/s in crust), pass through solids and liquids, and are refracted at velocity boundaries.
  • S waves (secondary, shear) travel at 3.5-4.5 km/s, are slower, more destructive and cannot pass through liquid.
  • S waves are absent beyond about 105 degrees from the focus: the outer core is liquid.
  • Direct P waves are absent in the P-wave shadow zone (105-140 degrees) because they are sharply refracted downwards on entering the lower-velocity core.
  • Velocity jumps at the Moho (6-7 to 8 km/s) and the drop at Gutenberg locate those boundaries; travel-time curves of refracted and direct arrivals allow crustal thickness to be calculated.

Know the refraction calculation used in Component 3 papers: H = (D/2) x sqrt[(V2-V1)/(V2+V1)], where H is crustal thickness, D the crossover distance of the two travel-time lines, V1 and V2 the P-wave velocities in crust and mantle. Practise substituting values (e.g. D = 100 km, V1 = 6.5, V2 = 8.0 km/s gives H of about 20 km) and state assumptions: layers are homogeneous and horizontal.

Exam trap: Moho depth varies

Never quote a single Moho depth. Say about 7 km beneath oceans and 30-70 km beneath continents, thickening under mountain belts (up to 70 km under the Himalaya) because of crustal thickening during orogeny.

Interpreting travel-time curves is a routine numerical skill: the direct wave plots as one straight line of slope 1/V1; the refracted wave (critically refracted along the Moho) plots as a second, shallower line of slope 1/V2 that overtakes the direct at the crossover distance D. Beyond D the refracted arrival arrives first. Deeper crust shifts D to greater distances. Isostasy explains the thick crust of mountain belts: the low-density crust floats on the denser mantle like an iceberg (Airy model), so elevated belts have deep roots of up to 70 km and gravity measurements are close to zero over them (isostatic compensation).

Travel-time drill

Two lines on a travel-time graph: steep line = direct wave in the crust (slower); shallow line = refracted wave along the Moho (faster, arrives first at distance). Crossover distance D increases with crustal thickness. Quote velocities: about 6-7 km/s crust, about 8 km/s mantle.

Mechanical vs compositional distinction is itself examinable: the lithosphere INCLUDES the uppermost mantle (it is not just crust) and behaves rigidly; the asthenosphere is solid but weak (near its melting point), flowing slowly by solid-state creep - allowing plate motion. Seismic velocities decrease in the asthenosphere (the low-velocity zone) - direct evidence for its weakness. State this distinction precisely: questions ask why plates are defined mechanically, not compositionally, and the low-velocity zone is the evidence.

Plate boundaries and tectonic processes

Learn the three boundary types as process + product + example triplets, because compare-and-contrast questions are routine:

BoundaryProcessesProductsExample
Divergent (constructive)Decompression partial melting of upwelling peridotite; dyke injection; normal faultingMid-ocean ridge, axial rift valley, pillow basalts, sheeted dykes, shallow earthquakes (less than 30 km), black smoker VMS depositsMid-Atlantic Ridge; Iceland (ridge above hotspot)
Convergent (destructive)Subduction; flux melting as water leaves the slab; accretionary prism construction; partial melting of crustTrench, Benioff zone (foci to 700 km), island arc or Andean arc, andesitic-rhyolitic volcanism, granite plutons, back-arc basinJapan and Mariana (ocean-ocean); Andes (ocean-continent)
Conservative (passive)Strike-slip shear; no creation or destruction of lithosphereFault-line valleys, offset ridge segments, shallow but powerful earthquakes, no volcanismSan Andreas Fault; Alpine Fault, New Zealand

Continent-continent collision (Himalaya, from closure of the Tethys Ocean) produces no subduction of continental material (too buoyant): instead crust doubles in thickness, generating regional metamorphism, granites and the highest seismicity on Earth. Distinguish island arcs (oceanic crust subducting beneath oceanic crust: thin, basaltic-andesitic, e.g. Aleutians) from marginal (Andean) arcs (thicker crust, large granodiorite plutons). SAMs questions ask you to judge whether arc plutons are likely to be intermediate - argue from magma contamination by continental crust versus crustal thickness, and note the limitation: pluton composition alone cannot prove arc type.

Why deep foci only occur at Benioff zones

Rocks at 300-700 km depth in normal mantle are too hot and ductile to store elastic strain, so earthquakes only occur where cold, brittle oceanic slab is carried to that depth - diagnostic evidence for subduction.

Refine the margin comparison further: back-arc basins form by extension behind island arcs (Sea of Japan, Lau Basin) - spreading behind subduction, with its own ridge and magnetic stripes. Subduction polarity controls margin type: steep slab dip (old, cold ocean floor - Marianas) gives oceanic island arcs; shallow dip (young, buoyant slab - Chile) gives marginal Andean arcs and shallow seismicity. Transform faults offset ridge segments into short spreading cells - the zig-zag ridge pattern of the Atlantic - and their first-motion studies confirm strike-slip sense. Cite these when comparing margins: the full evidence chain (foci distribution, volcanism, heat flow, magnetic stripes) is what earns A* credit.

Palaeomagnetism and seafloor spreading

Igneous minerals such as magnetite align with the ambient field as they cool through the Curie point (about 580 C for magnetite), then freeze the record in place - this is palaeomagnetism. Two independent lines of evidence underpin plate tectonics:

  1. Magnetic anomaly stripes: Vine, Matthews and Morley (1963) showed that the ocean floor carries symmetrical bands of alternately normal and reversed polarity parallel to ridge axes. Because reversals are global and time-parallel, symmetrical stripes can only be explained by new crust forming at the ridge and moving outwards symmetrically. This is the classic evidence for seafloor spreading - learn to argue it, not just state it.
  2. Apparent polar wander curves: palaeomagnetic inclination fixes palaeolatitude; declination fixes the palaeomeridian. Rocks of different ages on the same continent give different pole positions, tracing a wander path; different continents give divergent paths, proving the continents (not the poles) have moved.

Supporting observations all co-vary with distance from the ridge and should be quoted together: age of the crust (oldest ocean floor only about 200 Ma, Jurassic), sediment thickness (zero at the axis, increasing outwards), water depth (shallowest at the hot, buoyant ridge, deepening with age as the plate cools and contracts), and heat flow (highest at the axis).

Spreading rate calculation: measure the distance from the ridge axis to a dated anomaly (e.g. the 780 ka Brunhes-Matuyama reversal), then rate = distance / time. Example: an anomaly 30 km from the axis gives 30,000 m / 780,000 yr = about 3.8 cm/yr. State whether you quote the half-rate (one flank) or full rate (both flanks). Atlantic full rates are about 2-4 cm/yr; the East Pacific Rise reaches about 15-18 cm/yr. Fast-spreading ridges are smoother and topographically subdued; slow ridges have deep axial rift valleys.

The field evidence for spreading strengthens into a complete case: palaeomagnetic measurements (declination and inclination) are made with a magnetometer on oriented cores; averaging many samples removes secular variation. The geomagnetic polarity time scale is built by tying anomaly patterns to radiometrically dated ash horizons and basalt flows, converting the stripes into a dated reversal sequence (the current normal chron, Brunhes, spans about 780 ka). The geodynamo itself operates in the liquid outer core - convecting iron generates the field - so the same seismic evidence that proved the liquid core underpins palaeomagnetism. Tomographic imaging of slabs (cold, fast seismic anomalies descending into the mantle) provides direct confirmation of subduction.

Driving mechanisms, hotspots and the Wilson cycle

A* answers evaluate mechanisms rather than list them. Slab pull is now regarded as dominant: the cold subducting slab is denser than the surrounding asthenosphere and drags the trailing plate; it explains why plates with long subducting margins (Pacific) move fastest. Ridge push contributes: elevated, buoyant ridge lithosphere slides away on the asthenosphere. Basal shear from mantle convection drags the plate base but is too weak alone. Hotspots are not plate-boundary features: fixed mantle plumes build trails of volcanoes whose ages increase along the chain - the Hawaii-Emperor chain youngs towards the present island, with a bend at about 47 Ma recording a change in Pacific plate motion.

The Wilson cycle sequences ocean basin evolution and is a reliable essay framework:

  1. Embryonic - continental rifting, fault-block grabens, volcanism (East African Rift).
  2. Juvenile - narrow linear sea, oceanic crust formed (Red Sea).
  3. Mature - wide ocean with mid-ocean ridge and passive margins (Atlantic).
  4. Declining - subduction begins, trenches and volcanic arcs (Pacific).
  5. Terminal - closing sea, collision imminent (Mediterranean).
  6. Suturing - collision, orogenic belt and suture zone (Himalaya; Iapetus suture in Scotland), after which the cycle may restart by rifting.

Link each stage to its rocks: rifting gives continental flood basalts and red beds; mature oceans give ophiolite sequences eventually obducted onto margins; collision gives regional metamorphic belts and granites. Examiners reward tying structure, process and rock type into one line of reasoning - the Quality of Extended Response (QER) marking in Component 2 rewards exactly this coherence.

Measure plate motion directly and historically: GPS networks record present rates (cm/yr) that match the magnetic-anomaly rates; hotspot chains record motion over tens of millions of years. In extended-response answers, combine evidence scales: present GPS rates + anomaly-derived average rates + palaeomagnetic polar wander = a coherent case that plates move and the mechanisms are gravity-driven. When sequencing a Wilson cycle, attach rates: rifting (East African Rift, a few mm/yr), mature spreading (Atlantic 2-4 cm/yr), fast closing basins (Pacific up to 15-18 cm/yr) - and note the Himalaya sutures at similar rates, closing the Tethys.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: With reference to the distribution of earthquake foci and the behaviour of seismic waves, explain how geologists know that (i) the outer core is liquid and (ii) oceanic lithosphere is being subducted at destructive plate margins. [6]
Mark-scheme logic: Mark-scheme logic (AO1 knowledge, AO2 application; expect 2-3 marks per part, each a linked point): (i) Liquid outer core (1 each): S waves cannot travel through liquid; S waves are recorded up to about 105 degrees from the focus but absent beyond, so the ray path to greater angular distances is blocked by liquid. Direct P waves are absent in the shadow zone 105-140 degrees because they are refracted sharply downwards at the Gutenberg discontinuity, where velocity drops on entering material of lower rigidity - consistent with liquid iron-nickel outer core at 2890 km. Link each observation to its inference: a bare list of shadow-zone figures without inference scores half credit. (ii) Subduction (1 each): earthquake foci define an inclined Benioff zone dipping beneath the overriding plate from the trench to about 700 km. Deep foci occur only in cold, brittle slab material carried to depth, because normal mantle at that depth is too ductile to store elastic strain. Foci deepen landward from the trench in the direction of slab descent; andesitic volcanism above the slab at about 100 km corroborates flux melting. Synoptic credit: oceanic crust is denser (about 3.0 g/cm3) than continental crust (about 2.7 g/cm3), so the denser plate is consumed at the trench. Structure the answer in linked cause-effect chains for full QER credit.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Explain how travel-time curves from a seismic refraction survey can be used to estimate the thickness of the crust. State two assumptions of the method. [6]
Answer: Marks: P waves travel at V1 (about 6-7 km/s) through the crust and V2 (about 8 km/s) in the mantle (1). Direct waves arrive first close to the source; refracted waves critically refracted along the Moho arrive first at greater distances, giving two straight-line travel-time segments (1). The crossover distance D, where the lines meet, depends on crustal thickness (1). Apply H = (D/2) x sqrt[(V2-V1)/(V2+V1)] (1) with correct substitution and units (1). A greater crossover distance indicates thicker crust, e.g. beneath mountain belts (1). Assumptions (any 2 of): layers homogeneous (1); layers horizontal (1); velocities constant in each layer (1).
A magnetic anomaly 24 km from the axis of a mid-ocean ridge dates from the Brunhes-Matuyama reversal (780,000 years ago). Calculate the half spreading rate in cm per year, and explain why the anomaly pattern is symmetrical either side of the ridge. [4]
Answer: Rate = 24,000 m / 780,000 yr (1) = 0.0308 m/yr = about 3.1 cm/yr (1). Symmetry (1 each): reversals of the magnetic field are global and time-parallel (1); new crust forms at the ridge axis and moves outwards equally on both flanks, recording alternating normal and reversed polarity as stripes mirrored about the axis (Vine-Matthews-Morley) (1).
Compare the processes and products of divergent and convergent plate margins. [6]
Answer: 1 per valid linked point, e.g.: Divergent - plates separate (1); decompression partial melting of upwelling peridotite gives basaltic magma (1); products include mid-ocean ridge, axial rift valley, pillow lavas, shallow earthquakes (1). Convergent - oceanic plate subducts (1); flux melting as water leaves the slab plus crustal melting gives andesitic-rhyolitic magmas (1); products include trench, Benioff zone to 700 km, island or Andean arc, accretionary prism, deep earthquakes (1). Credit explicit contrast, e.g. basaltic vs andesitic volcanism, shallow vs deep foci, creation vs destruction of lithosphere.
Evaluate slab pull and ridge push as mechanisms driving plate motion. [4]
Answer: Slab pull (1): dense, cold subducting slab pulls the trailing plate; accounts for fast Pacific plate motion (1). Ridge push (1): elevated ridge lithosphere slides off the topographic high on the asthenosphere (1). Evaluation (1): slab pull is regarded as dominant because plates attached to long subducting margins move fastest and ridge topography alone is too small to account for observed velocities; basal drag is weak. Accept reasoned argument either way if evidence is cited.
Distinguish between the lithosphere and the asthenosphere, and explain why this distinction is important for plate tectonic theory. [4]
Answer: Lithosphere (1): rigid outer shell of crust plus uppermost mantle, about 100 km thick (up to 200+ km beneath cratons). Asthenosphere (1): mechanically weak, partially molten zone of the upper mantle beneath it, roughly 100-350 km deep, showing reduced seismic velocities. Importance (1 each): plates are blocks of rigid lithosphere (1); movement is possible because the weak asthenosphere allows the plates to glide and is where decompression melting occurs beneath ridges (1).
