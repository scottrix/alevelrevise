Geological Mapwork

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level WJEC/Eduqas

Dip and strike, outcrop patterns, folds, faults, structure contours, cross-sections and borehole interpretation - Eduqas A-Level Geology (rock deformation and geological map applications) revised with exam-style questions and full mark schemes.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Record and interpret dip and strike, and apply the V-rule and outcrop-pattern rules to dipping, horizontal and vertical beds
  • Recognise folds on maps and sections, determine plunge direction from outcrop closure, and classify fold types
  • Identify normal, reverse, thrust and strike-slip faults on maps, determine downthrow and calculate throw and heave
  • Construct structure contours from three-point problems and calculate true dip, apparent dip and stratigraphic thickness
  • Draw accurate cross-sections and interpret borehole data to establish subsurface structure
  • Recognise unconformities on maps and integrate them into the geological history

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Strike: The compass bearing of the line of intersection between an inclined plane (bedding, fault) and the horizontal; perpendicular to the true dip direction.
Dip: The maximum angle between an inclined plane and the horizontal, measured in the vertical plane perpendicular to the strike, together with the dip direction.
Apparent dip: The dip of a bed measured in any vertical plane not perpendicular to the strike; always less than the true dip.
Plunge: The angle between the hinge line of a fold and the horizontal, measured in the vertical plane containing the hinge; the outcrop nose points in the plunge direction.
Axial plane (surface): The plane joining the hinge points of the folds in a fold train, bisecting the limbs; its trace on a map is the axial trace.
Limb: The part of a fold between the hinge and the next fold's hinge; in an overfold both limbs dip in the same direction and one is inverted.
Throw: The vertical component of displacement on a fault; heave is the horizontal component (heave = throw / tan of fault dip).
Structure contour: A line joining points of equal altitude on an inclined plane such as a bedding surface or fault plane; spacing gives the horizontal equivalent of the dip.
Outcrop: The area on a map or at the surface where a rock unit is exposed at bedrock level (solid or drift).
V-rule: Where a dipping bed crosses a valley its outcrop forms a V: the apex points in the direction of dip if the dip is steeper than the valley gradient, and up-dip if it is gentler.

📌 Key Points

Horizontal beds: outcrops parallel contours. Vertical beds: straight outcrops ignoring topography. Dipping beds: V-rule in valleys.
True dip is always measured perpendicular to strike; apparent dip is always less. tan(apparent) = tan(true) x sin(angle between section and strike).
Plunging anticline: oldest rocks at the nose; plunging syncline: youngest at the nose; nose points in the plunge direction.
Downthrow: match a marker bed across the fault - the side on which beds are omitted is the direction of downthrow side; repeated beds indicate the opposite.
Thickness: vertical thickness = true thickness / sin(dip); on flat ground outcrop width w = t / sin(dip).

Dip, strike and outcrop patterns (Eduqas core: rock deformation; theme: geological map applications)

Strike is the bearing of the line a bed makes with the horizontal (e.g. 045 degrees); dip is the maximum inclination of the plane, measured perpendicular to strike in a vertical plane (e.g. 30 degrees south-east). Every mapwork answer starts here: state dip DIRECTION as well as amount.

Outcrop patterns discriminate dip from topography:

  • Horizontal beds: outcrops follow the contours exactly (parallel bands); the youngest bed is at the highest ground.
  • Vertical beds: outcrops are straight lines crossing topography at right angles regardless of relief; width of outcrop = true thickness.
  • Dipping beds: V-rule - where a bed crosses a valley the outcrop forms a V; the apex points in the dip direction if the dip is steeper than the valley gradient, and points up-dip (opposite to dip) if the dip is gentler than the gradient. On ridges the V reverses.

Practise reading the common patterns: a homoclinal dip-slope/scarp sequence (regional dip one direction); inliers (older rocks surrounded by younger, e.g. through an unconformity or in a dome) and outliers (younger rocks surrounded by older, e.g. an eroded dome remnant). Unconformities on maps: older rocks below the unconformity surface are truncated by it, younger beds above overlap them at an angle (angular unconformity) or parallel (disconformity/paraconformity); basal conglomerate commonly overlies the surface. Exam questions often ask you to prove an unconformity from a map: quote the truncation of strike directions plus a way-up/fossil age difference.

Scarp and vale topography is the surface expression of regional dip: alternating hard (scarp-forming: Chalk, limestone, gritstone) and soft (vale-forming: clay) beds dip gently one way, giving a steep scarp face and gentle dip slope (the Chalk scarps of southern England). Read dip from the landscape: steep scarp faces the up-dip direction; dip slopes descend in the dip direction. On BGS maps, the legend gives lithology colours and symbols; the scale and contour interval set what precision of dip calculation is possible. Apparent dip on any cross-section not perpendicular to strike is less than true dip - state this when projecting dips onto sections at an angle to strike: tan(apparent) = tan(true) x sin(angle between the section line and the strike).

Folds on maps and sections

Fold recognition from outcrop pattern and dip arrows: anticlines (oldest rocks in the core) and synclines (youngest in the core); antiform/synform where way-up is unknown. Plunge: a plunging fold's outcrop pattern closes to a nose (U-shape) - the nose points in the direction of plunge; for a plunging anticline the OLDEST beds form the nose, for a plunging syncline the YOUNGEST. Non-plunging folds produce parallel, endless outcrops. Vertical (isoclinal, overturned) folds show both limbs dipping the same way; identify inverted limbs from way-up structures (graded bedding, cross-bedding) or from cleavage-bedding relations (cleavage steeper than bedding on the normal limb).

Terminology to deploy: hinge, limbs, axial trace (the line joining hinges on the map), axial plane dip, vergence (direction of overturning - asymmetric folds verge towards the foreland, e.g. Caledonian folds verge NW in the Scottish Highlands). Culminations and basins: domes (oldest at centre, concentric outcrops) and structural basins (youngest at centre). Parasitic folds on the limbs of major folds record shear sense (S/Z asymmetry). Mapwork questions ask you to state fold type, axial trace orientation, plunge amount/direction and to label a section accordingly - practise sketching anticlines/synclines with plunge shown by the closure direction and dip symbols.

Quick drill

Closure with oldest rocks in the nose = plunging anticline. Closure with youngest = plunging syncline. Concentric rings with oldest at centre = dome. Youngest at centre = basin. Straight parallel bands with alternating old-young-old = non-plunging fold train.

Fold-recognition workflow for exam answers: (1) establish way-up from sedimentary structures (graded bedding, cross-bedding) or cleavage-bedding relations - cleavage transecting bedding proves younging direction; (2) read dip arrows on both limbs; (3) identify the axial trace (line through the hinge points); (4) determine plunge from outcrop closure. Vergence - the direction the asymmetric folds overturn - records the shear sense of the orogeny: Caledonian folds in the Scottish Highlands verge NW towards the foreland. Culminations and basins: map-scale domes (oldest at centre) and basins (youngest at centre) show concentric outcrops; plunging fold trains show repeated noses. Parasitic folds (S on one limb, Z on the other, M on the hinge) record which limb of the major fold they sit on - a genuine A* discriminator.

Faults on maps and sections

Classify faults by the relative movement of the hanging wall (rock above the fault plane) and footwall (below): normal faults (hanging wall down - extension), reverse faults (hanging wall up - compression) and thrusts (reverse faults dipping under 45 degrees), plus strike-slip (wrench) faults with horizontal dextral/sinistral offset. On sections, mark the fault plane, hade (angle from vertical) and the displaced beds.

Recognising faults on maps: straight or curved fault lines truncating outcrops; offset of beds or dykes across the fault; omission (missing beds at the surface on the downthrow side, where the fault plane dips the same way as the beds) or repetition of strata; fault-line valleys along weaker fault rocks (breccia, gouge). Determining downthrow - the routine exam method: (1) match a distinctive marker bed either side of the fault; (2) project its dip across the fault; the side on which the sequence sits lower is the downthrow side. Alternatively: where strata are cut out (omitted) at the surface, the downthrow side is the direction towards which the missing beds dip; where beds are repeated, the downthrow is in the opposite sense. Quote the marker-bed method in answers and label the throw.

Throw and heave: throw = vertical displacement; heave = throw / tan(dip of fault) (horizontal displacement). Example: a fault dipping 60 degrees with 100 m throw has heave = 100 / tan60 = about 58 m. Horsts and grabens (paired normal faults - rift valleys, North Sea fault blocks) and the tectonic setting of each fault type (normal = rifts/extension; reverse/thrust = collision zones; wrench = conservative margins) earn the synoptic marks. Faults control ore localisation, aquifer boundaries and landslide risk - a standard applied point in Component 3.

Structure contours on fault planes give the most rigorous downthrow determination: construct contours on the fault surface by the three-point method from where it intersects two beds plus its dip; then compare the altitude of a marker bed on each side projected to the fault - the difference in altitude is the throw. Hadé is the complement of the fault dip (angle between the fault plane and the vertical); a 60 degree dip has a 30 degree hade. Fault regimes link to tectonics: normal faults and graben (paired listric faults) dominate rifts and the North Sea rotated fault blocks; reverse faults and thrusts dominate collision zones (Moine Thrust); wrench faults dominate conservative margins. Fault-line scenery: fault-line valleys follow weaker fault rocks (breccia, gouge); reactivated faults may juxtapose resistant against weak lithology, controlling escarpment lines.

Structure contours, cross-sections and boreholes

Structure contours join points of equal altitude on a planar surface (bed, unconformity, fault plane). From three spot heights on the same bed (the three-point problem): draw the contour through the intermediate point, spaced between the high and low points, then compute dip = tan-1(vertical interval / horizontal equivalent). Contour spacing gives the horizontal equivalent: closer contours = steeper dip. If a structure contour and an outcrop line intersect, the bed at that point is at the contour's altitude - use this to find the depth to a horizon anywhere on the map: depth = contour altitude at the point minus ground height.

Thickness calculations are examined numerically: vertical thickness (measured down a vertical borehole between the top and base of a bed) = true thickness / sin(dip); so true thickness t = vertical thickness x sin(dip). On flat ground the horizontal outcrop width w gives t = w x sin(dip). Example: a bed with 40 m vertical thickness dipping 30 degrees has true thickness 40 x sin30 = 20 m. For inclined boreholes the downhole interval is not the vertical thickness - correct using the borehole angle (standard AO3 trap).

Cross-section construction - the routine Component 3 practical: (1) draw the section line on the map; (2) mark every topographic detail (use the contour profile) and every geology boundary at its intersection with the section line; (3) project dips at each boundary - for folds, use the dip arrows and axial trace; (4) extend boundaries smoothly downwards, keeping thicknesses constant and honouring the data (extrapolate no deeper than about equal to the width of exposure); (5) draw faults with their dip, mark downthrow, and restore displaced sections if asked. Borehole data: logs record depths, lithologies, dips of beds, groundwater strikes; correlate marker beds between boreholes to build the subsurface picture (the same logic as field correlation - biostratigraphic matching preferred to lithological). Exam questions give borehole depths for a marker bed in two holes plus its outcrop dip - expect a three-point calculation for structure/dip and a statement of the assumptions (beds planar, no folding between holes).

Worked three-point problem: spot heights on the base of a sandstone are A (200 m), B (150 m) and C (100 m), with B between A and C horizontally 200 m from each. The contour through B runs parallel to strike; the vertical interval A-B = 50 m over 200 m horizontal, so dip = tan-1(50/200) = about 14 degrees towards C. Depth-to-horizon: at any point, depth = structure-contour altitude at that point minus ground height - draw contours across the whole map by interpolating from the outcrop intersections. Cross-section rules: mark true dips (correct apparent dips first), keep bed thicknesses constant down-section, honour all data, and extrapolate downward no deeper than roughly the width of exposure; show faults with downthrow arrows and the displaced beds. Borehole correlation: match marker beds (or better, fossil/isotope-dated horizons) between holes, draw dips from the logs, and state assumptions: beds planar between holes, no undetected folding or faulting.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: A geological map shows a dipping sequence (limestone, sandstone, mudstone) cut by a fault that truncates all three outcrops. Describe how you would determine (i) the direction of downthrow and (ii) the type of fault, stating the evidence you would use. [6]
Mark-scheme logic: Mark-scheme logic (AO1 method + AO2 application; 3 marks per part): (i) Downthrow (1 each): match a distinctive marker bed (e.g. the limestone) on both sides of the fault (1); project its dip direction and amount across the fault (structure contours on the bed help) (1); the side on which the beds are omitted (cut out) at the surface is the downthrow side, because downthrow carries shallower (younger) levels of the sequence down against the fault trace (1). Where beds are repeated across the fault, downthrow is in the opposite sense. (ii) Fault type (1 each): determine the fault plane dip from its outcrop/structure contours (1); if the downthrown side is the hanging wall side consistent with extension, and the fault plane dips towards the downthrow, the fault is normal; if it dips away from the downthrow it is reverse (1); state the test explicitly - a normal fault brings younger beds against the fault on the downthrow side with omission; a reverse fault causes repetition of beds at the surface on the downthrow side (1). Full credit needs the method stated with the marker bed named and the omission/repetition reasoning explicit.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

A bed has a vertical thickness of 50 m and dips at 30 degrees. Calculate its true thickness and its outcrop width on flat ground. [3]
Answer: True thickness = vertical thickness x sin(dip) = 50 x sin30 = 25 m (1 for method, 1 for answer). Outcrop width on flat ground = vertical thickness / sin(dip) = 50 / sin30 = 100 m (1). Credit correct substitution and units.
Explain how the V-rule is used to determine the dip direction of beds crossing a valley. [4]
Answer: The outcrop of a dipping bed forms a V where it crosses a valley (1); the apex points in the direction of dip when the dip is steeper than the valley gradient (1); when the dip is gentler than the gradient the apex points up-dip, opposite to the dip direction (1). Contrast: horizontal beds follow the contours exactly (V opens upstream); vertical beds form straight traces ignoring topography (1).
How would you recognise a plunging anticline on a geological map? [4]
Answer: Outcrop pattern closes to a nose/U-shape (1); the OLDEST rocks form the core of the nose (1); the nose points in the direction of plunge (1); dips on the two limbs converge towards the axial trace, and the axial trace is inclined (has a plunge component) (1). Contrast with non-plunging folds: endless parallel outcrops.
A fault dips at 70 degrees and has a throw of 120 m. Calculate the heave, and state the fault type if the hanging wall has moved down. [3]
Answer: Heave = throw / tan(dip) = 120 / tan70 = about 44 m (1 method, 1 answer). Hanging wall down = normal fault (1) - extensional regime; beds are omitted at the surface on the downthrow side.
Describe how to construct a structure contour and calculate dip from three spot heights on the same bedding surface. [6]
Answer: Join the highest and lowest spot heights (1); interpolate the contour for the altitude of the intermediate point along that line (1); the direction of the contour = strike (1). Horizontal equivalent = perpendicular distance from the intermediate spot height to the contour line (1); dip = tan-1(vertical interval / horizontal equivalent), stated in degrees and direction (1). Assumptions: beds are planar between the points (1).
