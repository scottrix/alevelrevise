Geological Resources

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level WJEC/Eduqas

Ore minerals, hydrocarbons, aquifers, building materials, exploration techniques and sustainable resource use - Eduqas A-Level Geology core (Earth materials and natural resources) revised with exam-style questions and full mark schemes.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain the formation of ore deposits by magmatic, hydrothermal, sedimentary and residual (weathering) processes, with named examples
  • Describe the petroleum system elements (source, maturation, migration, reservoir, seal, trap) and apply them to a named basin
  • Define porosity and permeability, classify aquifer types and explain groundwater exploitation and contamination problems
  • Describe the exploration sequence from desk study through geophysics and geochemistry to drilling, evaluating each technique
  • Evaluate resource exploitation against sustainability, including energy-transition metals, recycling and mining environmental impacts

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Ore: A rock or mineral deposit containing a metal (or other material) in sufficient concentration and tonnage, at the prevailing price, to be mined economically.
Grade: The concentration of metal in an ore, usually quoted as a percentage or grams per tonne; the cut-off grade is the lowest concentration that can be mined at a profit.
Porosity: The percentage of a rock's volume that is void space (pores, fractures); determines storage capacity for water or hydrocarbons.
Permeability: The ease with which a fluid can flow through a rock, controlled by the connectedness and size of pores/fractures; measured in darcies or millidarcies.
Aquifer: A permeable rock body that stores and transmits useful quantities of groundwater (e.g. Chalk, Permo-Triassic sandstones); aquitards (e.g. London Clay) transmit water slowly; aquicludes effectively not at all.
Source rock: An organic-rich (kerogen-bearing) fine-grained sediment, e.g. the Kimmeridge Clay, which generates hydrocarbons when heated through the oil window (about 60-120 C for oil; gas at higher temperatures).
Oil window: The burial depth/temperature range (roughly 60-120 C, about 2-4 km) in which kerogen matures to liquid oil; above it gas and condensate form and beyond about 150 C oil is destroyed.
Structural trap: A trap formed by deformation - anticlinal crest, fault block or salt dome - that stops migrating hydrocarbons; stratigraphic traps (pinch-outs, unconformity overlaps) are formed by depositional change.
Placer deposit: A surficial concentration of dense, chemically resistant heavy minerals (gold, cassiterite) sorted and deposited by flowing water (alluvial, beach, or ancient palaeoplacers such as Witwatersrand).
Acid mine drainage: Sulfuric acid and iron hydroxide runoff formed when pyrite in mine waste and workings oxidises on exposure to air and water, contaminating streams and groundwater.

📌 Key Points

Ore processes: magmatic segregation (Bushveld chromite/Pt, Sudbury Ni-Cu); hydrothermal (porphyry Cu, epithermal Au, VMS black smokers); sedimentary/exhalative (BIFs, SEDEX Pb-Zn); residual (bauxite) and placer (Witwatersrand, Cornish cassiterite).
Petroleum system: source (Kimmeridge Clay) - maturation (oil window 60-120 C) - migration (buoyancy) - reservoir (porous sandstone/carbonate) - seal (salt/shale) - trap (anticline/fault/pinch-out).
Porosity = storage; permeability = flow. Clay is porous but impermeable. Fractured Chalk aquifer: storage in matrix, flow in fractures.
Exploration sequence: desk study/mapping - remote sensing - geophysics (magnetic, gravity, seismic, resistivity) - geochemistry (stream sediment) - drilling - evaluation.
Energy-transition metals (Li, Co, Cu, rare earths) drive new exploration; finite resources require recycling, rehabilitation and environmental management.

Ore minerals and metallogenesis

An ore is metal in economic concentration - grade x tonnage at the prevailing price defines profitability (cut-off grade). Learn each ore-forming process with its diagnostic setting and examples:

ProcessMechanismDeposits/examples
Magmatic segregationDense oxide/sulfide crystals settle in layered basic intrusions or magma immiscibilityChromite and PGMs (Bushveld Complex, South Africa); Ni-Cu (Sudbury, Canada)
HydrothermalHot metal-bearing fluids precipitate in fractures, porphyries or on the sea floorPorphyry Cu-Mo (huge tonnage, low grade); epithermal Au-Ag; skarns; volcanogenic massive sulfide (VMS) at ridges - black smokers
Sedimentary/exhalativeChemical precipitation or basin brines exhaled onto the sea floorBanded iron formations (Precambrian, Hamersley); SEDEX Pb-Zn-Ba (Silvermines, Ireland)
Residual/weatheringIntense leaching leaves insoluble residuesBauxite (tropical weathering of aluminous rocks); lateritic Ni
PlacerWater sorts dense resistant grains into surficial concentrationsGold (Witwatersrand palaeoplacer); cassiterite (Cornish stream tin)

Metamorphic and tectonic links earn synoptic marks: hydrothermal fluids are commonly driven by granites (Cornwall: tin-tungsten-copper zoned around granite cupolas) or by circulating seawater at ridges; BIFs record Precambrian anoxic oceans; ophiolite-hosted deposits (Cyprus) mark former ocean floor. Exam questions supply an ore description or locality and expect the process, the mechanism and one piece of supporting evidence - practise arguing from texture (banded, massive, disseminated) and setting.

Ore textures diagnose the process: disseminated (fine grains spread through the host - porphyry copper), massive sulfide (near-solid metal sulfide - VMS), banded (alternating iron oxide and silica/chert layers - BIF), vein (hydrothermal fluids filling fractures, often with a zoned mineral sequence), disseminated cumulate (settled crystals - Bushveld chromitite layers). Zonation around granites is the classic Cornwall case: skarns/tungsten at the contact, then copper, then lead-zinc, then iron and manganese outwards, with cassiterite close to the cupola - the sequence records falling fluid temperature with distance. Hydrothermal fluids originate from the granite itself (late-stage magmatic water), from circulating groundwater heated by the intrusion, or from seawater circulating at ridges (VMS) - state the fluid source when explaining a deposit.

Hydrocarbons and the petroleum system

Revise the petroleum system as a chain of elements, each examined in turn: source rock - organic-rich mudrock (kerogen) such as the Kimmeridge Clay (Late Jurassic, North Sea); maturation - burial heats kerogen through the oil window (about 60-120 C; deeper, gas and condensate; beyond about 150 C oil is cracked/destroyed); primary migration out of the source and secondary migration through permeable carrier beds driven by buoyancy; reservoir - porous AND permeable rock (clean sandstone, e.g. Brent Group; oolitic/fractured carbonate) holding oil in its pore space; seal - impermeable caprock (salt, shale, e.g. the Zechstein evaporites of the Southern North Sea gas fields); trap - structural (anticlinal crest, fault-block - the rotated fault blocks of the North Sea), stratigraphic (pinch-out, unconformity overlap, reef) or combination; plus timing - the trap must exist before migration. Sketch an anticlinal trap with oil-gas-water contacts: gas on top, oil beneath, water below (buoyancy stratification).

Exploration: seismic reflection surveys (2D/3D) image subsurface structure by recording two-way travel times of reflections - depth = velocity x time/2; identify anticlines, fault blocks, pinch-outs. Well logs identify reservoirs and fluids downhole: gamma ray (distinguishes shale - high K - from clean sandstone), resistivity (high in hydrocarbon-filled pores - fluids are insulators), sonic and density-neutron (porosity; crossover indicates gas), caliper. Drilling (rotary, mud logging, cuttings and core) tests the prospect. Unconventional resources: shale gas by hydraulic fracturing (Bowland Shale, Lancashire), coal-bed methane, oil sands - know that fracking injects water/sand/chemicals at pressure to open fractures, and the environmental concerns (groundwater, induced seismicity). These questions link directly to the mapwork topic (seismic sections, fault blocks) and to stratigraphy (source/reservoir ages).

Extend the North Sea case with its thermal history - the timing element examiners reward: rifting (Permian-Triassic and Jurassic) created rotated fault blocks and deposited the Jurassic Kimmeridge Clay source; Cenozoic burial of the source into the oil window generated oil that migrated (Tertiary) into pre-existing traps - Paleocene/Eocene sandstone reservoirs (Forties) and Jurassic fault-block traps (Brent province). The Southern Gas Basin differs: Carboniferous Coal Measures generate gas, trapped beneath the impermeable Zechstein salt seal in Permian Rotliegendes sandstone. Maturation evidence: vitrinite reflectance and burial/thermal history curves show when the source entered the window; a trap formed after migration is barren - timing is the mark. Sketch the anticlinal trap with gas-oil-water contacts: gas top (lowest density), oil middle, water below.

Migration and trapping detail: primary migration is expulsion of oil from the compacting source rock into carrier beds (driven by overpressure from compaction and hydrocarbon generation); secondary migration is buoyant flow up-dip through permeable beds until stopped by a seal - oil accumulates in the trap crest with gas on top. Impermeable seals depend on capillary entry pressure (fine-grained clay, evaporite); fault seals can both trap (clay smearing) and leak (open fractures) - a standard AO2 point. If the seal fails or the trap is breached, hydrocarbons seep to the surface (oil seeps, tar pits) - historically the first indicators of petroleum provinces.

Groundwater, aquifers and building materials

Hydrogeology: porosity (void percentage - storage) versus permeability (connectedness - flow). Clay is porous but effectively impermeable (pores too small); the Chalk stores water in its matrix but transmits it through fractures - a classic discriminating point. Aquifer types: unconfined (water table open to the surface, recharge by rainfall), confined (aquifer bounded above and below by aquitards - water under pressure) giving artesian wells where the piezometric surface is above ground (historically in the London Basin, where Chalk is confined by London Clay); perched water tables above local aquitards. The major UK aquifers are the Chalk and the Permo-Triassic sandstones - know their characters: Chalk fissure flow and high storage; sandstones intergranular flow.

Exploitation and problems: wells, springs and boreholes; over-abstraction lowers the water table (dry wells, reduced baseflow to rivers, land subsidence - London's historic decline and recovery) and causes saline intrusion along coasts (sea water replaces drawn-down fresh water under London). Contamination: nitrates from agriculture, hydrocarbons (LNAPLs float on the water table - petrol; DNAPLs sink - solvents), landfill leachate; protection via source protection zones and aquifer vulnerability mapping (BGS/Evironment Agency mapping). Engineering geology of construction: aggregates (crushed limestone/gritstone; sand and gravel - the largest UK quarry product), dimension stone (Portland Stone, granite - Aberdeen), slate (Welsh quarries - Penrhyn), brick clay, gypsum (plaster), and cement (limestone + clay calcined). Foundations: rock strength, dip of bedding out of slope, perched water and mined ground (shafts) control site suitability - standard Component 3 applied questions give a site description and ask for hazards and suitability judgments.

Water table behaviour: unconfined water tables rise with recharge (winter) and fall with drought and abstraction - contour maps of the water table show flow directions perpendicular to the contours, from recharge to discharge areas (springs, river baseflow, the sea). Groundwater age can be dated by C-14 (dating the dissolved carbonate) - deep Chalk groundwater may be thousands of years old, meaning slow recharge and slow recovery from over-abstraction: a genuine A* synthesis link to radiometric dating. Building stone durability: limestones suffer sulphation in polluted cities (SO2 forms gypsum crusts that spall), sandstones resist; Portland Stone weathers to a honeyed patina in clean air - stone selection is a classic applied question. Aggregate quality: strength (crushing value), polish resistance for road stone (gritstone, not limestone) and alkali-silica reactivity control use - exam questions give a proposed quarry site and ask for the quality tests and market.

Exploration techniques and sustainability

Exploration sequence (learn as an ordered workflow with the purpose of each stage): (1) desk study - geological maps, borehole records, archives (BGS GeoReports); (2) remote sensing - satellite/aerial imagery, lineaments; (3) geophysical surveys - magnetic (magnetite ores, dykes), gravity (dense ores, salt domes - low gravity), electrical resistivity (aquifers, clays), induced polarisation (disseminated sulfides), seismic reflection (basins, traps); (4) geochemical surveys - stream sediment, soil and lithogeochemistry trace ores to source (anomalies); (5) drilling - percussion/rotary (cheap testing) and diamond core (structural and grade data); (6) evaluation - resource estimation, grade-tonnage curves, feasibility. Evaluate each: geophysics covers ground cheaply without drilling but is ambiguous (non-uniqueness - multiple geologies fit one anomaly); geochemistry localises but not quantifies; drilling is definitive but expensive and point-sampled - hence the sequence from cheap-wide to expensive-narrow. Exam questions give a scenario (e.g. suspected porphyry copper beneath soil cover) and expect the chosen techniques justified in order.

Sustainability: resources are finite; demand rises with population and the energy transition (lithium from brines and pegmatites, cobalt, copper for electrification, rare earth elements for magnets - monazite/bastnasite). Evaluate: recycling extends supply but cannot fully replace mining; new exploration opens environmentally sensitive areas; mining impacts include acid mine drainage (pyrite oxidation producing sulfuric acid and iron hydroxides), subsidence (coal fields), tailings dam failures and habitat loss; rehabilitation (restoration to agriculture/wetland), water management and planning conditions mitigate. Examiners reward balanced judgment: economic benefit versus environmental cost, the role of geological survey data (BGS) in responsible land-use decisions, and the difference between renewability (groundwater - recharge rate matters) and finite stocks (ores, hydrocarbons). Link back to hazards (tailings, subsidence) and mapwork (site evaluation) for the synoptic marks.

Evaluation depth for the exploration sequence: state the non-uniqueness of geophysical anomalies (one magnetic anomaly could be a dyke, a magnetite body or buried iron - only drilling resolves it), the point-sample problem of drilling (grade between holes interpolated, hence grade-tonnage curves), and the cost logic that drives the sequence from cheap-wide (desk study, remote sensing) to expensive-narrow (diamond drilling). BGS role in the UK: regional mapping, borehole records, GeoReports and landslide/ground stability data underpin both exploration and planning decisions - cite it in sustainability answers. Sustainability synthesis: groundwater is renewable only if abstraction stays below recharge (the aquifer over-abstraction point cross-links to the hydrogeology section); ores and hydrocarbons are finite stocks, so recycling, substitution and efficiency extend them; the energy transition creates new demand (lithium, cobalt, rare earths) whose mining carries the same environmental management duties - balance economic benefit against environmental cost, with named impacts, for the evaluation marks.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: With reference to a named hydrocarbon province, describe the elements of the petroleum system and explain how seismic reflection surveys and well logs are used in exploration. [8] (QER)
Mark-scheme logic: Mark-scheme logic (AO1 + AO2; levels-based QER): Named province + elements (1 each): the UK North Sea - source: Kimmeridge Clay (Late Jurassic, kerogen-rich); maturation: burial through the oil window (about 60-120 C); migration: buoyant secondary migration into carrier beds; reservoir: porous, permeable Brent Group sandstones (or Paleocene sands); seal: impermeable shale/mudstone caprocks (Zechstein salt in the Southern Gas Basin); trap: rotated fault blocks (structural) and pinch-outs (stratigraphic); timing: traps formed by Jurassic-Cretaceous rifting before migration. Seismic reflection (1-2): energy source sends waves down; reflections at velocity contrasts recorded at receivers; two-way travel time gives depth (v x t/2); images anticlines, fault blocks and pinch-outs to plan wells. Well logs (1-2): gamma ray separates shale from clean sandstone; resistivity high where pores are hydrocarbon-filled; sonic/density-neutron give porosity (gas crossover). Synthesis (1): the system elements must all be present and timed - explaining why exploration combines seismic imaging of structure with log analysis of reservoir quality. Named basin and rock formations required throughout.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Explain how banded iron formations formed, and state one reason why they are absent from Phanerozoic rocks. [4]
Answer: Formation (1 each): chemical precipitation of iron and silica in Precambrian oceans (1); iron supplied by hydrothermal vents/weathering, precipitated as hematite/magnetite in alternating bands, often seasonally cyclic (1); photosynthetic oxygenation oxidised dissolved Fe(II) to insoluble Fe(III). Absence (1): after about 1.8 Ga oceans became fully oxygenated, so iron stayed dissolved only briefly/iron was no longer supplied in the same way - banded iron formation requires the anoxic Precambrian ocean chemistry.
Distinguish between porosity and permeability, and explain why the Chalk can be both a good and a poor aquifer. [4]
Answer: Porosity (1): percentage of void space - storage capacity. Permeability (1): ease of flow through connected pores/fractures; clay is porous but impermeable. Chalk (1 each): high matrix porosity - stores large volumes of water (1); matrix permeability is low because pores are minute - flow occurs mainly through fractures, so yield depends on fracturing and fissure flow; over-abstraction can rapidly lower the water table.
Explain how a porphyry copper deposit forms and why such deposits are low grade but high tonnage. [5]
Answer: Formation (1 each): a silica-rich (intermediate-acid) magma chambers at a destructive margin exsolves metal-bearing hydrothermal fluids (1); fluids circulate through the cooling pluton's fracture network and precipitate disseminated chalcopyrite (with molybdenite, gold) in a broad halo/zoned shell around the cupola (1); stockwork of veinlets gives the characteristic texture. Low grade/high tonnage (1 each): metals precipitate finely dispersed through a huge volume of rock rather than concentrated in veins (1); mined by open pit at large scale where bulk tonnage offsets the low cut-off grade (e.g. Chilean deposits).
Describe the exploration sequence you would recommend for a suspected gold-bearing quartz vein system beneath soil cover, justifying each stage. [6]
Answer: Sequence (1 each, with justification): (1) desk study - geological maps and borehole archives to locate the vein trend (cheap, wide); (2) geochemical survey - stream sediment and soil sampling for gold/pathfinder elements (As, Sb) to localise anomalies; (3) geophysics - magnetic/induced polarisation or resistivity to trace the quartz-sulfide veins under cover (non-invasive); (4) trenching/pitting to sample the anomaly at shallow depth; (5) diamond drilling along the vein trend to test depth continuation and grade (definitive but expensive - last); (6) evaluation - resource estimation and feasibility. Credit the cheap-to-expensive ordering logic and justification of each technique.
Explain the formation of acid mine drainage and describe two mitigation strategies. [4]
Answer: Formation (1 each): pyrite in mine workings/waste oxidises on exposure to air and water (1); produces sulfuric acid and iron hydroxide precipitates (yellow boy), acidifying streams and mobilising metals into groundwater. Mitigation (1 each): prevent oxidation - seal/adit capping, water tables kept high by flooded workings; treat the water - lime neutralisation/settlement lagoons, or passive reed-bed wetlands; accept active treatment plants and mine rehabilitation.
