Media Language

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level OCR, WJEC/Eduqas

A-Level Media Studies revision on media language (OCR H409 Component 01; WJEC/Eduqas A680 Component 1): codes and conventions, genre (Neale), narrative (Todorov, Propp), semiotics (Barthes) and multimodality, applied to the set products for A* analysis.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Analyse how technical, symbolic and written codes construct meaning and response in any set product (AO2)
  • Apply Neale's genre theory to explain how products balance repetition and difference for industries and audiences (AO2)
  • Use Todorov, Propp and Barthes' narrative codes to map narrative structure and audience engagement (AO2)
  • Denote then connote: apply Barthes' order of signification and anchorage to print and audiovisual texts (AO2)
  • Evaluate how multimodal texts (websites, video games, vlogs) use the affordances of different modes (AO2)
  • Deploy precise media language vocabulary under timed exam conditions (AO1)

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Code: A system of signs or technical conventions that carries meaning: technical (camera, editing, sound, lighting), symbolic (colour, costume, props), written (headlines, captions, typography).
Conventions: The established, repeated features audiences expect within a form or genre (e.g. a newspaper masthead, a soap cliffhanger, a vlogger's direct address); deviation from them is itself meaningful.
Genre hybridity: The blending of two or more genres in one product (e.g. Luther = crime procedural + psychological thriller; The Returned = supernatural horror + domestic drama), creating novelty within familiarity.
Enigma code: Barthes' hermeneutic code: narrative devices that pose and delay answers to questions, sustaining audience interest (e.g. why do the dead return in The Returned?).
Anchorage: Barthes' term for text or image elements (captions, voiceover, headlines) that fix or steer the preferred meaning of an ambiguous image; central to newspaper and advertising analysis.
Mise-en-scene: Everything placed in the frame: setting, costume, make-up, props, lighting, colour and blocking of actors; the symbolic code most directly read as representation.
Diegetic sound: Sound whose source exists inside the world of the text (dialogue, traffic, a radio in the scene); non-diegetic sound (score, voiceover) exists outside it. Contrapuntal sound clashes with the image's mood.
Multimodality: The combination of several meaning-making modes (visual, verbal, aural, gestural, spatial) in one text, and the different affordances each mode and platform offers (Kress and Van Leeuwen).

📌 Key Points

Nothing is accidental: every technical and aesthetic choice is a meaning-making decision you must be able to justify (AO2).
Neale: genres are 'instances of repetition and difference' - familiarity sells, novelty sustains.
Todorov: equilibrium, disruption, recognition, repair, new equilibrium - changed, not restored, in complex texts.
Barthes: denote first, then connote; ask how anchorage steers the reading.
Structure every analysis: convention named, example located, meaning explained, audience response inferred.

Codes, conventions and genre: constructing meaning

Start from the examiner's premise: nothing in a media product is accidental. Media language is the toolkit of codes and conventions through which producers construct meaning, and the exam rewards candidates who can name a convention, locate it precisely, explain the meaning it constructs and infer the response it seeks from an audience. That four-step chain - convention, example, meaning, audience - is the default architecture of every strong AO2 answer in OCR H409 Component 01 and Eduqas A680 Component 1.

Codes fall into three families. Technical codes are the production choices: camera (shot size, angle, movement, focus), editing (pacing, transitions, continuity, montage), sound (diegetic, non-diegetic, parallel, contrapuntal) and lighting. Symbolic codes are what is placed inside the frame or layout: colour palettes, costume, props, setting, typography. Written codes are the verbal layer: headlines, captions, straplines, slogans, and in audiovisual texts any on-screen text. In the set newspaper front pages, the Daily Mirror and The Times deploy all three: a red masthead and condensed sans-serif headlines (symbolic/written) signal populism and urgency, while the blue masthead, serif type and generous white space of The Times signal authority and tradition - the same day's news, two constructed realities.

  • Advertising: high-key lighting, direct address and slogan anchorage (the set Tide advert's typography pins its meaning to 1950s domesticity).
  • Television: low-key lighting, shallow focus and slow editing rhythms encode psychological threat in the set episode of Luther.
  • Online and participatory media: the vlog conventions Zoella established - direct address, thumbnail expression, subscribe call-to-action - are codes audiences have learned to read.
  • Video games: HUD (heads-up display) elements are written codes that diegetically manage the player's attention.

Genre is the framework of conventions shared between texts of a type. It matters to industries because it reduces risk (Hesmondhalgh: cultural industries are risk-averse, and genre is the cheapest form of insurance - a genre label writes the marketing copy, sets the shelf space and pre-sells the audience) and to audiences because it generates the pleasure of expectation. Neale's theory is the one to quote: genres are 'instances of repetition and difference'. Pure repetition would bore; pure difference would confuse. Every successful product tunes the ratio. The set episode of Luther repeats crime-procedural iconography (crime scene, detective with a troubled past, interrogation) but differs by injecting horror-fantasy elements and an almost operatic emotional register - hybridity that refreshed a saturated genre.

Altman's semantic/syntactic distinction sharpens this: semantic elements are the countable surface features (guns, hotels, voiceovers - the genre's iconography), while syntactic elements are the relationships between them (the enigma structure, the moral test). A* answers classify iconography but then argue at the syntactic level: The Returned borrows zombie-genre iconography but its syntax is that of grief drama, not horror - which is why it resists the resolution audiences of the genre expect.

Genre also evolves. Watch for three engines of change: hybridity (combining genres), subversion (deliberately breaking conventions - risky; audiences can read it as incompetence rather than innovation) and historical drift (conventions date: the Tide advert's cheerfulness was conventional in 1955 and is now the text's most analysed 'difference'). Media language choices are therefore historically contingent - always ask what was conventional at the time of production before judging a product by today's norms.

Genre and representation interlock. Crime drama has long coded criminality as masculine, so Luther's casting of Idris Elba as the brilliant, compromised detective and Alice Morgan as an equal-intellect villain manipulates genre-gender expectations simultaneously - a point worth carrying into representation answers. Likewise the biopic genre's syntax of 'rise, fall, redemption' frames how Straight Outta Compton constructs its real-life subjects as tragic heroes rather than provocateurs, with clear ideological consequences for how the audience is invited to judge them.

Online media generate their own genres with their own codes: the YouTube vlog (direct address, jump-cut editing, personal disclosure), the listicle, the influencer 'haul', the charity campaign hashtag. Zoella's set channel is effectively the textbook for vlog conventions - intimacy, authenticity cues (messy hair, casual register), platform-native calls to action - and Attitude's website repurposes magazine conventions (cover star, sections, exclusives) into hypertext. Recognising that genres migrate across platforms, mutating as they go, is exactly the kind of conceptual extension that separates A* from A.

Exam method: the four-step chain

Name the convention precisely (technical/symbolic/written). Locate it in the set product (scene, frame, page). Explain the meaning or ideology it constructs - not just 'it shows', but what it argues. Infer the audience response sought (pleasure, identification, anxiety, action). Then, for top-band marks, evaluate: how does this choice balance repetition and difference (Neale)? What alternative choice could the producer have made, and why does this one suit the product's industrial and historical context?

Finally, keep the synoptic link alive: media language is never just aesthetic. Every convention a product repeats is a risk-reduction decision for an industry, and every difference it introduces is a bet on its audience. When an extended answer asks you to 'explore how media language creates meanings and responses', the strongest conclusions fold this in: the text's language is a commercial and cultural argument, not merely a style.

Conventions are cultural learning, not natural laws. Audiences learn to read codes through exposure: the newspaper masthead, the soap cliffhanger, the trailer's three-act compression are all conventions the audience has been trained to expect, and deviation from them is itself meaningful. This is why realist conventions carry an ideological charge: continuity editing conceals its own construction, so the text appears simply to show the world, when every cut is a decision. Non-realist or stylised conventions (Ayoade's freeze-frames in Submarine, Luther's expressionist lighting) foreground construction and invite the audience to notice the making - a different contract with the viewer, and one you should be able to name when a question asks how a product constructs 'response' rather than mere meaning.

Genre lives in the paratexts too. Trailers, posters, packaging and social campaigns are genre performances before the product arrives: they pre-frame the audience's expectations, and industries use them to manage the risk Neale's theory describes. The set Straight Outta Compton campaign included the 'Straight Outta Compton' meme generator - a paratext that let audiences perform the brand themselves, blending marketing with participation (Jenkins, Gauntlett) and demonstrating that genre and audience are managed together across the campaign, not just inside the text. When a question asks how media language 'positions audiences', remember that positioning begins before the first frame or front page: in the poster, the thumbnail, the headline seen on a feed.

The compare discipline. Many questions ask you to compare media language across two set products ('compare how... create meanings'). The rule: one analytical framework, applied to both, with the differences doing the argumentative work. Mirror vs Times (same day, two constructions of the public), Tide vs WaterAid (same form, six decades apart, two constructions of femininity), Luther vs The Returned (both prestige serials, opposite pacing philosophies). Comparability proves constructedness: if the same events or genre can be constructed two ways, neither construction is natural. That is the strongest closing move available in a compare question - and it is also Hall's representation theory seen from the language side.

Narrative: Todorov, Propp and structure

Narrative theory explains how stories are organised - and, crucially for the exam, how that organisation manufactures engagement. You already know the theories; revision means applying them fast and knowing their limits.

Todorov proposed that narratives move through five stages: equilibrium, disruption, recognition of disruption, attempted repair, and a new equilibrium. The model is deceptively simple, so sharpen it with two refinements. First, the new equilibrium is rarely a return to the original - it is changed, and the nature of the change carries the text's ideology. Second, the model applies at any scale: a three-minute advert has a complete Todorov arc. The set McCain 'We Are Family' campaign compresses it brutally: equilibrium (family mealtime harmony), disruption (implied chaos of modern family life - kids, mess, competing schedules), repair (the shared plate of chips resolves it), new equilibrium (a warmer, more inclusive image of the family than the 1950s default). That reading links narrative directly to representation and to the campaign's commercial strategy.

  • Submarine: equilibrium of Oliver Tate's self-consciously curated school life; disruption when Jordana and his mother's affair destabilise it; the repaired equilibrium is ironic - Oliver learns that controlling the narrative is not the same as living one.
  • Straight Outta Compton: the biopic's five stages map onto the group's formation, the police harassment that disrupts, the rise, the internal conflicts (attempted repair), and the bittersweet new equilibrium of Eazy-E's death and the group's legacy.
  • Luther: episodic equilibrium disrupted by Alice Morgan's 'perfect crime'; the procedural repair is attempted each week, but the serial form means the new equilibrium is always provisional - Luther's own moral equilibrium erodes across the series.
  • The Returned: a text that refuses Todorov - disruption is never repaired because the dead simply stay. Recognising when a text violates the model, and what that violation means (unresolvable grief; trauma without closure), is exactly the evaluative move top-band answers need.

Propp analysed Russian folktales to argue that narratives deploy a limited set of character functions: 31 narrative functions and seven spheres of action - hero, villain, donor, dispatcher, helper, princess (and her father), false hero. Applying Propp is strongest when you note how modern texts complicate the roles. In Luther, the villain (Alice) becomes something closer to a helper or dark double; the hero is himself criminally transgressive, collapsing the hero/villain binary - which is also a representation argument about masculinity and authority. In Straight Outta Compton, the dispatcher and villain roles are uncomfortably real: the music industry exploits the group (the false hero of management), while the LAPD functions as an institutionalised villain, giving the folktale structure a political charge Propp never imagined. In advertising, Propp is compact: the product is the donor granting the magic gift (clean laundry, family harmony), the consumer is the hero on a mini-quest - a structure that makes Tide and McCain advert narratives instantly legible.

Barthes' narrative codes give you a finer instrument than either model. The hermeneutic (enigma) code poses questions and delays answers: the central enigma of The Returned (why have the dead returned?) is deliberately never closed, sustaining the serialized suspense. The proairetic (action) code covers the sequence of actions the audience reads as causal (the procedural beats of Luther). The semantic code plants connotations (the cold blue palettes of The Returned connote emotional numbness). The symbolic code organises themes through opposition and repetition (water, returning, the family meal). The referential (cultural) code invokes shared cultural knowledge - N.W.A's real history, the LA riots, the audience's extra-textual memory. Enigma plus referential codes is a powerful pairing for any answer about why audiences keep watching.

Levi-Strauss adds the structuralist backbone: meaning is generated by binary oppositions - living/dead (The Returned), law/criminality (Luther), authenticity/commercialisation (Straight Outta Compton), natural/artificial (food advertising), tradition/modernity (The Times vs the Mirror's news values). The strongest answers use binaries to expose ideology: whichever side of the opposition a text privileges is the argument it is making. The Returned is a model case because it refuses to resolve its central binary - the living and the dead coexist in one household - and that refusal is precisely its theme: grief is not a problem to be solved.

Open vs closed, linear vs non-linear

Closed endings grant catharsis and brand confidence (procedurals reset weekly by design - an industrial as much as an aesthetic choice). Open endings sustain enigma across a series and fuel online speculation - fandom behaviour (Jenkins) that markets the text for free. Non-linear or fractured narratives (flashbacks in Submarine, the distributed timelines of The Returned) put the audience to work as active meaning-makers, which is exactly the bridge to Hall's reception theory: the same fractured text can be read as artful by one viewer and incoherent by another. When the question says 'explore how narrative positions audiences', you should reach for both the structure (Todorov, enigma) and the positioning (a preferred reading constructed by pacing, withheld information and point of view).

Two limits to acknowledge for evaluation marks. First, these theories were built on folktales and classic realist texts; applying them to interactive media (video games, where the player co-authors sequence) or multi-strand ensemble drama stretches them - say so, and score for critical distance. Second, narrative in advertising is subordinate to anchorage: the story exists to fix the product's connotations, so Todorov alone under-explains a text whose real mechanism is Barthes' myth-making. Structure your answer to apply, then complicate - the examiner is marking the quality of your thinking, not your ability to recite five stages.

Narrative in news. Front pages construct mini-narratives, and news values function as narrative selection: what counts as a story, who is the hero, who is the villain. The Mirror's recurring structure casts the ordinary Briton as protagonist betrayed by elites - a folktale shape (Propp) running through political coverage; The Times constructs slower narratives of process and expertise, where the institution (government, markets, courts) is the protagonist and the reader is the informed observer. Narrative selection is representation selection: whose story gets told, and from whose point of view, is the ideology question in its purest form - which is why narrative and representation questions frequently overlap and the strongest answers treat them as one analysis.

Propp's limits are gendered. His folktale roles harden gender: the princess is passive, the hero and villain male by default. Modern texts complicate this productively - Jordana in Submarine refuses the prize-position entirely; Alice Morgan in Luther occupies villain and helper functions with more intelligence than the hero; Claudia in the WaterAid advert is both the questing hero and the beneficiary, collapsing the donor/hero structure that charity advertising historically used (Western donor as hero, recipient as passive beneficiary). Noting that Propp's structure is gendered - and that texts which redistribute the functions are making a representational argument - is exactly the kind of cross-topic extension that marks A* narrative answers.

Narrative and ideology. Ask of any text: whose story is it, told from whose position, resolved in whose favour? Todorov's new equilibrium carries the answer: Submarine's ironic ending argues that self-narration fails; Straight Outta Compton's bittersweet equilibrium argues that the group's legacy outlives its exploitation; the McCain campaign's warm new equilibrium argues that family - whatever its shape - survives the week. And open endings do ideological work too: The Returned's refusal of closure is an argument that grief does not resolve. Open endings also fuel the online speculation that markets serialised texts between episodes (Jenkins' fandom behaviour as free marketing) - one more place where narrative, audience and industries meet in a single analysis.

Semiotics, technical codes and multimodality

Barthes' semiotics is the theoretical spine of media language analysis. A sign = signifier (the form the sign takes) + signified (the concept it represents). The order of signification runs from denotation (the literal, first-order meaning: a woman holding a bottle of Tide) to connotation (the second-order, cultural meaning: contented domestic femininity, the promise that love is expressed through laundry). Myth is Barthes' third-order operation: when a connotation hardens into 'common sense' - naturalised, invisible ideology. The Tide advert is a myth-machine in exactly this sense: it does not argue that women belong at the sink; it presents that arrangement as the natural order of a happy home, so the ideology arrives pre-packaged as realism. In the newspaper set products, the same operation is visible: the Mirror's colloquial headline register connotes 'one of us' and mythologises the tabloid reader as the ordinary Briton betrayed by elites; The Times' restrained register mythologises measured authority itself.

Anchorage completes the toolkit. Images are polysemic - open to multiple readings - so texts anchor them: captions, headlines, slogans and voiceover select one preferred meaning. The set WaterAid advert uses its voiceover and title cards to anchor images of a young woman walking for water to a message of empowerment and progress rather than passivity - a deliberate representational strategy worth citing in both language and representation answers. When analysing any print product, your first question should be: what could this image mean, and what has been done (typography, juxtaposition, caption) to pin it down?

Technical codes are where analysis becomes concrete. Revise them as decision-points, not as a glossary:

  • Camera: shot size controls intimacy (close-up = identification; extreme close-up = significance or threat); angle encodes power (low angle elevates, high angle diminishes); movement directs attention and generates energy; focus (shallow vs deep) tells the audience where meaning lives.
  • Editing: pace sets pulse - fast cutting for action and advertising, long takes for realism and dread (The Returned's refusal of urgency is itself a code). Continuity editing conceals construction (realist ideology); visible editing foregrounds it (postmodern play in Submarine). Cross-cutting builds causality between spaces; montage compresses time and argues by juxtaposition.
  • Sound: diegetic sound builds the world's texture; non-diegetic score tells the audience how to feel. Contrapuntal sound - music that clashes with the image - is the most examinable choice: it forces the audience to actively reconcile the conflict, generating meaning rather than receiving it.
  • Mise-en-scene: setting, costume, props, lighting, colour, blocking. High-key lighting and saturated colour in the Tide advert connote optimism and cleanliness; the low-key, sodium-lit streets of Luther connote moral murk; the muted rural palette of The Returned connotes suspension - a world where nothing can move forward.

Baudrillard extends semiotics into postmodernity: in simulacra, signs no longer refer to realities but to other signs, generating hyperreality - a condition more real than real. Media language is where this bites: Zoella's set channel is not a window onto a life but a curated assemblage of vlog conventions whose 'authenticity' is itself a produced code (Baudrillard would call it a simulacrum of intimacy). Advertising constructs hyperreal domesticity; reality formats construct hyperreal ordinariness; the newspaper front page constructs a hyperreal 'public mood'. For evaluation, note the counter-argument: audiences are not dupes, and can enjoy hyperreality knowingly - which loops back to active-audience theory and keeps your answer dialectical.

Multimodality is the conceptual frame for texts that combine modes: visual, verbal, aural, gestural and spatial. Kress and Van Leeuwen's 'grammar of visual design' gives you the vocabulary: images make representational meaning (what is depicted), interactive meaning (the gaze: a direct 'demand' gaze addresses and involves the viewer; an 'offer' gaze presents for detached contemplation) and compositional meaning (layout: salience, framing, reading paths). The Tide advert's central figure offers a direct demand gaze that interpellates the 1950s housewife-consumer; Zoella's thumbnails deploy demand gazes engineered for click-through; news photographs typically offer, preserving the pretence of detached authority. Interactive meaning is the fastest route from language analysis to representation analysis - the gaze is where the two theories physically meet.

Multimodality also explains platform affordances: what each mode and medium lets a producer do. Print combines typography, layout and still image but is static and linear. Websites add hypertext, navigation and mixed media, making the reading path user-selected (Attitude's site: masthead, navigation, cover-star exclusives, embedded video). Vlogs add the human face, voice and parasocial direct address. Video games add agency: the player's input is itself a mode, and meaning is produced through interaction rather than contemplation - the deepest challenge to purely semiotic analysis, since the 'text' differs playthrough by playthrough. In an exam answer, name the affordance, explain what it enables the producer to do to the audience, and evaluate what it forecloses: every affordance is also a constraint, and industries choose platforms for audience-reach reasons as much as creative ones (the synoptic bridge to industries and audiences).

Timed-analysis routine (30 seconds per product)

1. Denote: what is literally there? 2. Select the three most meaningful codes (one technical, one symbolic, one written). 3. Connote: what cultural meaning does each construct? 4. Ask: how does anchorage steer the reading? 5. Ask: which mode dominates, and what does the platform afford? 6. Evaluate: is the meaning naturalised as myth, and what ideology does the myth serve?

Close with the exam-craft point that matters most: precision beats quantity. A paragraph that names one editing choice exactly (match-on-action at the moment of recognition in Luther, say), explains its constructed meaning and its intended audience response, will outscore a page of vague 'camera angles show' assertions. Build the four-step chain into your muscle memory, keep Barthes and Baudrillard loaded as your evaluative artillery, and every media language question becomes the same disciplined move executed on new material.

Colour and typography are semiotic systems. Treat them as codes with connotation repertoires, not decoration. The Mirror's red masthead connotes urgency, passion, populism; The Times' blue connotes authority, trust, establishment permanence; the serif tradition of broadsheet type connotes heritage while condensed sans-serif connotes speed and accessibility. The WaterAid advert's sunlit palette connotes optimism and forward motion (anchoring its empowerment message), while the muted rural blues of The Returned connote emotional suspension. In advertising, colour is engineered to product: the saturated primary palette of the Tide advert makes cleanliness legible at a glance. Typography works the same way - a website's type hierarchy is a written code directing the reading path, and a poster's font choice anchors its register (institutional, playful, urgent) before a word is read.

Semiotics has limits - deploy them. Connotations are culturally variable: the same colour or gesture connotes differently across cultures and periods, so semiotic analysis is always an argument about a reading community, not a universal decoding. And audiences decode actively - a contrapuntal soundtrack or an ambiguous image does not inject a meaning but invites one, which is exactly the bridge to Hall's reception theory: the same text yields different readings from different social positions. The strongest media language answers acknowledge this: the producer constructs through codes, but the reading is completed by the audience - and the gap between the two is where most interesting analysis happens.

Two-product practice, one routine. For any pair (Mirror/Times, Tide/WaterAid, Luther/The Returned): denote both, select the three most meaningful codes in each, connote, check anchorage, then argue the contrast - what different version of reality does each construct, for which audience, in whose interests? Thirty seconds per product in planning, five paragraphs in writing: shared framework, contrast as argument, constructedness as conclusion. Drill it on three pairs and every compare question becomes the same disciplined move.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: Explore how media language is used to construct meanings and responses in the set newspaper front pages of the Daily Mirror and The Times. [12]
Mark-scheme logic: Mark-scheme logic (AO2 analysis of media language in the set products, with AO1 terminology): (1) Identify the form conventions of each front page - masthead, lead story, image, typography, price, promo straps. (2) The Mirror: red masthead and bold sans-serif headline connote urgency and populism; colloquial written code ('one of us' register) constructs a preferred reading aligned with its centre-left, ordinary-Briton news values; image choice dramatises emotion and anchors the story to human interest. (3) The Times: blue masthead, serif typography, restrained headline and multi-story layout connote authority, tradition and measured judgement; the anchorage of images with formal captions constructs the reader as informed citizen rather than entertained consumer. (4) Responses sought: Mirror seeks emotional identification and indignation; The Times seeks trust and deliberation - two constructed positions for two segmented audiences. (5) Top band: evaluate via Levi-Strauss (binary oppositions of elite/ordinary, tradition/modernity) and note that the same day's events are re-presented differently, proving constructedness.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Explain how genre conventions create audience pleasure in the set television product you have studied. [8]
Answer: Identify the genre and its conventions (Luther: crime procedural - crime scene, interrogation, enigma-driven investigation) and explain the pleasure of recognition (Neale: repetition). Show how the product introduces difference (hybridity with psychological thriller/horror: Alice Morgan as genius villain, expressionist lighting) creating novelty that sustains engagement (Neale: difference). Conclude that pleasure = the calibrated interplay of the two: familiar beats deliver security, deviation delivers surprise.
Explore how narrative is used in the set advertising and marketing products you have studied. [12]
Answer: Apply Todorov at advert scale: Tide runs a micro-arc (disharmony - product - domestic harmony) whose new equilibrium encodes the housewife myth; McCain's 'We Are Family' compresses equilibrium-disruption-repair into celebration of inclusive family mealtimes. Use Barthes: the narratives exist to anchor the product's connotations (cleanliness = love; chips = family togetherness), so myth, not story, is the real mechanism - a limit on Todorov worth noting for top band. Link to audience: the arcs construct the consumer as hero and product as donor (Propp), positioning purchase as resolution.
How useful is Todorov's narratology for understanding the set products you have studied? [25]
Answer: Apply: map Todorov's five stages across a range (Submarine's ironic new equilibrium; Straight Outta Compton's biopic arc; Luther's provisional weekly reset). Evaluate usefulness: strong for linear, single-protagonist texts; weaker for The Returned, which deliberately withholds repair - the violation itself generates meaning (unresolvable grief), showing the theory's limits produce insight when texts break it. Extend: serial and interactive forms complicate 'equilibrium' (episodic reset is an industrial convention). Conclude: useful as a starting framework whose failures are as diagnostic as its fits - the mark of a truly analytical use of theory.
Explain how Barthes' concepts of denotation, connotation and myth can be applied to the set products you have studied. [12]
Answer: Define the order of signification, then apply: Tide - a woman with laundry (denotation) becomes contented domestic femininity (connotation), naturalised as the common sense of the 1950s home (myth); WaterAid - a young woman walking for water (denotation) is anchored by voiceover to empowerment rather than victimhood, resisting the poverty-victim myth; Mirror front pages - colloquial headlines (denotation) connote populist solidarity and mythologise 'the ordinary Brit'. Evaluate: myth is ideological - it presents constructed, interested meanings as natural, so identifying it exposes the text's politics.
Explore how multimodality shapes audience response in the set online media products you have studied. [12]
Answer: Define modes and affordances (Kress and Van Leeuwen). Zoella: visual (thumbnail demand gaze), verbal (casual direct address), gestural (facial expression) combine to simulate intimacy and parasocial friendship, engineering response (loyalty, clicks); platform affordances (subscribe button, comments) convert response into participation. Attitude: magazine conventions (masthead, cover star) migrate into hypertext - navigation, embedded video, shareability - constructing a niche community reader. Evaluate: multimodality personalises response but also commodifies it; the 'authentic' mode-mix is itself a produced code (Baudrillard).
