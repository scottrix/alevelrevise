Media Industries

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level OCR, WJEC/Eduqas

A-Level Media Studies revision on media industries (OCR H409 Component 02; WJEC/Eduqas A680 Component 1): ownership and conglomeration, funding models, the BBC and public service broadcasting, regulation (Ofcom, BBFC, IPSO) and digital disruption, with Curran and Seaton, Livingstone and Lunt, and Hesmondhalgh applied.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain vertical and horizontal integration, conglomeration and synergy with named examples (AO1)
  • Compare funding models (licence fee, advertising, subscription, tax relief, microtransactions) and their consequences for content (AO2)
  • Evaluate the BBC's public service remit, charter framework and funding under political and market pressure (AO2)
  • Map the UK regulatory landscape (Ofcom, BBFC, IPSO, ASA, PEGI/VSC) and its difficulties in the digital age (AO2)
  • Apply Curran and Seaton, Hesmondhalgh and Livingstone and Lunt to the set products and current industries (AO2)
  • Assess how digital disruption has transformed production, distribution, funding and regulation (AO3)

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Vertical integration: One company owning multiple stages of the supply chain - production, distribution and exhibition (e.g. Disney owning studios, streaming and parks), maximising profit at every stage.
Horizontal integration: One company owning many competitors at the same stage (e.g. Reach plc owning the Daily Mirror, Daily Express and Daily Star), concentrating market share and cross-promoting.
Conglomeration: The absorption of media companies into diversified conglomerates, where media sit alongside other businesses; the standard structure of the global 'Big Five' film studios.
Synergy: Cross-promotion of linked products across a conglomerate's brands so that the combined value exceeds the parts (a film franchise generating games, soundtracks and merchandise).
Public service broadcasting (PSB): Broadcasting required to serve citizens as well as consumers - impartial news, distinctive UK content, serving all audiences - historically justified as correcting market failure.
Licence fee: The BBC's principal funding stream: a flat annual charge set by government (currently around 174 pounds, index-linked) paid by all households watching live TV or iPlayer.
Watershed: Ofcom's 9pm broadcasting boundary before which material unsuitable for children must not air; the flagship example of content regulation protecting audiences.
Self-regulation: An industry regulating itself through a voluntary code - the press model (IPSO and the Editors' Code) - criticised as weak where the regulator is funded by the regulated.

📌 Key Points

Curran and Seaton: concentrated ownership prioritises profit over pluralism - less diversity, weaker democracy.
Hesmondhalgh: cultural industries are risk-averse; 'independent' labels are often brands owned by majors.
Livingstone and Lunt: regulators face a dilemma - protect or enable choice - sharpened by digital convergence.
BBC: Reith's inform, educate, entertain; charter-funded licence fee; Ofcom-regulated since 2017.
Digital disruption: streaming, piracy and Web 2.0 dismantled the vertical, stable, analogue industry model.

Ownership, conglomeration and funding

Ownership is the exam's causal question: who owns the media shapes what gets made, how it is distributed, and whose versions of the world circulate. Start with the two structural patterns. Vertical integration means owning the chain: production, distribution and exhibition. Disney is the textbook case - studios (Disney, Pixar, Marvel, Lucasfilm, 20th Century Studios after the 71-billion-dollar Fox acquisition of 2019), the Disney+ streaming platform, and downstream merchandise and parks - each stage extracts margin and each cross-promotes the others. Horizontal integration means owning the competition at one stage: Reach plc (formerly Trinity Mirror) owns the Daily Mirror, Daily Express, Daily Star and dozens of regional titles, concentrating press market share and enabling shared content. Conglomeration is the umbrella pattern: media absorbed into diversified empires alongside non-media business, stabilising risk across sectors.

The set products make ownership concrete. The Daily Mirror sits inside Reach's portfolio - one newsroom's output shared across titles, shaping news values and cost structures - while The Times belongs to News UK (Rupert Murdoch's UK arm, alongside The Sun): an ownership position that historically shaped its political editorialising and its proximity to power. Straight Outta Compton was financed and distributed by Universal, a Comcast-owned major: the film's 160-million-dollar-plus box office and its carefully staged marketing campaign demonstrate conglomerate scale, while its subject matter (Black counter-cultural anger at policing) shows the tension Hesmondhalgh identifies - radical content, corporate packaging. Submarine is the counter-case: Warp Films, a genuinely independent UK production company, co-produced with Film4 - illustrating how the indie sector survives at the margins of the majors, and how 'indie' as an aesthetic (lo-fi, arthouse, British) is partly an economic category (low budgets, public subsidy, niche distribution).

Funding models determine content, because every funding stream buys a different master. Map them:

Hesmondhalgh ('The Cultural Industries') is your theory of why industries look like this: cultural industries are risky (nobody knows which film will hit), so they manage risk through stars, genres, franchises and format repetition, through horizontal and vertical integration, and through the deliberate construction of 'independent' brands - many of which are owned by, or distributed by, the majors. Hesmondhalgh also names the industry's central contradiction: cultural production is valued for creativity and self-expression, yet organised for profit - which is why genuinely innovative output tends to be bought up (and blanded) or left to the subsidised margins. Apply him to Straight Outta Compton (biopic genre = risk management; N.W.A's story = brand) and to Submarine (Warp = the indie exception that proves the pattern).

Curran and Seaton ('Power Without Responsibility') supply the critical frame: media power has historically been concentrated in few hands, and their argument is that concentrated ownership driven by profit motive produces limited variety, creativity and quality - media serve capital before citizens, weakening the pluralism a democracy needs. Their historical evidence is the UK press: barons and their politics. The evaluation the exam wants: digital markets complicate the claim - distribution barriers have collapsed (anyone can publish, stream, self-distribute), so voices have diversified even as ownership concentrated. But the counter-counter-argument is strong: platform gatekeepers (Google, Meta, Amazon, Netflix) are themselves new conglomerates whose algorithms perform the gatekeeping once done by moguls - concentration has migrated rather than dissolved. Carry that dialectic into any 'ownership' essay and you have a 25-mark structure.

Synergy is the operational payoff of conglomeration: the franchise model. A tentpole film generates sequels, a streaming series, games, soundtracks, merchandise and theme-park presence - each arm markets the others. The set products gesture at it: Straight Outta Compton's campaign included the 'Straight Outta Compton' meme generator, a Web 2.0 synergy tactic that turned the audience into unpaid marketers (fold in Jenkins and Gauntlett here for synoptic credit). Zoella's set channel is synergy in miniature: vlog, book deals, product ranges, each platform feeding the brand.

Exam method: ownership questions

1. Define the structure (vertical/horizontal/conglomeration) with a named example. 2. Anchor it in the set product (Mirror/Reach; Times/News UK; Submarine/Warp; Straight Outta Compton/Universal). 3. Apply the theorist: Hesmondhalgh (risk, branding) and/or Curran and Seaton (profit vs pluralism). 4. Evaluate with the digital counter-argument and its rebuttal. 5. Conclude on consequences for content and audiences - ownership is only interesting for what it does to what we watch.

Keep one distinction sharp for AO1: conglomeration (owning across sectors) versus globalisation (distribution across borders). Disney is both; Netflix is globalised without owning theme parks; Warp is neither. And remember the audience consequence that examiners love: concentrated ownership narrows the range of versions of the world available - which is Hall's representation theory seen from the producer's side. Industries and representation are one system.

Conglomeration across sectors. The pattern is not film-specific. Music: the 'big three' majors - Universal, Sony, Warner - control the majority of global recorded-music revenue, so the independent label that breaks an artist is typically distributed (and often owned) by one of them: Hesmondhalgh's 'independent branding' in operation. Publishing: a handful of conglomerates (Penguin Random House, HarperCollins, Hachette, Macmillan) dominate trade publishing. Streaming: Netflix, Amazon and Disney are the new conglomerates - platforms whose scale lets them outbid broadcasters and whose algorithms perform the gatekeeping schedulers once did. Keep one distinction sharp: conglomeration (owning across sectors) differs from globalisation (distributing across borders) - Disney is both; Netflix is globalised without owning theme parks; Warp is neither. The set products let you anchor the scale: Straight Outta Compton inside Universal's chain, Submarine outside it, the Mirror and The Times inside rival press portfolios.

The independent sector. The counter-case deserves detail. Warp Films (Submarine) co-produced with Film4, a publicly owned film funder; UK film tax relief (up to 25% of qualifying spend) and BFI funding are the state's subsidy layer - the reason a UK indie sector exists at all. The examinable pattern: independents supply the innovation, majors or funders absorb the risk, and 'independent' as an aesthetic (lo-fi, British, arthouse) is partly an economic category - low budgets, niche distribution, subsidy. Hesmondhalgh's sharpest point lands here: the industry's creativity/profit contradiction means genuinely innovative output either gets bought up and blanded or survives at the subsidised margins. Submarine is the survival case: modest budget, stylised choices that read as art, cultural longevity out of proportion to its box office.

The audience-side of ownership. Exam questions often ask the consequences for audiences, and the answer is Hall's theory seen from the producer's side: concentrated ownership narrows the range of versions of the world available. One company controlling many titles can set the news agenda across them (Reach's shared newsrooms; News UK's cross-title politics); one conglomerate controlling franchises decides which stories get made into games, series and merchandise. The counter-argument runs through distribution: barriers to entry have collapsed, so voices have diversified even as ownership concentrated - but platform gatekeepers re-concentrate attention. Run that dialectic and any ownership question becomes a 25-mark structure: definition, set-product evidence, digital counter-argument, rebuttal, consequences for audiences.

Public service broadcasting and the BBC

Public service broadcasting is the UK's distinctive institutional settlement and a guaranteed essay topic. The rationale is market failure: commercial markets underserve citizens - impartial news, minority output, quality children's television and experimental drama are unprofitable - so broadcasting is licensed to serve the citizen, not just the consumer. Reith's founding mission - inform, educate, entertain - still structures the BBC's public purposes under the 2017 Royal Charter (running to the end of 2027): impartial news, support for learning, distinctive creative output, reflecting UK diversity, serving the nations and regions, and bringing the UK to the world.

What makes the BBC examinable is that everything about it is contested. Funding: the licence fee - a flat annual charge set by government (around 174 pounds, index-linked) - is the industry's most distinctive revenue model and its most attacked. The attacks: regressive (a flat tax on owning a television), coercively enforced, and increasingly escapeable as audiences move to streaming. The defences (deploy Curran and Seaton here): it is the only funding model that buys independence - no shareholder demanding mass appeal, no advertiser demanding brand safety - which is precisely what funds risky, distinctive output the market would not. The over-75s free-licence policy (transferred to the BBC itself in 2020, with means-testing) is the standing example of government shifting costs onto the corporation - evidence for the argument that the licence fee settlement is politically unstable.

Regulation: since 2017 the BBC is regulated by Ofcom - its first external regulator - which enforces impartiality, fair dealing and the public purposes. This is a rich exam detail: the PSB is simultaneously the most regulated broadcaster (charter duties, Ofcom enforcement, editorial guidelines) and the least commercially driven, making it the test case for the whole regulation debate. When a question asks whether regulation is too strong or too weak, the BBC lets you argue both: strong enough to constrain editorial freedom, structurally too weak to force distinctiveness in a streaming market.

The set BBC Radio 1 Breakfast Show is the PSB case study to apply. Radio 1 targets 16-24s - a demographic commercial radio underserves or serves with pure pop - and the Breakfast Show's mix of music, Newsbeat bulletins, comedy features and presenter personality (Greg James since 2018) is PSB logic at platform scale: entertainment as the sugar coating for the public purposes (inform, educate). Apply the industrial analysis: Radio 1 competes with Spotify's algorithmic playlists and commercial rivals like Capital, and its response is distinctiveness - live presenters, public-service content, personality-led scheduling - which is exactly the charter's language. The BBC's own digital pivot (BBC Sounds consolidating radio and podcasts) shows a public service institution borrowing streaming-industry tactics to defend a licence-fee-funded remit. That tension - PSB mission vs platform economics - is the paragraph examiners want to see.

  • PSB beyond the BBC: ITV and Channel 4 carry statutory obligations (Channel 4 is publicly owned but advertising-funded and commissioning-only - a hybrid worth citing for AO1 precision).
  • The market-failure argument's counter: streaming and digital radio now serve niche tastes profitably, weakening the claim that only PSB can fund diversity (Livingstone and Lunt's convergence point).
  • The defence's counter: streaming serves niches through subscription choice, not universal obligation - no equivalent of impartial news or universally available children's content.
  • Privatisation debates: recurring proposals to shrink or sell the BBC; the exam wants you to test each against the public purposes, not just the price tag.

The funding-model comparison is the analytical heart. Licence fee vs advertising vs subscription buys three different media: the first buys citizenship, the second buys audiences-for-sale, the third buys retention. Apply to set products: Radio 1 (licence fee - distinctiveness duties), commercial radio and press (advertising - mass reach), The Returned (Canal+ subscription origin - prestige drama for subscribers, then acquired by Channel 4 for UK PSB-adjacent broadcast: a case that shows both models cooperating). Luther is the licence fee's flagship argument: risky, distinctive, star-led serial drama that a commercial scheduler would strand. The exam question 'is the licence fee still justified?' wants exactly this: define PSB, apply the purposes to named output, run the market-failure debate both ways, and conclude on what a post-licence-fee BBC would lose that a market would not replace.

BBC exam paragraph: the dialectic in miniature

Claim: the BBC's licence fee buys independence that no commercial model can. Evidence: Radio 1's distinctiveness; Luther's risky commissioning; impartial Newsbeat. Counter: streaming serves niches profitably; the fee is regressive and escapeable; charter obligations are unenforceable against platform economics. Rebuttal: subscription buys choice, not universality; no commercial model funds impartial news at scale. Conclusion: the settlement is politically fragile but the market-failure argument retains force - Curran and Seaton's pluralism case, updated for streaming.

Close with the synoptic frame: PSB connects to ownership (public ownership as the alternative to conglomeration), to regulation (the BBC as the most regulated broadcaster), and to audiences (universality vs segmentation - the licence fee obliges service to everyone, which is also why Radio 1 must chase the youth demographic other broadcasters can ignore). When the extended question lands on the BBC, structure it as mission - funding - regulation - threat, with the set products as evidence at every stage.

Radio 1 as a case in detail. The Breakfast Show's schedule logic is PSB strategy at platform scale: music (the diversion layer), Newsbeat bulletins (the surveillance layer - youth-focused news a commercial station would not fund at the same frequency), features and comedy (the engagement layer), and presenter personality (Greg James since 2018 - the para-social bond that builds the morning ritual). The competition frame: Spotify's algorithmic playlists serve pure diversion with zero public-service content; commercial rivals like Capital serve the same demographic with advertising-funded pop. Radio 1's answer is distinctiveness - the charter's own word - and it is the examinable point: distinctiveness is what a licence-fee-funded institution owes its funders that an algorithm does not owe anyone. The BBC's Sounds platform (consolidating radio and podcasts) shows the same institution borrowing streaming tactics to defend a licence-fee remit - PSB mission vs platform economics in one case study.

The BBC under pressure. The settlement's instability is examinable evidence. Charter renewal (the current charter runs to the end of 2027) arrives with periodic debates over funding: subscription models, advertising on some services, or a levy on streaming subscriptions have all been proposed as licence-fee alternatives. The over-75s free licence (transferred from government to the BBC in 2020, with means-testing) is the standing example of cost-shifting onto the corporation. Politicisation claims run alongside: governments of both parties have periodically attacked BBC coverage, and the impartiality duties cut both ways - they protect the corporation's independence while constraining its editorial freedom. The A* structure: for every attack on the licence fee, test it against the public purposes, not just the price tag - what would a market-funded BBC stop making that nothing else would replace?

PSB values across the set products. The licence fee's flagship argument is Luther: risky, star-led, morally complex serial drama that a commercial scheduler would strand - public money funding the distinctive. The Returned shows the models cooperating: prestige drama originating at Canal+ (French subscription broadcasting), acquired by Channel 4 for UK broadcast - curation as a PSB-adjacent service. The Big Issue extends the logic beyond broadcasting: a street paper owned as a social enterprise, where vendors buy copies and sell them - alternative ownership models exist outside the licence-fee/conglomerate binary, and citing one shows the examiner you can think structurally rather than just rhetorically. When the question lands on 'is public service broadcasting still necessary?', run mission - funding - regulation - threat with these set products as evidence at every stage.

Regulation and digital disruption

The UK regulatory map - know it precisely, board-exactly. Ofcom (under the Communications Act 2003) regulates broadcasting: impartiality rules, the 9pm watershed, fairness and accuracy, and since 2017 the BBC's charter duties. Its newest and largest mandate is the Online Safety Act 2023: Ofcom now polices platforms' duties on illegal content and children's safety, including age-assurance for pornography - the state's answer to convergence. BBFC classifies film (U, PG, 12A, 12, 15, 18) and increasingly online video; its classifications are advisory for cinema but locally enforceable, and its guidelines are a standing debate (12A criteria, streaming ratings). Video games in the UK are classified by the VSC Rating Board using PEGI labels (12, 16, 18) - legally enforceable since the Digital Economy Act - a detail examiners reward because candidates confuse BBFC and PEGI. The press is self-regulated: IPSO (and the Editors' Code) since 2014, the Leveson Inquiry's aftermath - the classic criticism being that a regulator funded by the regulated is structurally weak (the phone-hacking scandal is the case study). Advertising is regulated by the ASA under the CAP/BCAP codes, with pre-clearance for broadcast ads.

Livingstone and Lunt are the set theory of regulation, and their argument is a dilemma: regulation must balance protecting citizens (especially children) against enabling consumer choice and free expression - and digital convergence has sharpened the dilemma to breaking point. Their research found regulators themselves uncertain: content flows across borders and platforms faster than any national regulator, and 'protection' increasingly means media literacy and platform-level tools rather than broadcast-style rules. Apply them precisely: the watershed protects linear television but is meaningless on-demand (streaming services self-rate; the BBFC's ratings apply only by agreement); IPSO governs UK newspapers but not global social platforms; PEGI governs purchased games but not the live social spaces around them. The Online Safety Act 2023 is the state's attempt to close exactly this gap - evidence that Livingstone and Lunt's diagnosis was right, and evidence of the scale of the problem (platform duties, fines, age assurance: regulation migrating from content to systems).

Digital disruption is the context that reframes everything else. The stable analogue model - vertical integration, scheduled broadcast, physical distribution, national borders - has been dismantled by five forces:

  • Streaming: Netflix and rivals replaced scheduling with on-demand, subscription with attention economics; prestige output (the box set) and data-driven commissioning followed. The Returned's journey (Canal+ subscription to Channel 4 acquisition) maps the transition.
  • Piracy and disaggregation: digital copying collapsed scarcity pricing in music and film, forcing legal streaming as the industry's response to its own disruption.
  • Web 2.0 and convergence: audiences became producers (Jenkins' convergence culture; Gauntlett's 'make is connecting'; Shirky's 'end of the audience'), destabilising the professional/amateur boundary that regulation assumed. Zoella is the set-product proof: an individual vlogger outperforming institutions in youth reach.
  • Print decline: advertising migrated online, gutting newspaper revenue; the Mirror and Times survived by paywalls, click-driven online strategies and cross-title consolidation within Reach and News UK.
  • Platform gatekeeping: Google, Meta and Amazon replaced editors and schedulers as the gatekeepers of attention - concentration migrated from moguls to algorithms, and regulation followed (Online Safety Act).

Apply the theorists across the disruption. Curran and Seaton: the disruption has not dissolved concentrated power - it has re-concentrated it in platforms, while the long tail of niches flourishes at the margins; their pluralism argument now targets algorithmic gatekeeping as much as press barons. Hesmondhalgh: cultural industries adapted rather than collapsed - aggregation, curation and platform intermediation are the new risk-management; 'independent' branding persists (Warp's aesthetic survives at the margins; Submarine remains the case study of subsidised indie sustainability). Livingstone and Lunt: regulation is migrating from content rules to platform duties and media literacy - their dilemma (protect vs enable) now runs through every Online Safety Act debate about encryption, age assurance and free expression.

Regulation: the exam dichotomy bank

Too weak: press self-regulation after phone hacking; unregulated global platforms; watershed bypassed on-demand. Too strong: prior restraint and censorship risks (BBFC cuts); chill effects on innovation; paternalism vs audience choice. Migrating: content rules to platform duties (Online Safety Act); enforcement to age assurance and fines; protection to media literacy (Livingstone and Lunt). Use all three columns in any 'is regulation fit for the digital age?' essay.

The exam-craft structure for industries extended answers: context - theory - evidence - evaluation. Open with the industrial context (concentration, funding, disruption), apply the named theorist whose argument fits the question, evidence with the set products (Mirror/Reach, Times/News UK, Radio 1/BBC, Submarine/Warp, Straight Outta Compton/Universal, The Returned/Canal+), then evaluate against the digital counter-arguments. The single most valuable habit: treat every industries question as a power question - who owns, who funds, who regulates, who profits - because that is the thread that ties this topic to representation and audiences and gives the examiner the synoptic architecture they are marking for.

BBFC in detail. The categories (U, PG, 12A, 12, 15, 18) gate cinema and physical media; 12A is the examinable nuance - parental discretion for under-12s, a judgement call encoded into the classification itself. Cuts are now rare (the BBFC's modern posture is information-led rather than censorious), and its classifications apply to streaming services only by agreement - a Livingstone and Lunt case in miniature: regulation by consent where convergence has outflanked compulsion. For age-restricted online content, the Digital Economy Act 2017's verification scheme was abandoned before implementation - the Online Safety Act 2023 superseded it with platform-level duties (age assurance for adult content, children's safety codes, illegal-content duties enforced by Ofcom with substantial fines). That legislative history is examinable gold: regulation migrating from content classification to platform systems, exactly the shift Livingstone and Lunt predicted.

The press: IPSO and Leveson. The phone-hacking scandal (2011) triggered the Leveson Inquiry, which recommended a new regulator backed by law; the industry instead established IPSO (2014) - self-regulation under the Editors' Code, with no compulsory arbitration. The structural criticism is as old as self-regulation: a regulator funded by the regulated is weak where the regulated have most to lose. The debate cuts both ways for the exam: statutory regulation risks state interference in a free press (the liberal objection); self-regulation risks impunity for the powerful (the democratic objection - Curran and Seaton's territory). Either way, the press remains the least-regulated major medium in the UK - a fact worth citing in any regulation essay, because it is the exception that tests every rule.

Exam craft: the 'fit for purpose' essay. 'Is media regulation fit for the digital age?' - run the three-column structure. Too weak: press self-regulation after phone hacking; unregulated global platforms; watershed bypassed on-demand. Too strong: prior restraint and censorship risks; chill effects on innovation; paternalism vs audience choice. Migrating: content rules to platform duties (Online Safety Act); enforcement to age assurance and fines; protection to media literacy (Livingstone and Lunt). Apply to set products where they fit (PEGI for games; the watershed against Luther's pre-watershed scheduling debates; BBFC ratings for streaming), and conclude on the mechanism: regulation is adapting from policing content to policing systems - which is exactly the migration Livingstone and Lunt theorised before it happened.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: Discuss the relevance of Curran and Seaton's ideas about ownership and control to the media industries you have studied. [OCR H409 Component 02 style]
Mark-scheme logic: Mark-scheme logic (AO2 application of theory to industries, with AO1 knowledge and AO3 evaluation): (1) State the theory: concentrated ownership driven by profit motive produces limited variety, creativity and pluralism - media serve capital before citizens. (2) Apply to set products: Mirror/Reach and Times/News UK press concentration and shared news values; Straight Outta Compton as Universal-packaged counter-cultural content; Submarine/Warp as the subsidised indie alternative. (3) Evidence beyond set products: Big Five studio conglomeration, Disney's vertical integration, press ownership and political alignment. (4) Evaluate: digital distribution has lowered barriers (diversified voices despite concentrated ownership) - but platform gatekeepers (Google, Meta, Netflix) re-concentrate power through algorithms, so Curran and Seaton's logic migrates rather than expires. (5) Conclude: their claim remains a powerful lens on ownership-concentration, updated for the platform age.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Explain how vertical and horizontal integration shape the media industries you have studied. [12]
Answer: Define both. Vertical: Disney owns production (studios), distribution (Disney+) and exhibition-adjacent arms, extracting margin at each stage and cross-promoting (synergy). Horizontal: Reach plc owns Mirror, Express, Star - shared newsrooms, concentrated press share, cost efficiency. Apply to set products: Straight Outta Compton inside Universal's chain; Mirror inside Reach's portfolio. Conclude: integration concentrates power over what is made and how it reaches audiences - the industrial logic behind Curran and Seaton's pluralism concern.
Is the licence fee still the best way to fund public service broadcasting? In your answer refer to the BBC. [25]
Answer: Define PSB (market failure rationale; inform, educate, entertain; charter purposes). Evidence for: buys independence - Radio 1's distinctiveness for underserved 16-24s, Luther's risky drama, impartial Newsbeat; no shareholder or advertiser master. Evidence against: regressive flat charge; escapeable via streaming; over-75s cost-shift shows political instability; charter duties hard to enforce in a streaming market. Evaluate both ways (Livingstone and Lunt: convergence weakens broadcast-style settlement). Conclude: subscription buys choice, not universality - the market-failure argument retains force, but the settlement is politically fragile.
How useful is Livingstone and Lunt's theory of regulation for understanding the media today? [25]
Answer: State the dilemma: protect citizens vs enable choice, sharpened by convergence. Apply: watershed meaningless on-demand; IPSO governs press not platforms; PEGI governs purchased games not live social spaces; Online Safety Act 2023 shifts Ofcom to platform duties and age assurance - regulation migrating from content to systems, exactly their diagnosis. Evaluate: prescient on convergence and media literacy; but their framework predates platform-scale gatekeeping and AI-generated content. Conclude: highly useful as a diagnostic of the protection/choice dilemma; the machinery of regulation has had to change around it.
Explain how digital disruption has changed the newspaper industry. Refer to the set newspaper products you have studied. [12]
Answer: Context: advertising migrated online, gutting print revenue. Responses: paywalls (The Times), click-driven online strategy and cross-title consolidation (Mirror inside Reach), live blogs and social distribution. Consequences: speed over depth, click economics shaping news values, reach concentrated in portfolio owners. Evaluate: print decline is revenue decline more than readership decline (online audiences grew) - but the funding model that once bought investigative depth is weakened, the Curran and Seaton concern in digital form.
How far do Hesmondhalgh's ideas about cultural industries explain the success of the set film products you have studied? [25]
Answer: State: cultural industries manage risk through genres, stars, franchises, integration and 'independent' branding; creativity vs profit is their central contradiction. Apply: Straight Outta Compton - biopic genre (risk management), N.W.A as brand, Universal distribution (160m+ box office), meme-generator marketing converting audience into marketers. Submarine - Warp's indie sustainability via Film4 co-production and public subsidy: the exception at the margins. Evaluate: Hesmondhalgh explains packaging and risk well; less good at predicting which counter-cultural content majors will co-opt. Conclude: strongly applicable - both films demonstrate the contradiction he names, one from the conglomerate side, one from the indie margin.
