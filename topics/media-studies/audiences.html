Audiences

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level OCR, WJEC/Eduqas

A-Level Media Studies revision on audiences (OCR H409 Component 02; WJEC/Eduqas A680 Component 1): Hall's encoding/decoding, uses and gratifications (Blumler and Katz), fandom (Jenkins), effects debates (Bandura, Gerbner), segmentation and the regulation of audiences, applied to the set products.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Apply Hall's encoding/decoding model to explain preferred, negotiated and oppositional readings of set products (AO2)
  • Apply Blumler and Katz's four gratifications to explain why audiences consume named products (AO2)
  • Use Jenkins to analyse fandom, participatory culture and transmedia behaviour (AO2)
  • Evaluate the effects debate: hypodermic syringe, Bandura's social learning and Gerbner's cultivation against active-audience evidence (AO2/AO3)
  • Analyse audience segmentation (demographic, psychographic) and its industrial logic (AO2)
  • Assess how and why audiences are regulated: BBFC, PEGI, watershed, Online Safety Act (AO2)

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Encoding/decoding: Hall's model (1973): producers encode a text with a preferred meaning through the theoretical framework; audiences decode it from social positions as preferred, negotiated or oppositional.
Preferred reading: The decoding the producer intends - the text's dominant ideology accepted as natural by the audience.
Negotiated reading: A decoding that broadly accepts the text's framework but modifies it against the reader's own experience or interests.
Oppositional reading: A decoding that recognises the preferred meaning and deliberately rejects or inverts it - the audience 'reads against the grain'.
Uses and gratifications: Blumler and Katz's active-audience model (1974): audiences use media to gratify four needs - surveillance/information, personal identity, personal relationships, diversion/entertainment.
Cultivation theory: Gerbner's claim that heavy, long-term television viewing cultivates a distorted worldview - 'mean world syndrome' - on average, across large audiences.
Moral panic: Cohen's term for exaggerated media-driven anxiety about a group ('folk devils') constructed as a threat to social values, producing disproportionate response.
Participatory culture: Jenkins' term for the Web 2.0 condition in which audiences produce, share and rework media rather than merely consuming - fandom, remix, fan fiction, memes.

📌 Key Points

Hall: producers cannot guarantee readings - decoding is a social act, positioned by age, culture and politics.
Blumler and Katz: audiences actively use media for surveillance, identity, relationships and diversion.
Jenkins: fandom is participatory - 'textual poachers' rework texts; convergence enables transmedia storytelling.
Bandura (social learning) vs Gauntlett (audiences as reflexive): the effects debate is the exam's classic dialectic.
Audience regulation is protection logic: BBFC, PEGI, watershed, Online Safety Act - test it with Livingstone and Lunt.

Encoding/decoding and reception

Hall's encoding/decoding model (1973) is the exam's most productive audience theory because it dissolves the passive/active argument in one move. Producers encode a text with a preferred meaning using the whole theoretical framework - media language, framing, anchorage, casting - but encoding is not transmission: audiences decode from their own social positions (age, class, culture, politics, lived experience), producing one of three readings. A preferred reading accepts the dominant ideology as natural; a negotiated reading accepts the framework but modifies it ('I see the message, but for me...'); an oppositional reading recognises the preferred meaning and deliberately rejects or inverts it. The theoretical point that earns marks: readings are socially positioned, not individual whims - Hall ties decoding to class and culture, which is why the same text produces patterned, not random, disagreements.

Apply the model to the set products with precision. The Tide advert encodes the 1950s housewife settlement as the natural happy order; its contemporary audience most likely decodes oppositionally or with heavy irony (the stereotype is dated and viewers know it) - a perfect demonstration that preferred readings decay as social context moves. The Daily Mirror and The Times front pages encode political positions, and their readerships decode largely in agreement - but the same front page read by the other paper's audience invites negotiated or oppositional readings: reception is structured by prior political identity, exactly Hall's point. The WaterAid advert encodes empowerment-through-donation; a negotiated reading ('good cause, but why is the West always the rescuer?') and an oppositional one (Gilroy-style critique of the charity gaze) are both patterned responses worth citing. Straight Outta Compton encodes N.W.A as sympathetic tragic heroes; American audiences decoded differently by race and by memory of the period - reception history for the film was itself divided along those lines.

  • The Returned: subtitled, slow-burn, ambiguous - encodes artful restraint; mass-audience decodings find it 'boring', niche audiences decode it as prestige. Reception here maps onto segmentation and cultural capital.
  • Luther: encodes a compromised-hero morality; negotiated readings accept the crime-thriller frame but question the vigilante glamour - a patterned, not accidental, divergence.
  • Zoella: encodes authenticity and intimacy; oppositional readings decry commodified 'relatability' - the authenticity critique is itself a recognisable decoding position.
  • The Big Issue: encodes vendor voice and dignity; oppositional readings can reject the entrepreneurial framing of homelessness - showing no text controls its readings.

The model's power in the exam is the causal chain it licenses: why do oppositional readings happen? Because decoding is social; because the audience brings context the producer cannot control; because anchorage can steer but not compel; because ideology is never invisible to everyone (Gramsci's hegemony requires consent, and consent can be withheld). When a question says 'explain how audiences may respond differently', that chain - encode, position, decode, justify with a set product - is the whole answer's skeleton.

Two evaluative refinements. First, Hall's model is often reduced to a three-box checklist - avoid that. The 1973 paper's point is that encoding and decoding are differentially structured moments in one communicative circuit, and 'distortion' between them is the norm, not the exception. Second, reception is measurable: streaming data, comment sentiment, review aggregates and social reactions give industries (and examiners' examples) concrete evidence of divergent readings - which links reception to the industrial logic of audience measurement (see Section 2).

Exam method: the reception chain

1. State the encoding: what preferred meaning does the set product construct, through which codes? 2. Identify the social positions that shape decoding (age, culture, politics). 3. Give patterned readings - preferred, negotiated, oppositional - each argued, not asserted. 4. Explain why the divergence happens (social positioning, historical distance). 5. Evaluate: the producer cannot guarantee readings; anchorage steers but does not compel (Barthes); consent can be withheld (Gramsci).

Close with the framework link: encoding/decoding connects to representation (a contested representation is precisely what produces oppositional readings) and to industries (audience measurement turns reception into data that feeds back into commissioning). Hall's reception model is the hinge between the audience topic and everything else on the specification - deploy it that way and extended answers write themselves.

Encoding in detail. Encoding happens through the whole framework: codes, casting, framing, anchorage, platform and scheduling all select the preferred meaning. But encoding manages polysemy rather than eliminating it - texts are always open to more than one reading, and anchorage (Barthes) steers without compelling. This is why Hall's model describes distortion as the norm: the meaning reconstructed by the audience rarely matches the meaning encoded exactly, and the exam rewards candidates who treat that gap as the object of analysis rather than a failure of communication. When a question asks why audiences respond differently, the mechanism is this gap plus the social positions that fill it - never a simple 'some people disagree'.

The social positions that shape decoding. Be specific about the variables: age (a 17-year-old and a 60-year-old decode the same front page differently), class and cultural capital (the niche prestige of The Returned vs its mass-audience 'boring'), gender (who identifies with the Tide woman's competence), ethnicity (who the WaterAid advert addresses, and who it positions as rescuer), political identity (Mirror vs Times readerships), and historical distance (the Tide advert's preferred reading has decayed - its contemporary audience decodes with irony). Hall's point is that these variables produce patterned divergences, not random ones: reading positions are social facts. The strongest answers name the position, argue the reading, and show the mechanism - three moves, not one assertion.

Community readings. Decoding is also collective: fandoms and communities share reading positions, and online platforms make the sharing visible. Fan communities reach consensus interpretations (and police deviant ones); comment sections and social reactions cluster around negotiated or oppositional positions; subcultures develop oppositional readings of mainstream texts as a group practice (hooks' oppositional gaze is precisely this, at the level of race and gender). The industrial consequence: community readings are measurable now - sentiment analysis, comment patterns, review aggregates - and feed back into commissioning and marketing. Reception is no longer private: it is data, and the data shapes what gets made next.

Exam craft: formats and openings. OCR papers use short 'explain' questions (8 marks), 'explore' questions (12) and extended 'evaluate/discuss' questions (25); Eduqas structures Component 1 across media products, industries and audiences. Whatever the format, the reception chain is the skeleton: encode (what preferred meaning, through which codes), position (which social variables), decode (preferred/negotiated/oppositional, each argued), mechanism (why the divergence - social positioning, historical distance), evaluate (producers cannot compel readings; consent can be withheld - Gramsci). Model opening sentence: 'The Tide advert encodes the 1950s domestic settlement as natural, but its contemporary audience's ironic decoding is itself a social position - historical distance is a decoding variable Hall's model predicts.' That is a top-band opening in one sentence.

Uses and gratifications, segmentation and fandom

Blumler and Katz (1974) are the active-audience model: audiences use media to gratify four needs - surveillance (information about the world), personal identity (comparing and constructing self), personal relationships (companionship, social currency, parasocial bonds) and diversion (escape, entertainment). The theory's exam value is that it explains consumption choices, not effects: the audience selects the product, and the industry competes for that selection. Apply it to the set products fast: the Radio 1 Breakfast Show is a uses-and-gratifications machine for 16-24s - surveillance (Newsbeat), diversion (features, presenter comedy), relationships (the para-social bond with Greg James, the morning ritual shared with peers) - and its distinctiveness is precisely its multi-gratification bundle against Spotify's pure diversion. GQ gratifies personal identity (the constructed aspirational self) and social currency. Zoella gratifies relationships and identity (intimacy, relatability, advice) - the vlog's entire grammar is engineered around those two needs. Video games gratify diversion and, increasingly, identity and community (live-service play as social space).

The theory's limits are also examinable: it explains why people choose media but not what those choices do to them - the effects question is left open, which is exactly the gap the Section 3 theories attempt to fill. It also assumes rational, self-aware selection - advertising's unconscious persuasion and algorithmic recommendation (choosing for you) sit awkwardly with it. Pair it critically: uses and gratifications (active choice) vs cultivation (passive accumulation) is the classic dialectic the extended questions reward.

Audience segmentation is the industrial complement: industries classify audiences to sell them. Demographic segmentation - age, gender, and the NRS social grades (ABC1/C2DE) - remains the baseline: Radio 1's 16-24 targeting; the Mirror's C2DE working-class address; The Times' AB professionals. Psychographic segmentation goes deeper - Young and Rubicam's 4Cs (mainstreamers, aspirers, succeeders, explorers, reformers, strugglers, resigned) classify by values and aspiration, which is how modern advertising actually addresses people. Apply to the set products: the Tide advert addresses 1950s mainstreamers through domestic security; WaterAid addresses reformers (ethical, cause-driven consumers); GQ addresses aspirers and succeeders through the constructed successful self. The industrial logic: gratifications + segmentation = targeting. Producers encode for a profile, and the profile determines the decoding positions available (Hall) - the three theories interlock, and showing that interlock is top-band work.

Fandom is where the active audience becomes visible as an economy. Jenkins ('Textual Poachers', 1992) argued fans are not passive consumers but textual poachers - they appropriate, rework and extend texts (fan fiction, edits, theories), forming participatory cultures with their own norms and hierarchies. In 'Convergence Culture' (2006) he extended this: digital convergence lets fan communities function as collective intelligence and fuels transmedia storytelling - narratives distributed across platforms, with fans stitching the world together. Apply to the set products: Zoella's audience is the textbook participatory culture (comments, fan accounts, collective identity around the vlogger); Luther's fandom sustains the brand between series through speculation and campaign energy (fandom famously revived or extended cancelled programmes - the classic industrial payoff); music fandom around the artists of Straight Outta Compton shows fandom's role in canonising counter-culture. The industrial point for synoptic credit: fandom is free marketing - the meme-generator campaign of Straight Outta Compton industrialised what fans used to do spontaneously (Gauntlett's 'making is connecting', Shirky's 'end of the audience').

Measurement completes the industrial picture - a set of facts that scores reliably. Television audiences are measured by BARB, radio by RAJAR, print circulation audited by ABC; digital platforms measure directly (views, likes, watch-time, subscriber counts) in real time. The consequence: measurement shapes content - commissioning follows the data, and the 'audience' becomes a profile of behaviour rather than a community of people. Evaluate: real-time measurement empowers niches (a small loyal audience is now legible and monetisable - the long tail) but also accelerates the click-driven, retention-first content the industries topic critiques. That dialectic - data empowers and disciplines - is the modern industries-audiences nexus.

Exam method: the gratification-targeting chain

1. Profile the intended audience (demographic + psychographic) for the set product. 2. Identify which gratifications the text gratifies and through which codes. 3. Show the industrial logic: how targeting follows segmentation and funding model. 4. Link to reception: the profile shapes available decoding positions (Hall). 5. Evaluate: active choice vs algorithmic recommendation; niche empowerment vs retention economics.

Close with the framework link: fandom and participatory culture connect audiences to industries (free labour, free marketing, transmedia revenue) and to identity (Gauntlett: media as a resource for constructing self). When the question lands on 'how do audiences interact with media today', Jenkins plus Gauntlett plus the measurement facts give you a contemporary answer no effects theory can match - and the examiner will mark the contemporaneity.

Para-social relationships. One of the four gratifications' most examinable applications: audiences form one-sided bonds with hosts and vloggers - intimacy without reciprocity. The Radio 1 Breakfast Show is built on it (the morning ritual, the presenter as companion); Zoella's entire register is engineered for it (direct address, personal disclosure, bedroom intimacy); celebrity culture monetises it (parasocial loyalty converts to streams, tickets, products). The theory point: parasocial bonds gratify personal relationships (Blumler and Katz) while functioning industrially as retention - the bond is the business model. The critical point: parasocial intimacy is simulated intimacy (Baudrillard's simulacrum applies), and its commercialisation is the standard critique of influencer media. One theory, both an audience explanation and an industry analysis - the cross-topic paragraph examiners reward.

The long tail and niche economics. Digital measurement empowers small audiences: a loyal niche is now legible (views, watch-time, subscriber counts) and therefore monetisable, so products can serve audiences too small for broadcast economics - the 'long tail' logic. Attitude's set products are the case: a niche LGBTQ community audience served profitably through digital subscription and community features that linear media could never fund. The dialectic the exam wants: the long tail empowers diversity (voices and tastes served), while the same measurement economics accelerates retention-first content (the click-driven, engagement-optimised output the industries topic critiques). Same data, two consequences - argue both and the answer is synoptic.

Measurement shapes content. The facts that score reliably: television audiences measured by BARB, radio by RAJAR, print circulation audited by ABC; digital platforms measure directly and in real time. The consequence is a feedback loop: commissioning follows the data, marketing follows the measurement, and the 'audience' becomes a behavioural profile rather than a community. Apply it: Radio 1's scheduling responds to RAJAR; streaming commissions respond to watch-time; newspaper strategies respond to click data. The evaluative edge: real-time measurement is also real-time pressure - content optimized for retention is content optimized away from risk, which loops back to Hesmondhalgh's risk-averse industries. Measurement is the hinge where audiences become industries.

Exam craft: the gratification-targeting chain. For any 'how does the product target its audience?' question: profile (demographic + psychographic), gratify (which of the four needs, through which codes), position (the profile shapes available decodings - Hall), industrialise (platform, funding, measurement logic). Model sentence: 'Radio 1's Breakfast Show gratifies surveillance through Newsbeat and diversion through its features - a bundle that is its distinctiveness, because a licence-fee-funded institution owes its 16-24 audience what an algorithm owes no one.' Chain complete: gratification, distinctiveness, industrial logic - three sentences, top-band material.

Segmentation applied to the set products. The Tide advert addresses 1950s mainstreamers through domestic security - the psychographic category that made it the best-selling detergent of its decade; WaterAid addresses reformers (ethical, cause-driven consumers) - the category that modern charity marketing is built for; GQ addresses aspirers and succeeders through the constructed successful self; the Mirror's C2DE address and The Times' AB professionals show demographic segmentation structuring the press. The interlock is the top-band move: gratifications + segmentation = targeting, and the targeting shapes the decoding positions available (Hall) - so the theories of this section interlock with reception rather than competing with it. Show that interlock in one sentence and the audience topic's three theories become one system, which is exactly what the extended questions are marking for.

Effects debates and regulation of audiences

The effects debate is the specification's classic dialectic, and the exam expects you to run it in stages. Start with the straw man: the hypodermic syringe model - media inject ideas directly into a passive audience - is discredited and exists in answers mainly to be dismissed: no theory on the specification defends it, but media panics routinely assume it (every 'this game caused a murder' headline is hypodermic thinking in tabloid form). Name it, dismiss it, move on.

Bandura supplies the strongest evidence-based effects theory: the Bobo doll experiments (1961-63) demonstrated social learning - children who observed an adult's aggression toward the doll imitated it, and imitated it more when the model was rewarded. The theory's claims for the exam: imitation is more likely with identification, reward and repetition - which is why the effects argument clusters around children, and why 'copycat' debates attach to violence, self-harm content and extremist material. Bandura's limits (know these for evaluation): the lab is artificial, the behaviour measured is short-term imitation of a doll rather than real-world violence, and Gauntlett's famous critique - participants may have simply done what the experiment implied was expected - undermines the leap from laboratory imitation to social harm. Apply Bandura to the set products carefully: video game violence debates are the natural case (PEGI's 18 label exists on exactly this logic), and Luther's vigilante violence raises the copycat question in television form - but the stronger answers note that decades of research have found no simple, direct causal chain from depiction to action.

Gerbner's cultivation theory works at a different scale: not single-text effects but the slow accumulation of heavy viewing. Heavy television viewers gradually 'cultivate' a worldview matching screen reality - crime-saturated output cultivates 'mean world syndrome' (the world perceived as more dangerous than it is), which then feeds punitiveness and anxiety. The claims: effects are cumulative, gradual, and operate on averages across large populations - not on individuals. Apply: news cultivation is the set-product case - sustained tabloid coverage of crime and migration cultivates the anxieties that the papers' politics then harvest; Luther and crime television generally feed the mean-world diet. Limits: cultivation is correlational (heavy viewers may be anxious, not made anxious); streaming fragmentation has shattered the shared ' television mainstream' Gerbner assumed - cultivation logic now applies, if at all, to algorithmic feeds rather than broadcast schedules. That last point is the contemporary update examiners reward: the feed is the new cultivation machine, personalised rather than average.

Cohen's moral panic theory ties the effects debate to representation: media construct folk devils (youth subcultures in his original mods-and-rockers study; today, video gamers, migrants, benefit claimants), amplify deviance, produce exaggerated public anxiety and disproportionate institutional response. Moral panics are the mechanism by which effects claims become policy - which leads directly to regulation of audiences.

Regulating audiences is protection logic made institutional. Map it: BBFC classifications (U, PG, 12A, 12, 15, 18) gate cinema and physical media by age; PEGI/VSC labels (12, 16, 18) legally gate video game sales; Ofcom's watershed (9pm) protects linear broadcast; the Online Safety Act 2023 obliges platforms to protect children (age assurance for adult content, duties on illegal and harmful content) - the state's response to the feed as the new frontier. Advertising to children is restricted under ASA rules. Apply Livingstone and Lunt to the whole map: their dilemma - protect vs enable - runs through every mechanism, and convergence has exposed the gaps (watershed meaningless on-demand; PEGI governs purchase but not the live social spaces around games; global platforms slip past national regulators). The Online Safety Act is the exam's strongest contemporary evidence that regulators are migrating from content rules to platform systems - exactly the shift Livingstone and Lunt predicted.

Effects debate: the dialectic bank

For effects: Bandura (imitation with identification/reward), Gerbner (cumulative cultivation), moral panics as evidence of public belief in effects. Against: Gauntlett (audiences reflexive, research methodologically weak), uses and gratifications (active selection), reception theory (readings are socially positioned, not injected). Contemporary update: algorithmic feeds as personalised cultivation; Online Safety Act as the regulatory answer. Use all three columns in any 'does media violence harm audiences?' essay.

The exam-craft structure for effects questions: dismiss the straw man, run the strongest version of both sides, land on the audience evidence. A* answers do not ask 'does media cause harm?' but 'what kind of theory of the audience does each position assume?' - hypodermic assumes passivity, cultivation assumes accumulation, reception assumes active positioning. The specification's own theorist list is effectively an argument between Bandura/Gerbner and Gauntlett/Jenkins/Shirky: effects vs activity. Structure the essay as that argument, evidence it with set products and regulation, and conclude on the genuinely contemporary question - whether the algorithmic feed has revived passive-audience thinking under a new name. That is the level of synthesis the top band is marking for.

Desensitisation. The other effects claim worth knowing: repeated exposure to media violence incrementally numbs response - the desensitisation hypothesis, often paired with cultivation in moral panic arguments. The examinable posture: it is a plausible mechanism with weak direct evidence - decades of research have not established a simple causal chain from depiction to behaviour, and the active-audience evidence (Hall, Jenkins, Blumler and Katz) explains why: readings are positioned, not injected. Name desensitisation alongside Bandura and Gerbner as the effects camp's claims, dismiss each at its strongest, and score for knowing the whole debate rather than one theory.

Copycat case studies. Moral panic mechanics (Cohen) are best evidenced through real cases. The Jamie Bulger case (1993): the trial and press debated whether violent videos (Child's Play 3) influenced the child killers - a misattribution the media amplified, and the classic example of effects thinking becoming public 'fact' without evidence. Columbine (1999): the moral panic around video games and music followed the same mechanics - exaggeration, prediction (more violence to come), symbolism (the games as folk devils). Both cases show Cohen's cycle: media amplify, public anxiety rises, institutions respond (ratings, panics, policy) - and both predate the regulatory answers (PEGI's enforceability, the Online Safety Act) that now govern the field. Cite one case precisely and the moral panic paragraph stops being theoretical.

Regulating the vulnerable. Protection logic clusters around children, and the mechanisms are specific: BBFC classifications and PEGI's legally enforceable age labels gate access by purchase; Ofcom's watershed gates broadcast by time; ASA rules restrict advertising to children; the Online Safety Act 2023 obliges platforms to protect children through age assurance and children's safety duties. The Livingstone and Lunt dilemma applies to all of it: protection vs choice - and the contemporary frontier is the algorithmic feed, where personalised recommendation reaches children through platforms that national regulation struggles to touch. The exam's strongest contemporary move: the feed is where the effects debate (what does media do to vulnerable audiences?) and the regulation debate (who is responsible - platforms, parents, states?) converge, and the Online Safety Act is the state's first structural answer.

Exam craft: the effects essay landed. Structure: dismiss the hypodermic straw man in one sentence; run Bandura (imitation with identification/reward) and Gerbner (cumulative cultivation) at their strongest; counter with the active-audience camp (Blumler and Katz, Hall, Jenkins, Gauntlett) and Gauntlett's methodological critique of effects research; land on the contemporary test case - whether the personalised feed revives passive-audience thinking under a new name. Model conclusion: 'Bandura and Gerbner capture imitation and cumulative anxiety, but neither supports direct causation; the genuine contemporary question is whether algorithmic recommendation - choosing for the audience rather than being chosen by it - has made cultivation theory relevant again.' That is a conclusion the top band is marked for.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: Explain how audiences can respond differently to the same media product. In your answer refer to Hall's encoding/decoding model and to the set products you have studied. [OCR H409 Component 02 style]
Mark-scheme logic: Mark-scheme logic (AO2 application of reception theory, AO1 terminology, AO3 evaluation): (1) State the model: producers encode a preferred meaning; audiences decode from social positions as preferred, negotiated or oppositional. (2) Apply with set products: Tide's encoded housewife ideal decoded with irony/opposition by contemporary audiences (historical distance); Mirror vs Times front pages decoded along political identity lines; WaterAid's empowerment encoding met by negotiated readings questioning the charity gaze; The Returned's artful restraint decoded as prestige by niche audiences and as dull by mass audiences (cultural capital). (3) Explain the mechanism: decoding is socially positioned (age, class, culture, politics), not random; anchorage steers but cannot compel (Barthes). (4) Evaluate: producers cannot guarantee readings; hegemony requires consent which can be withheld (Gramsci). (5) Conclude: divergent reception is the norm - 'distortion' between encoding and decoding is structured, not accidental.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Explain why the hypodermic syringe model is considered discredited, using the set products where relevant. [8]
Answer: Define the model (direct injection into passive audiences). Discredit with three points: audiences are socially positioned, not uniform (Hall); effects research shows no simple direct causation (Gauntlett's critique of Bandura-style claims); active-audience evidence - uses and gratifications shows selection, reception theory shows divergent readings of the same text (Tide decoded ironically today; The Returned read as art or boring by different segments). Conclude: the model survives only as media-panic assumption, not theory.
How far do Bandura's and Gerbner's theories explain the effects of media violence on audiences? [25]
Answer: State both: Bandura - social learning, imitation with identification/reward (Bobo doll); Gerbner - cumulative cultivation, mean world syndrome on averages. Apply: video game violence debates (PEGI logic), news cultivation feeding punitive attitudes. Evaluate both: Bandura - artificial lab, doll-imitation not real violence, Gauntlett's methodological critique; Gerbner - correlational, pre-streaming 'mainstream' assumption, now personalised in algorithmic feeds. Weigh with active-audience theories (Hall, Jenkins). Conclude: both theories capture something - imitation and cumulative anxiety - but neither supports direct simple causation; the contemporary question is whether the personalised feed revives cultivation under a new name.
Explain how uses and gratifications can be applied to the set radio or online media products you have studied. [12]
Answer: Define the four gratifications. Radio 1 Breakfast Show: surveillance (Newsbeat), diversion (features, presenter comedy), relationships (parasocial bond with Greg James, shared morning ritual), identity (youth belonging) - a multi-gratification bundle that is its PSB distinctiveness against Spotify's pure diversion. Zoella: relationships and identity (intimacy, relatability, advice) engineered into the vlog's grammar. Evaluate: explains selection, not effects; assumes rational choice - algorithmic recommendation complicates it.
How useful is Jenkins' theory of fandom for understanding contemporary audiences? [25]
Answer: State: fans as textual poachers - reworking and extending texts in participatory cultures; convergence enables collective intelligence and transmedia storytelling. Apply: Zoella's audience (fan accounts, comments, collective identity); Luther's fandom sustaining the brand between series; Straight Outta Compton's meme-generator as industrialised fandom. Evaluate: prescient on participation and free labour (Gauntlett's making is connecting, Shirky's end of the audience); limits - fandom is a segment, not the whole audience; platform capture of fan labour complicates the celebratory tone. Conclude: highly useful for contemporary participatory audiences; needs pairing with industrial analysis of who profits from participation.
Explain how and why audiences are regulated. Refer to the set products where relevant. [12]
Answer: Map the mechanisms: BBFC age classifications (U-18) for film, PEGI/VSC (12, 16, 18) for games - legally enforceable, existing on Bandura-style protection logic; Ofcom's 9pm watershed for broadcast; ASA restrictions on advertising to children; Online Safety Act 2023 platform duties and age assurance. Explain why: protection of children and vulnerable audiences from harm claims (moral panics convert effects anxieties into policy). Evaluate with Livingstone and Lunt: protect vs enable dilemma; convergence exposes gaps (watershed bypassed on-demand; PEGI governs purchase not live social play).
