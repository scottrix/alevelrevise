Representation

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level OCR, WJEC/Eduqas

A-Level Media Studies revision on representation (OCR H409 Component 01; WJEC/Eduqas A680 Component 1): Hall's construction theory, stereotypes (Dyer), feminist readings (Mulvey, Butler, hooks, van Zoonen) and representations of ethnicity, age and disability, applied to the set products.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain Hall's claim that representation is constitutive - media do not reflect reality but construct it (AO1)
  • Apply Dyer's account of stereotypes and their functions to analyse set products past and present (AO2)
  • Deploy Mulvey, van Zoonen, Butler and bell hooks to evaluate gendered representation, including counter-arguments (AO2)
  • Analyse representations of ethnicity, age, disability, sexuality and class with precise terminology (AO2)
  • Evaluate how far representation has changed over time, using historical vs contemporary set products (AO3)
  • Structure P-E-A analytical chains that link choices to ideology to audience positioning (AO2)

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Constructedness: Hall's principle that all representation is construction: media re-present the world through selective codes, so every depiction is an argument, never a neutral reflection.
Stereotype: A simplified, standardised representation of a social group, reduced to a few fixed traits (Dyer); shorthand that orders the world but fixes identities and serves power.
Countertype: A representation that deliberately works against an established stereotype of a group, offering a positive alternative without escaping categorisation entirely.
Male gaze: Mulvey's concept (1975): classical visual media code women as 'to-be-looked-at-ness' for an implicitly male spectator, fragmenting women into spectacle while men drive action.
Performativity: Butler's theory that gender is not expressed but produced through repeated stylised acts; media representation participates in constructing the norms it appears merely to depict.
Postcolonial melancholia: Gilroy's diagnosis of a culture unable to move on from empire: nostalgic representations of national greatness that repress colonial violence and perpetuate racial hierarchies.
Tokenism: Minimal, surface-level inclusion of a marginalised group that claims diversity while leaving dominant norms untouched - the standard critique of 'box-ticking' casting.
Hegemonic norm: Gramsci-informed term for the dominant group's traits presented as the unmarked default against which 'others' are defined (e.g. white, male, middle-class, able-bodied as the presumed viewer).

📌 Key Points

Hall: representation is constitutive, not reflective - the media help construct the realities they claim to describe.
Dyer: stereotypes order the world, shortcut identification and can project the dominant group's fears onto the Other.
Mulvey: the male gaze codes women as image, men as bearer of the look - test it against Tide, GQ and music video.
Butler: gender is performed through repetition, so representation participates in constructing gender norms.
Always historicise: what was conventional at production is not what is conventional now.

Hall, constructedness and stereotypes

Hall's foundational claim is that representation does not mirror reality - it constitutes it. Media 're-present' the world: they select, mediate and organise signs into versions of reality that carry ideology. This flips the naive question ('is this depiction accurate?') into the exam question ('what version of reality is constructed, how, and in whose interests?'). In OCR H409 Component 01 and Eduqas A680 Component 1, this principle is the doorway to every representation answer: begin by demonstrating constructedness - the selection of codes, the framing, the anchorage - before evaluating the version of the world the product builds.

Constructedness is easiest to prove comparatively. The set front pages of the Daily Mirror and The Times construct the same day differently: story selection, headline register, image choice and layout build two different publics - one organised around emotional, ordinary-Briton solidarity, the other around informed, establishment judgement. Neither is 'the truth'; each is a re-presentation serving its readership and its ownership. That single comparison, made analytically, is worth more than any list of stereotypes.

Dyer supplies the theory of stereotypes: a stereotype is a simplified, few-trait representation of a social group, and Dyer argues it does three kinds of work. It orders the world (making complex social difference instantly legible); it provides an identification shortcut (audiences know 'who' someone is without narrative labour); and it can project the dominant group's fears and desires onto the marginalised Other. Dyer's sharpest point: stereotypes are most rigid precisely where the power to represent is most unequal. You cannot change how you are represented if you do not control the means of representation - a point that connects representation to industries (who makes the media) and to audiences (who decodes it).

Apply Dyer to the set products with precision. The Tide advert constructs the 1950s housewife stereotype: cheerfully domestic, defined entirely by laundry, addressed as an expert in the one domain she is permitted. The stereotype does its ordering work - woman = home - and its ideological work, naturalising the post-war domestic settlement. Crucially, the stereotype was conventional when made; the exam rewards candidates who historicise rather than anachronistically condemn. The WaterAid advert offers a richer case: charity advertising historically relied on the passive, grateful victim stereotype (the 'poverty porn' critique), but the set advert constructs Claudia as agent - singing, moving, forward-facing - a countertype that resists the victim script while still operating within the charity genre's need to solicit the donor. Ask the Dyer question: does the countertype escape categorisation, or simply invert it? The advert still defines its subject by need (for water) even as it grants her agency - the tension is the analysis.

  • Luther: cast a Black actor as the brilliant, compromised lead detective - a countertype against both the criminal-Black-male stereotype and the white-detective default of the genre; but the character's transgression still invites the 'troubled Black masculinity' reading, so evaluate rather than merely applaud.
  • Straight Outta Compton: constructed through the biopic's tragic-hero syntax, restoring agency and historical context to N.W.A - but produced and distributed by a major studio (Universal), raising Dyer's power question: who profits from representing Black struggle?
  • The Big Issue: constructs homelessness through voice rather than pity - vendors as entrepreneurs with agency, a structural counter to the beggar stereotype.
  • Zoella: constructs a version of young femininity (aspirational, wholesome, entrepreneurial) that some read as empowerment and others as a new domesticity of the bedroom - both readings are examinable if argued.

Tokenism is the standard critique of cosmetic diversity: one marginalised face included without disturbing the hegemonic norm. The test is structural: does the representation change who holds narrative power, or just who appears in frame? Luther passes the first test (Elba's detective holds the narrative); a background-casting gesture does not. Counterstereotype fatigue is worth a sentence in evaluation: some argue that endlessly positive representation can itself flatten - representation becomes a public-relations exercise rather than genuine pluralism.

Two further Dyer-adjacent tools sharpen answers. First, anchorage (Barthes): the same image of a group can be steered to sympathy or suspicion by caption and context - headlines do this work in newspapers daily. Second, symbolic annihilation: marginalised groups can be represented badly or, worse, not at all - absence is a representational decision too. Older people, disabled people and working-class voices have historically suffered annihilation more than distortion, and the set products let you measure that: count who is present, who speaks, who is spoken about.

Exam method: the constructedness chain

1. Prove construction: identify the selection of codes (who is chosen, framed, lit, anchored). 2. Name the representation with precision (not 'negative' - say which stereotype, whose interests). 3. Apply the theorist (Dyer on the stereotype's function; Hall on constitutive representation). 4. Historicise: what was the norm at production? 5. Evaluate: does the representation consolidate or challenge the hegemonic norm? Who benefits? This chain converts description into analysis and is the backbone of every high-band representation answer.

Close the section with the synoptic move: constructedness links representation to industries (the power to represent follows ownership - Curran and Seaton's pluralism argument; Hesmondhalgh on who gets to make culture) and to audiences (the same representation can be read differently - Hall's reception theory, covered in the Audiences topic). The examiner wants to see representation as a node in the whole theoretical framework, not an isolated topic.

Mediation and selection. Constructedness operates through selection: every representation involves choosing what is shown, what is central (salience) and what is absent. Absence is a decision too - symbolic annihilation is the standard term for groups represented badly or not at all, and it is the quieter form of misrepresentation. In news, selection is institutional: news values (drama, proximity, elite involvement) determine which events become stories and which do not, so the front pages of the Mirror and The Times are doubly constructed - in what they show and in what they omit. When analysing any set product, ask three selection questions: who is present, who speaks, who is missing? The third question is where most A* marks hide.

Self-representation and the constructed self. Web 2.0 changed the power question: audiences now represent themselves. Zoella's set channel is a case of self-representation - a constructed version of young femininity (aspirational, wholesome, entrepreneurial) made by the subject herself - which complicates Dyer's power point: control of production has partly migrated to the represented. But the complication cuts both ways: the self-representation is curated for a platform economy (Baudrillard's simulacrum of intimacy), and its massive circulation makes the constructed self normative for millions. The examinable tension: more control, same construction - Gauntlett's identity-as-resource and the commodification of that resource in one text.

Place, nation and belonging. Groups are not only people: regions, nations and communities are constructed through recurring codes. The North of England as grim and post-industrial, Scotland as wild, the countryside as idyllic, the city as anonymous - each construction serves narratives (often political ones) about belonging. Gilroy's postcolonial melancholia lives here: nostalgic national representations that repress imperial history keep a particular version of Britishness available as common sense. The set newspaper products are the natural evidence: national identity constructed through crisis, nostalgia or solidarity depending on the paper's politics. Regional identity questions reward the same analysis as any group: codes, anchorage, interests, audience.

Exam craft: the chain in action. Compare the difference between description and analysis. Description: 'The Tide advert represents women positively.' Analysis: 'The Tide advert constructs the 1950s housewife as competent within the only domain permitted her - domestic expertise is her whole performance of femininity (Butler), anchored by typography to the post-war settlement (Barthes) - a stereotype whose ordering function (Dyer) naturalised women's domestic role as common sense.' The second is three moves deep: codes, theorist, function. Build every representation answer as chains of exactly that shape and the mark scheme does the rest.

Feminist and gender readings: Mulvey, van Zoonen, Butler, hooks

Gender is the densest representational arena, and the specification arms you with four theorists who are distinct, not interchangeable. Use them as different instruments: Mulvey for the look, van Zoonen for discourse, Butler for construction, hooks for positioning of the spectator.

Mulvey's male gaze ('Visual Pleasure and Narrative Cinema', 1975) argues that classical visual media organise pleasure for an implicitly male spectator: women are coded as image - 'to-be-looked-at-ness' - fragmented into parts (legs, lips, hair) through framing and editing, while men are coded as bearers of the look, driving narrative action. Scopophilia (pleasure in looking) is structured so the female figure halts the story and becomes spectacle. Apply it fast: the Tide advert constructs its woman as competent-in-the-kitchen spectacle, framed for the approving gaze of the (implicitly male) post-war consumer; the advert sells laundry by selling a version of femininity that exists to be admired and validated. In men's lifestyle magazines like the set GQ, cover conventions (confident pose, direct gaze, physique foregrounded) can be read through the male gaze - or, interestingly, against it: male cover stars invite an appraising look that destabilises Mulvey's gendering of the gaze, opening the 'female gaze'/consumer power debate.

van Zoonen extends the analysis: gender is constructed through discourse - the accumulated ways media talk about men and women - and its expression varies with cultural and historical context, not biology. Her two workhorses for the exam: (1) men and women are represented as different kinds of beings across genres (women as bodies/domestic; men as action/authority), and (2) the male gaze is one discourse among several, not a universal law. Apply van Zoonen where Mulvey strains: news representation (women as suits vs voices-of-authority), advertising across decades (compare Tide's 1950s discourse with the WaterAid advert's female agency - discourse has visibly shifted), and reality/vlog femininity (Zoella's discourse of intimacy, self-care and 'relatability' constructs a femininity of emotional labour - a fresh application examiners reward).

Butler's performativity ('Gender Trouble', 1990) is the deepest claim: gender is not an expression of an inner truth but a production - 'an act which has been rehearsed', maintained through the repetition of stylised acts (dress, gesture, register). There is no gender identity behind the performance; the performance is the identity. Media matter because representation supplies and circulates the scripts. Apply Butler to the set products: Zoella's on-camera femininity (soft register, grooming rituals, bedroom aesthetics) is a performance learned from and fed back into media culture - and its massive circulation means the performance becomes normative for an audience of millions. The Attitude set products (LGBTQ magazine and website) are Butler's best case: representation of drag, camp and fluid identity demonstrates that gender norms are citational and breakable, not natural. Butler also licenses the strongest evaluation of Mulvey: if gender is performed, the 'woman' of the male gaze is itself a construction - the gaze fixes a performance as essence.

bell hooks adds the crucial dimension of race and spectator position. Her 'oppositional gaze' (1992) argues that Black female spectators, excluded by both the white-dominated frame and the male gaze, historically developed a critical, interrogating spectatorship - looking back. Her broader formula - 'white supremacist capitalist patriarchy' - names the compound structure that representation serves. Apply hooks to Straight Outta Compton (Black experience represented, but through a major studio's commercial syntax - who is the spectacle, who profits?) and to Luther (a Black male lead invites the question of which gaze the audience occupies). hooks also powers the critique of tokenistic 'girl power' feminism in advertising - empowerment as a selling device.

Gauntlett is your counter-argument bank. Against the deterministic reading of Mulvey/van Zoonen, Gauntlett ('Media, Gender and Identity') argues contemporary audiences use media as a resource for identity - 'pick 'n' mix' from a wider menu of gender scripts than previous generations had; media now offer more diverse role models, and audiences are reflexive, not duped. The A* structure: apply Mulvey (the Tide advert fixes femininity as spectacle), apply Gauntlett (a contemporary audience reads it historically, ironically - the stereotype has dated and viewers know it), and evaluate which theorist better explains the actual audience relationship. This is precisely the 'theoretical debate' structure the extended questions reward.

Gender representation: the changing conventions

Historicise everything. 1950s advertising (Tide): domesticity as destiny - conventional then, condemnable now. Contemporary advertising: empowerment discourses dominate, but hooks-style critique asks whether 'empowerment' is feminism or its commodification. Music video and men's magazines: hyper-masculinity and hyper-femininity coexist with visible subversion (fluid identities on Attitude's pages). The exam question 'how far has representation changed over time?' wants exactly this arc: convention, shift, commodification critique, Gauntlett's reflexive audience.

Keep three evaluative habits. First, name the gaze precisely: whose look is structured, and who is positioned to occupy it? Second, do not stack theorists: one theorist applied in depth beats four name-dropped; the mark scheme rewards application and argument. Third, connect to constructedness: gender representation is a special case of Hall's constitutive claim - media do not reflect 'men' and 'women'; they help construct what those categories are taken to mean. That closing move ties the whole section back to the topic's foundation and signals synoptic control to the examiner.

The gaze across forms. Test Mulvey wherever looking is engineered. Music video and advertising hyper-femininity: fragmented framing, slow motion, cosmetic close-ups - the to-be-looked-at code at its most literal. But watch the counter-cases: GQ's male cover stars invite an appraising look that destabilises the gaze's gendering - if men can be spectacle, the gaze is not structurally male (van Zoonen's discourse point, and the opening for a consumer-power argument: women as a purchasing audience have commercial power over how they are looked at). And Kress and Van Leeuwen's demand vs offer gaze is where language and representation physically meet: the Tide woman's direct address demands the viewer's involvement; news photographs typically offer for detached contemplation, preserving the pretence of neutrality. Name the gaze precisely and half the representation analysis writes itself.

Intersectionality. hooks' compound structure - 'white supremacist capitalist patriarchy' - is the theory that one-dimensional analysis fails: categories overlap, and a representation is always read through several at once. Straight Outta Compton represents race and masculinity and class (Compton's disinvestment) simultaneously - analysing only the racial representation misses how the film's hyper-masculinity frames its counter-cultural anger. Zoella represents gender and class (aspirational consumption) and age (youth entrepreneurship). The examinable move: name two intersecting categories in the same product and show how the representation is legible only through both. It converts a single-group answer into a structural one, which is precisely what top-band questions reward.

Gender in news and television. The set newspaper products let you test van Zoonen's discourse claim: who is quoted as authority, who is photographed, whose experience is personalised? Coverage that quotes men as experts and images women as family members is discourse in action - gender constructed through accumulated patterns, not single depictions. In Luther, the genre's gender dynamics are in play: the maverick male detective is a convention (repetition), Alice Morgan's equal-intellect villainy is the difference - and the strongest answers read her as a complication of the genre's gendered structure rather than simple progress. Reality and vlog formats construct gender through intimacy and emotional labour (Zoella's care-work register is a fresh application examiners reward).

Exam craft: the theorist pairing worked. Model sentence: 'Mulvey explains the Tide advert's visual structure - the woman as competent spectacle - but van Zoonen's discourse argument explains why it reads as dated today: gender discourse has shifted, so the contemporary audience decodes the construction with irony rather than absorbing it (Hall).' One applied theory, one complication, one audience mechanism - three sentences, top-band material. Revise by building these pairing sentences for every theorist combination you might deploy, and the 25-mark essay becomes assembly rather than invention.

Ethnicity, age, disability and the wider field

Ethnicity. Gilroy's postcolonial melancholia ('After Empire'/'Postcolonial Melancholia', 2005) diagnoses a Britain unable to mourn or relinquish empire: media representations recur to imperial nostalgia - 'the blitz spirit', wartime greatness, 'civilising' scripts - that represses colonial violence and keeps racial hierarchy available as common sense. In the newspaper set products, watch for melancholic nation-building: coverage that constructs national identity through nostalgic frames serves the politics of the paper's ownership. Gilroy pairs naturally with Hall (both theorise race as constructed through signifying practices) and with Dyer (the stereotype's projection of dominant fears onto the racialised Other).

Apply to the set products with precision. Straight Outta Compton constructs Black American experience through the biopic's redemption syntax - restoring historical context (police harassment, disinvestment) that the group's critics ignored; but it is a major-studio product whose commercial success depends on packaging Black anger as entertainment, so hooks' question (who profits? who is spectacle?) is the evaluative cutting edge. Luther casts Idris Elba as the lead detective: a structural countertype in a genre historically coded white, yet the character's pathological edge invites the 'brilliant but damaged' trope - evaluate the achievement without ignoring the residue. The WaterAid advert engages the charity-advert debate directly: the genre's history is the passive African victim ('poverty porn'), but the set text constructs Claudia as agent and the narrative as progress; whether this escapes the Othering gaze or merely softens it is exactly the analytical tension to argue. The Big Issue offers a parallel case of voice-giving: vendors represented as entrepreneurs rather than objects of pity.

Age. Youth is the double-coded group: over-represented as consumers (every brand targets them) but represented as problem in news (Cohen's folk devils: youth subcultures and knife-crime coverage construct moral panics that justify policing) - the set front pages let you cite this directly. Submarine constructs adolescence from the inside: Oliver Tate's self-dramatising interiority is a countertype against both the feral-youth and the carefree-teen stereotypes; the film argues that adolescence is performance, which loops to Butler. Older people suffer the opposite fate: symbolic annihilation - largely absent, or present as frailty or comic relief. The Tide advert's family construction and McCain's inclusive 'We Are Family' campaign let you measure age representation across decades: who is centred, who is decoration. BBC Radio 1 Breakfast Show targets 16-24s with a public-service justification - age as audience segmentation (see the Audiences topic) as much as representation.

Disability. Two frameworks: the medical model (disability as individual deficit, producing pity/tragedy narratives) versus the social model (disability as produced by inaccessible social arrangements, producing structural critique). Media representation has historically defaulted to the medical model: tragedy, cure narratives, or 'inspiration porn' (disabled achievement framed as exceptional despite the body - which flatters the able-bodied viewer). Disabled people are among the most symbolically annihilated groups on screen; when present, tokenism is the standard critique. The examinable move: apply both models, name the narrative grammar (tragedy/inspiration/threat), and note that disabled makers gaining control of production is the structural fix - again Dyer's power point.

Sexuality and the wider field. Heteronormativity - the assumption of heterosexuality as default - governed representation for decades (the 'sissy', the closeted villain, sexuality-as-punchline). The set Attitude products construct LGBTQ identity as community, consumer power and visibility - a genuinely changed representational landscape, though the critique remains that commercial gay media packages a narrow, aspirational, male version of the community (who is missing?). Social class rounds out the field: the 'chav' stereotype and the ' benefits scrounger' discourse in tabloid coverage (cite the Mirror's solidarity framing vs the Daily Mail's moralising) show class as one of the most ideologically loaded representations in British media. Regional and national identity follow the same logic: the North, Scotland, Wales and England are each constructed through recurring codes (grim up north; quaint; troubled) that serve political narratives.

  • Method for any group: prove construction (codes, anchorage, framing) before evaluating the representation.
  • Name the stereotype precisely and apply Dyer's function analysis.
  • Historicise: convention at production vs now.
  • Test structure not surface: does the representation change who holds narrative power?
  • Connect: industries (who makes it), audiences (who decodes it) - the synoptic close.

Constructedness is the section's through-line and the topic's conclusion. Hall's insight scales from individuals to societies: a media culture constructs not just characters but categories - what 'woman', 'immigrant', 'teenager', 'disabled person' are understood to mean. That is why representation is never merely fair-or-unfair: it is a site of power (Gramsci's hegemony - dominant groups' versions naturalised as common sense). The strongest exam answers end here: the representation under analysis participates in the ongoing construction of social reality, and analysing it means identifying who is doing the constructing, with what codes, in whose interests, and with what room for the audience to resist.

Group-by-group revision checklist

Ethnicity: Gilroy (melancholia), Hall (construction), Dyer (projection); set products: Straight Outta Compton, Luther, WaterAid. Age: Cohen (folk devils), symbolic annihilation; set products: Submarine, Radio 1, McCain. Disability: medical vs social model, inspiration porn critique. Sexuality: heteronormativity, Attitude. Class: chav discourse, Mirror vs Mail. Gender: the four theorists of Section 2. For each: convention - change - critique - who profits.

Finally, the exam-craft discipline: representation questions reward specific claims. 'The representation of ethnicity is negative' scores nothing; 'the WaterAid advert anchors its imagery to progress rather than victimhood, resisting but not escaping the charity genre's Othering gaze' scores at the top. Build the chain - construction, stereotype, theorist, historicise, evaluate - and representation becomes the most formulaic high-scoring topic on the paper.

Celebrity as construction. Dyer's Stars (1979) extends his stereotype analysis: the star image is a multimedia construction - built across films, interviews, publicity, social media - structured by a tension between the public persona and the 'authentic' private self that the persona claims. Stars are representations like any other, and Dyer's power question applies: who constructs the star, and who profits? Straight Outta Compton constructs stars (N.W.A) through the biopic's redemption syntax; Luther's Idris Elba carries a star image that stabilises the programme's brand; Zoella is a star constructed in real time across platforms, whose authenticity is her product. The examinable move: treat the celebrity as a representation - codes, construction, interests - and celebrity questions become ordinary representation questions with extra evidence.

Class and the 'chav' discourse. Social class is among the most ideologically loaded representations in British media: the tabloid 'chav' stereotype and benefits-scrounger discourse construct the working class as feckless; the Mirror's solidarity framing constructs the same group as betrayed; reality formats construct class as comedy. The mechanism is hegemony (Gramsci): the dominant class's version of the poor circulates as common sense, and representation is the machinery. The set products give you the contrast: The Times' establishment voice, the Mirror's ordinary-Briton address, The Big Issue's vendor-entrepreneurs - three constructions of class and poverty in one course, each serving its funding and ownership logic.

Exam craft: the past-and-present question. 'How far has representation of [group] changed over time?' is a guaranteed pattern, and the set products are built for it: Tide (1950s) vs WaterAid (2016) for gender and ethnicity; the Mirror and The Times for class and nation; Submarine and Zoella for youth. The A* arc: convention at production - shift - commodification critique - who profits. Do not tell a progress story: representation shifts track ownership, audience power and commercial incentive (Dyer, Curran and Seaton), not simply time. Close every such essay on the through-line: constructedness. A media culture constructs not just characters but categories - what 'woman', 'immigrant', 'teenager' are understood to mean - so analysing representation means identifying who constructs, with what codes, in whose interests, and with what room for the audience to resist (Hall, Gramsci, hooks).

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: Discuss the ways in which gender is represented in the set advertising and marketing products you have studied. In your answer you should refer to feminist theories. [WJEC/Eduqas A680 Component 1 style]
Mark-scheme logic: Mark-scheme logic (AO2 application of feminist theory to set products, with AO1 knowledge and historical context): (1) Tide: housewife stereotype - domestic competence as the whole of femininity, anchored by typography to the 1950s domestic settlement; apply Mulvey (woman as spectacle for the approving consumer gaze) and van Zoonen (gender as discourse of the period). (2) WaterAid: Claudia constructed as agent - singing, moving - a countertype resisting the passive-victim script; apply hooks (empowerment vs charity-commodification of the female subject). (3) McCain: inclusive family construction - gender roles broadened, but family still defined through mealtime care labour. (4) Evaluate: historicise (conventional then vs now); Gauntlett's counter (audiences pick 'n' mix, read ironically); conclusion - representations construct, not reflect, gender (Hall/Butler), and change tracks shifts in who controls production and what sells.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Explain how Hall's theory of representation can be applied to the set newspaper products you have studied. [12]
Answer: State Hall's constitutive claim: media re-present, selecting and coding reality. Apply: Mirror and Times construct the same day differently - story selection, headline register, image anchorage build two publics (ordinary-Briton solidarity vs informed establishment). The 'representation' of events is an argument serving readership and ownership, not a reflection. Evaluate: anchorage (Barthes) steers meaning; comparison proves constructedness; conclusion - analysing representation means identifying the constructed version and its interests.
How useful are Dyer's ideas about stereotypes for understanding representation in the set products you have studied? [25]
Answer: Apply Dyer's three functions: ordering (Tide's housewife instantly legible), identification shortcut (genre casting in Luther), projection of dominant fears (news folk devils; charity victim script). Evaluate: strong for fixed group images; weaker for hybrid, self-aware texts (Submarine's performed adolescence; countertypes like Claudia that resist and reproduce stereotype simultaneously). Extend with Dyer's power point: representation rigidity tracks who controls production (link to industries). Conclude: highly useful as a diagnostic - stereotypes reveal the dominant culture's investments.
Explore how the male gaze is used and/or challenged in the set products you have studied. [12]
Answer: Define Mulvey: women as to-be-looked-at spectacle, men as bearers of the look. Apply: Tide frames its woman for approving consumption; GQ cover conventions invite an appraising look that destabilises the gaze's gendering. Challenge: WaterAid grants the female subject agency (demand gaze as participant, not object); Attitude's fluid identities break the heteronormative frame (Butler). Evaluate: Gauntlett - contemporary audiences read reflexively; the gaze is a discourse that has shifted, not a universal law (van Zoonen).
Explain how Butler's theory of gender performativity can be applied to the set products you have studied. [12]
Answer: State the theory: gender is produced through repeated stylised acts, not expressed from an inner essence. Apply: Zoella's on-camera femininity (register, grooming, bedroom intimacy) is a performance circulated to millions, making it normative; Attitude demonstrates gender norms as citational and breakable (drag, fluidity); Submarine's Oliver Tate performs masculinity as self-conscious script. Evaluate: media both supply the scripts and normalise them - representation participates in constructing gender (Hall); Butler thereby exposes the 'natural' woman of the male gaze as itself a construction.
How far is representation of marginalised groups in the media improving? In your answer refer to the set products you have studied. [25]
Answer: Evidence for: countertypes (Luther's Black lead detective holding narrative power; WaterAid's agent-subject; The Big Issue's vendor-voice; Attitude's LGBTQ visibility) - structural inclusion, not just surface casting. Evidence against: tokenism and inspiration porn persist; commercial packaging raises hooks' question (who profits from representing struggle?); Gilroy's melancholia keeps nostalgic national frames alive; symbolic annihilation still governs disability and older age. Evaluate: 'improving' assumes a linear story - representation shifts track ownership and audience power (Dyer, Curran and Seaton), not simply time. Conclude: visibility has improved, power distribution less so.
