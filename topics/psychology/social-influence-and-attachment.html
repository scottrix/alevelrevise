Social Influence and Attachment

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level All Boards (AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC, CCEA) AQA

A-Level Psychology revision: Social Influence and Attachment. Learning objectives, key points, worked examples and practice questions across AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC and CCEA.

📌 Key Points

Key Fact: Conformity: Compliance (public only), Identification (group membership), Internalisation (true belief); Asch (line length, 32% conform), Zimbardo (Stanford prison)
Key Fact: Obedience: Milgram (65% to 450V), agency theory (agentic/autonomous state), legitimacy of authority, gradual commitment
Key Fact: Resistance: social support (ally), locus of control (internal resist); Minority influence: consistency, commitment, flexibility, snowball effect
Key Fact: Attachment: Bowlby (evolutionary, monotropy, critical period 0-2.5yrs, IWM), Ainsworth: Secure (B), Insecure-avoidant (A), Insecure-resistant (C); Disorganised (D)
Key Fact: Cultural: van IJzendoorn (meta-analysis, secure universal but variations), Romanian orphans (Rutter, ERA study, privation effects)
Key Fact: Influence on later relationships: IWM -> parenting, peer, romantic (Hazan & Shaver)

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain conformity: types (compliance, identification, internalisation), Asch, Zimbardo
  • Explain obedience: Milgram, situational vs dispositional factors, agency theory
  • Understand resistance to social influence: social support, locus of control
  • Explain minority influence: Moscovici, consistency, commitment, flexibility
  • Understand attachment: Bowlby (monotropy, critical period, internal working model), Ainsworth (Strange Situation, types)
  • Evaluate cultural variations in attachment, Romanian orphan studies, institutionalisation

💡 Worked Example

Exam-Style Question

Question: Explain why Milgram's participants obeyed using agency theory

Model Answer:

Agentic state: individual sees self as agent of authority, not responsible. Autonomous state: self-directed. Shift triggered by: legitimate authority, institutional context, gradual commitment (15V steps), buffers (victim not visible). Dispositional: authoritarian personality (Adorno)

Q1: Distinguish between compliance, identification and internalisation as forms of conformity. (6 marks)

Show Model Answer

Answer: Compliance: publicly conforming to the majority while privately keeping one's own views -- a superficial, temporary change that stops once group pressure is removed (driven by normative social influence). Identification: conforming to the role or group we value and adopting its views while we remain a member, e.g. a new army recruit adopting regimental attitudes. Internalisation: accepting the group's views both publicly and privately -- the deepest and most permanent form of conformity (driven by informational social influence), e.g. becoming vegetarian after joining an environmental campaign group.

Q2: Evaluate Zimbardo's Stanford Prison Experiment in terms of ethics and methodology. (8 marks)

Show Model Answer

Answer: Ethics: participants were not protected from psychological harm -- several prisoners showed extreme distress and were released early; there was deception, as guards were arrested at home without full informed consent. However, Zimbardo did hold debriefing sessions and allowed withdrawal (prisoner 8612 left after a day), and the study spurred the development of modern ethics codes such as the BPS guidelines. Methodology: low control means demand characteristics are likely -- guards may have acted to please the researcher, who took part as the prison superintendent, so the "situational" explanation may be overstated; Haslam and Reicher argue the behaviour depended on guards identifying with their role rather than blind situational power. The simulated prison also has limited ecological validity. Conclusion: the study would be ethically unacceptable today, but it remains influential in demonstrating how quickly roles and situations can shape behaviour.

Q3: Outline the Strange Situation procedure and compare secure and insecure-resistant attachment types. (8 marks)

Show Model Answer

Answer: Ainsworth's Strange Situation (Baltimore, 1971) is a controlled observation of infants aged 12-18 months with their mother and a stranger, using episodes of separation and reunion to measure proximity-seeking, exploration, separation anxiety, stranger anxiety and reunion behaviour. Secure attachment (Type B, about 66% of US infants): the mother is used as a safe base for exploration, there is moderate separation and stranger anxiety, and the infant seeks contact and is comforted on reunion. Insecure-resistant (Type C, about 12%): high stranger and separation anxiety, low exploration, and ambivalent reunion behaviour -- the infant seeks contact but resists being comforted; it is associated with inconsistent caregiving. Evaluation: the controlled procedure is reliable and replicable, but it has low ecological validity and is culturally biased -- Takahashi found Japanese infants responded differently, questioning its use outside Western samples.
