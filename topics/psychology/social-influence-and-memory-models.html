PY1: Social Influence and Memory Models

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level All Boards (AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC, CCEA) AQA

Conformity (Asch), obedience (Milgram), Multi-Store Model (Atkinson-Shiffrin), Working Memory Model (Baddeley & Hitch), eyewitness testimony.

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Conformity: A change in a person's behaviour or opinion as a result of real or imagined group pressure (NSI vs ISI).
Working Memory Model: Multi-component active memory model comprising Central Executive, Phonological Loop, Visuo-Spatial Sketchpad, Episodic Buffer.
Types of Conformity: Compliance (public agreement, private disagreement -- normative social influence), Identification (conforming to a role or group we value while a member), Internalisation (public and private change -- informational social influence).
Explanations of Obedience: Agency theory (Milgram: the agentic state -- acting as an agent of authority with reduced moral strain -- versus the autonomous state), legitimacy of authority (power from status and position), and gradual commitment (small escalations make defiance progressively harder).
Multi-Store Model (MSM): Atkinson & Shiffrin (1968): sensory memory → (attention) → STM (acoustic coding, capacity of 7+/-2, duration 18-30 seconds) → (rehearsal) → LTM (semantic coding, unlimited capacity). Supported by the primacy and recency effect; criticised as over-simplified.
Resistance to Social Influence: Social support (an ally breaks unanimity -- Asch's dissenter condition dropped conformity to about 5%) and locus of control (Rotter: internals believe they control outcomes and are more resistant to social pressure than externals).

🔍 Key Principles & Specification Requirements

  • Asch's line experiment: 75% conformed at least once; variables: group size (peaks at 3 confederates), unanimity, task difficulty.
  • Milgram's obedience: 65% administered full 450V lethal shock; situational variables: proximity, uniform, location.
  • Loftus and Palmer: misleading post-event information (leading verbs: 'smashed' vs 'hit') significantly alters eyewitness memory reconstruction.

💡 Worked Example Question

Exam-Style Question

Question:

Evaluate the Working Memory Model compared to the Multi-Store Model of memory.

Model Solution & Mark Scheme:

Strengths: Supported by dual-task performance studies (Baddeley) showing concurrent visual and verbal tasks perform well, proving separate slave systems. Clinical evidence from patient KF (impaired verbal memory but intact visual memory).
Limitations: The Central Executive is poorly specified and vague ('the most important but least understood component'). Does not fully account for long-term memory interactions.

Q3: Outline Milgram's (1963) baseline obedience study and explain why participants obeyed. (8 marks)

Show Model Answer

Answer: Milgram recruited 40 male volunteers (paid USD 4.50) at Yale University for a supposed study of learning. Participants played the "teacher", delivering what they believed were electric shocks from 15V up to 450V each time the "learner" (a confederate) made an error, with standardised prods such as "The experiment requires that you continue". In the baseline condition, 65% of participants went to the full 450V (all went to at least 300V). Explanations: the agentic state -- participants saw themselves as agents of the experimenter and shifted responsibility for harm onto him; legitimacy of authority -- the prestigious university and scientific setting made the experimenter's orders appear legitimate; and gradual commitment -- escalating shocks in small increments made each further step feel minor, making defiance progressively harder. A physical buffer (separation from the learner) also reduced moral strain.

Q4: Evaluate the Working Memory Model with reference to research evidence. (10 marks)

Show Model Answer

Answer: Strengths: dual-task studies support separate stores -- Baddeley and Hitch found participants could track a moving light while performing a verbal task, but performance collapsed when both tasks used the same component. The word-length effect (longer words are harder to recall) supports the limited capacity of the articulatory process in the Phonological Loop. Clinical evidence: the brain-damaged case KF had impaired verbal STM but intact visual STM after a motorcycle accident, supporting the distinction between the Phonological Loop and the Visuo-Spatial Sketchpad; brain-imaging studies show different activation areas for verbal and visual tasks. Limitations: the Central Executive is poorly specified and hard to test empirically, making it the weakest part of the model; case-study evidence may not generalise, since KF's impairment may involve more than one component. Conclusion: the model has strong experimental and clinical support and is more sophisticated than the Multi-Store Model, but the Central Executive needs further specification.
