Memory

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA, Edexcel, OCR

A-Level Psychology revision on Memory: multi-store and working memory models, types of long-term memory, interference and retrieval failure, eyewitness testimony and the cognitive interview, with A* evaluation and exam practice.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Describe and evaluate the multi-store model (Atkinson-Shiffrin 1968), citing coding, capacity and duration evidence (AQA 4.1.2)
  • Describe and evaluate the working memory model (Baddeley-Hitch 1974): central executive, phonological loop, visuo-spatial sketchpad, episodic buffer (AQA 4.1.2)
  • Distinguish episodic, semantic and procedural LTM with supporting case studies (HM, Clive Wearing) (AQA 4.1.2)
  • Evaluate interference (proactive/retroactive) and retrieval failure as explanations of forgetting (AQA 4.1.2)
  • Evaluate eyewitness testimony research: misleading information and anxiety (Loftus and Palmer, weapon focus) (AQA 4.1.2; OCR Loftus and Palmer 1974)
  • Describe and evaluate the cognitive interview and its effectiveness (AQA 4.1.2)

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Encoding: The way information is changed into a form the memory system can store: acoustic (sound) in STM, semantic (meaning) in LTM.
Capacity: The amount of information a store can hold: 7 ± 2 items in STM (Miller 1956), effectively unlimited in LTM.
Duration: How long information remains in a store: under 30 seconds in STM without rehearsal (Peterson and Peterson 1959), potentially a lifetime in LTM (Bahrick et al. 1975).
Multi-store model: Atkinson and Shiffrin's (1968) linear model: sensory memory, STM and LTM as separate unitary stores linked by attention and rehearsal.
Working memory model: Baddeley and Hitch's (1974) replacement for unitary STM: an active multi-component system comprising the central executive, phonological loop, visuo-spatial sketchpad and (added 2000) episodic buffer.
Proactive interference: Forgetting when older (previously learned) memories disrupt recall of newer information - old disrupts new.
Retroactive interference: Forgetting when newer learning disrupts recall of older memories - new disrupts old.
Retrieval failure: Forgetting that occurs because a memory is available in LTM but the cues needed to access it are absent (cue-dependent forgetting).
Encoding specificity principle (Tulving): A retrieval cue is only effective if it was present at encoding - the cue must match the way the memory was originally encoded.
Cognitive interview: Geiselman and Fisher's method for improving eyewitness recall using four techniques: context reinstatement, report everything, reverse order and change perspective.

📌 Key Points

MSM: sensory (iconic/echoic, <1s) → attention → STM (acoustic, 7±2, 18-30s) → rehearsal → LTM (semantic, unlimited); supported by primacy/recency (Glanzer and Cunitz)
Evidence for stores: Peterson and Peterson (1959) STM duration; Bahrick et al. (1975) LTM duration; Baddeley (1966) acoustic vs semantic coding; KF case challenges unitary STM
WMM: CE (attention/allocation, limited), PL (articulatory process, word-length effect), VSSP (visual/spatial, Logie), EB (added 2000, integrates); supported by dual-task studies and KF
Types of LTM: episodic (time-stamped events), semantic (facts), procedural (skills); HM and Clive Wearing: episodic impaired, procedural intact; Tulving PET: episodic right prefrontal, semantic left
Forgetting: interference (proactive old→new, retroactive new→old; McGeoch and McDonald; Baddeley and Hitch rugby players), retrieval failure (Godden and Baddeley 1975 divers; Goodwin state cues)
EWT: misleading information (Loftus and Palmer 1974 'smashed' 41mph vs 'hit' 34mph; Gabbert et al. 2003 conformity effect 71%), anxiety (weapon focus, Deffenbacher 2004 inverted-U; Christianson and Hubinette better real recall)
Cognitive interview: context reinstatement, report everything, reverse order, change perspective; Fisher et al. 1990 more correct details; Kohnken et al. 1999 meta-analysis; enhanced CI used by UK police

The multi-store model (Atkinson and Shiffrin, 1968) (AQA 4.1.2; OCR Baddeley 1966b)

The multi-store model (MSM) proposes three separate memory stores linked in a linear sequence by attention and rehearsal: sensory memory (iconic for visual, echoic for auditory; very large capacity but duration under one second, unless attention is paid, in which case information passes to...), STM (coding mainly acoustic, capacity of 7 ± 2 items - Miller 1956's 'magic number'; duration of 18-30 seconds - Peterson and Peterson 1959, where 90% of three-consonant trigrams were forgotten after 18 seconds of distraction) and LTM (coding mainly semantic, unlimited capacity, potentially lifelong duration). Information moves from sensory to STM via attention and from STM to LTM via rehearsal: maintenance rehearsal (repetition, keeps in STM) and elaborative rehearsal (linking to existing knowledge, transfers to LTM). Retrieval from LTM returns information to STM for conscious use.

Evidence: (1) Glanzer and Cunitz (1966) - the serial position effect: participants recalled words from the start of a list (primacy, LTM via rehearsal) and the end (recency, still in STM) but not the middle; a 30-second counting task before recall abolished the recency effect, supporting a separate STM store. (2) Baddeley (1966) - participants confused acoustically similar words (cat, cap, can) in STM but semantically similar words (great, large, big) in LTM, supporting different coding. (3) Case study KF (motorcycle accident) - impaired verbal STM but intact visual memory, challenging the idea of one unitary STM store. (4) Bahrick et al. (1975) - US graduates recognised yearbook photos up to 48 years later at 90% accuracy, showing LTM duration is potentially lifelong.

Evaluation (AO3):

  • Strength - supporting evidence: brain-imaging (different active regions for STM/LTM tasks), case studies (HM, KF) and the serial position effect all converge on multiple stores.
  • Limitation - oversimplified: KF's dissociation (impaired verbal, intact visual STM) contradicts a unitary STM; the working memory model supersedes it.
  • Limitation - rehearsal not the only route: Craik and Lockhart's (1972) levels-of-processing approach and flashbulb memories show information enters LTM without prolonged rehearsal (deep processing matters more than repetition).
  • Limitation - artificial evidence: most support (trigrams, word lists) uses lab tasks with low ecological validity; everyday memory is richer (faces, events).
  • Strength - practical: the model underpins revision technique itself (elaborative rehearsal, chunking) and multi-store logic appears in exam questions on encoding/capacity/duration studies.

The working memory model (Baddeley and Hitch, 1974) (AQA 4.1.2)

The working memory model (WMM) replaces unitary STM with an active, multi-component system that manipulates information while performing tasks. Components: (1) the central executive - allocates attention and slave-system resources, has very limited capacity and no modality-specific storage; (2) the phonological loop (PL) - processes speech-based and auditory information, split into the phonological store (holds heard words, 'inner ear', ~2 seconds) and the articulatory process ('inner voice', allows subvocal rehearsal; its limited capacity explains the word-length effect - longer words are harder to recall because they take longer to rehearse, Baddeley et al. 1975); (3) the visuo-spatial sketchpad (VSSP) - processes visual and spatial data, subdivided by Logie (1995) into the visual cache and inner scribe; (4) the episodic buffer (added 2000) - integrates the CE, PL and VSSP with LTM, providing a limited-capacity 'store' for combined information and explaining how we form integrated episodes.

Evidence: (1) Dual-task performance - Baddeley and Hitch found participants could track a moving light (VSSP) while performing a verbal reasoning task (PL) with little interference, but performance collapsed when both tasks used the same component - supporting separate slave systems. (2) Case study KF - impaired verbal STM but intact visual STM supports the PL/VSSP distinction. (3) Brain imaging - different activation areas for verbal (left hemisphere) versus visual (right) tasks. (4) The word-length effect disappears when articulation is suppressed (saying 'the the the'), confirming the articulatory process.

Evaluation (AO3):

  • Strength - experimental support: dual-task studies are robust, replicable and directly test the model's predictions; the word-length effect is a reliable laboratory finding.
  • Strength - clinical evidence: KF and other brain-damaged patients support dissociable components, giving the model converging validity across methods.
  • Limitation - central executive underspecified: the CE is 'the most important but least understood component' - vague, unitary and hard to test empirically; some researchers argue it should be subdivided.
  • Limitation - case study generalisability: KF's impairment may involve more than one damaged component (Hitch and others noted other damage); single cases may not generalise.
  • Limitation - LTM interaction: the original model said little about how working memory links to LTM; the 2000 episodic buffer was bolted on partly to fix this, showing the model is incomplete.

Types of long-term memory (AQA 4.1.2)

Tulving (1972, 1985) distinguished three LTM systems. Episodic memory: personal, time-stamped memories of events (your last birthday), consciously recalled and prone to distortion. Semantic memory: general knowledge and facts (capital of France), not time-stamped, constantly updated. Procedural memory: implicit motor skills and actions (riding a bike), recalled without conscious effort and highly resistant to forgetting.

Evidence: HM (hippocampus removed to treat epilepsy in 1953) could form no new episodic memories but retained procedural skills and some semantic knowledge; Clive Wearing (herpes encephalitis) similarly lost episodic memory (seconds-long awareness) while remaining able to play the piano - dissociation supporting distinct stores. Tulving's PET scans (1989): episodic retrieval activated the right prefrontal cortex, semantic the left - physical evidence for separate systems. The case studies also link to the MSM debate: LTM is not a single store.

Evaluation (AO3):

  • Strength - converging evidence: clinical cases (HM, Clive Wearing), neuroimaging (Tulving) and everyday dissociations (knowing how vs knowing that) all support separate LTM systems.
  • Strength - practical application: distinctions guide dementia care (procedural memory often spared) and eyewitness research (episodic memory is what EWT measures).
  • Limitation - case study weakness: HM's and Wearing's brain damage was severe and widespread, so clean dissociation is hard to prove; cases may not generalise.
  • Limitation - overlapping categories: semantic and episodic systems interact in everyday recall (a personal memory of a fact lesson), so the divisions may be less neat than the model suggests; some psychologists propose more than three systems.

Explanations of forgetting: interference and retrieval failure (AQA 4.1.2)

Interference explains forgetting in LTM: memories compete, with proactive interference (old disrupts new - an old phone number intruding on a new one) and retroactive interference (new disrupts old - new parking spot habits making the old one harder to recall). Evidence: McGeoch and McDonald (1931) - participants learned a word list then a second list; when the second list was semantically similar, recall of the first was much worse, showing similarity drives interference. Baddeley and Hitch (1977) - rugby players who had played every match in the season recalled fewer recent games than those who missed matches, showing new games (retroactive interference) displaced older ones. Müller and Pilzecker (1900) showed retroactive interference occurs when new learning follows closely on old (consolidation time).

Retrieval failure (cue-dependent forgetting) explains forgetting when a memory is available but lacks access cues: Tulving's encoding specificity principle states a cue only aids recall if it was present at encoding. Godden and Baddeley (1975) - deep-sea divers learned word lists on land or 15ft underwater; matching learning and recall contexts produced much better recall (context-dependent forgetting). Goodwin et al. (1969) - heavy drinkers who learned tasks drunk recalled them better drunk (state-dependent cues). Abernethy (1940) - students tested in the same room by the same teacher performed better. Forgetting can also be explained by decay (trace fades - only really applies to sensory memory) and motivated forgetting/repression (Freud - distressing memories pushed into the unconscious, weak empirical support but relevant to anxiety-based forgetting).

Evaluation (AO3):

  • Interference - strength: lab studies (McGeoch and McDonald) and field studies (rugby players) converge; similarity effect is reliable.
  • Interference - limitations: lab studies use artificial word lists (low ecological validity); interference explains only some LTM forgetting and predicts forgetting should be faster the more you learn, which real-life expertise contradicts; research by Anderson (1974) shows interference is strongest when forgetting tests are cued by the competing material.
  • Retrieval failure - strength: robust, replicable evidence (Godden and Baddeley, Goodwin) and clear practical value - the cognitive interview's context reinstatement and exam revision advice (revisit the study environment) derive from it.
  • Retrieval failure - limitations: effects are mainly demonstrable in lab word-list recall; in real life cues are rarely absent entirely; and Baddeley himself argued context effects are weak in everyday memory; the explanation is hard to falsify - we cannot prove whether a memory is gone or merely lacking cues.
  • A* synthesis: the two explanations are complementary rather than competing - interference is about competing material at encoding, retrieval failure about absent cues at retrieval; exam questions often reward noting this.

Eyewitness testimony: misleading information, anxiety and the cognitive interview (AQA 4.1.2; OCR Loftus and Palmer 1974)

Misleading information (leading questions and post-event discussion) distorts EWT. Loftus and Palmer (1974): 45 US students watched clips of car accidents and were asked how fast the cars were going when they smashed, collided, bumped, hit or contacted - estimates ranged from 41 mph ('smashed') to 32 mph ('contacted'); a week later, 'smashed' participants falsely recalled broken glass, showing response-bias and memory distortion. Loftus and Zanni (1975) - 'the' versus 'a' broken headlight changed false recollection. Gabbert et al. (2003) - pairs watched different versions of the same event, discussed it, and 71% (versus 0% of controls) incorrectly recalled details they could not have seen - memory conformity (going along to fit in, not true distortion).

Anxiety has mixed effects. Weapon focus (Loftus et al. 1987) - participants' eyes fixated on a gun, reducing identification of the holder; anxiety narrows attention onto the threat. Deffenbacher et al. (2004) meta-analysis - an inverted-U (Yerkes-Dodson) relationship: moderate arousal aids accuracy, extreme arousal impairs it. But Christianson and Hubinette (1993) - real bank-employees who were victims of threats recalled more accurately than bystanders, showing real-life emotional involvement can aid memory. Yuille and Cutshall (1986) - witnesses to a real shooting gave accurate accounts 4-5 months later, and misleading questions had little effect - challenging lab findings.

The cognitive interview (CI) (Geiselman and Fisher, 1985) uses four techniques: context reinstatement (recreate the scene, weather, feelings - uses retrieval-failure logic), report everything (all details, however trivial - triggers may cue others), reverse order (recall backwards - disrupts schemas and prevents dishonesty) and change perspective (view events from another witness's position - disrupts schema expectations). The enhanced CI (Fisher) adds social techniques (building rapport, minimising distractions). Evidence: Fisher et al. (1990) - detectives trained in the CI elicited 47% more correct information from real witnesses in Miami; Kohnken et al. (1999) meta-analysis of 50 studies - CI produced significantly more correct details than standard interviews (34% average increase).

Evaluation (AO3):

  • Misleading information - limitations: lab clips lack real-world stress (ecological validity), participants know they are in a study (demand characteristics); Loftus's findings reflect response bias as much as true memory change; but Yuille and Cutshall's real-shooting study shows lab effects may not transfer.
  • Misleading information - strength: robust replicable evidence with huge practical impact - police now avoid leading questions, and the legal system recognises post-event discussion contamination.
  • Anxiety - contradiction: weapon-focus studies suggest anxiety harms accuracy while Christianson and Hubinette suggest it helps - the inverted-U resolves this but individual differences (trait anxiety) moderate effects.
  • CI - strengths: strong empirical support (Fisher, Kohnken) and real police adoption; individual techniques are effective in isolation.
  • CI - limitations: change perspective demands speculation and can increase recall of incorrect details; the full CI is time-consuming and requires expensive training, so UK forces typically use the enhanced CI with selected elements; Kohnken et al. also found increased errors alongside more correct information.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: AQA 4.1.2 (16 marks): Discuss research into the effects of misleading information on the accuracy of eyewitness testimony.
Mark-scheme logic: 16-mark essay structure (AO1 = 6 marks description, AO3 = 10 marks evaluation). AO1 paragraph 1: Loftus and Palmer (1974) - 45 students watched car-crash clips; leading verb changed speed estimates ('smashed' 41 mph vs 'contacted' 32 mph) and produced false memory of broken glass a week later, showing post-event information distorts EWT. AO1 paragraph 2: Gabbert et al. (2003) - pairs watched different versions of an event, discussed it; 71% of witnesses recalled details they could not have seen (memory conformity); Loftus and Zanni (1975) - definite vs indefinite articles ('the broken headlight') changed false recall. AO3 point 1 (ecological validity): lab clips lack real stress; Yuille and Cutshall (1986) - real shooting witnesses accurate 4-5 months later and unaffected by misleading questions. AO3 point 2 (demand characteristics): participants may guess the aim (response bias rather than memory change); Foster et al. argue participants attend more when they believe a real court case is involved. AO3 point 3 (individual differences): older adults and high-anxiety witnesses are more susceptible; Anastasi and Rhodes found younger adults more accurate. AO3 point 4 (application): police avoid leading questions; post-event discussion is recognised in court - practical impact is strong. AO3 point 5 (methodological counterpoint): lab control isolates variables (verb type) - internally valid; Gabbert used video to control exposure, replicable. Conclusion: misleading information demonstrably distorts EWT, but lab effects may overstate real-world distortion, which depends on stress, awareness and witness characteristics.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Compare the multi-store model with the working memory model of memory. (8 marks)
Answer: The multi-store model (Atkinson and Shiffrin, 1968) proposes a linear, three-store system: sensory memory, STM (acoustic coding, capacity 7±2, duration 18-30 seconds) and LTM (semantic, unlimited), with attention and rehearsal transferring information between stores. The working memory model (Baddeley and Hitch, 1974) replaces unitary STM with an active multi-component system: the central executive allocates attention, the phonological loop handles speech-based information (word-length effect), the visuo-spatial sketchpad handles visual data, and the episodic buffer (added 2000) integrates the systems. Similarity: both describe short-term systems feeding LTM. Differences: WMM is active rather than passive, explains dual-task performance and the word-length effect, and is supported by the brain-damaged case KF, whose verbal STM was impaired while visual STM remained intact. Evaluation: MSM is supported by the primacy and recency effect (Glanzer and Cunitz) but criticised as over-simplified because rehearsal is not the only route to LTM and the central executive is poorly specified; WMM is more sophisticated but its central executive is hard to test.
Explain retrieval failure as an explanation for forgetting, using research evidence. (6 marks)
Answer: Retrieval failure (cue-dependent forgetting) occurs when a memory is available in LTM but the cues needed to access it are absent. Tulving's encoding specificity principle states a cue only aids recall if it was present at encoding. Godden and Baddeley (1975): deep-sea divers learned word lists on land or 15ft underwater and recalled them best when learning and recall contexts matched, showing context-dependent forgetting. Goodwin et al. (1969) showed state-dependent cues with alcohol have the same effect; Abernethy (1940) found students tested in the same room by the same teacher performed better. Evaluation: the explanation applies mainly to LTM and is hard to falsify - we cannot prove whether a memory is forgotten or simply lacking cues - but it has practical value, underpinning context reinstatement in the cognitive interview.
Distinguish between episodic, semantic and procedural long-term memory, with supporting evidence. (6 marks)
Answer: Episodic: personal, time-stamped memories of events (your last birthday), consciously recalled, prone to distortion. Semantic: general knowledge and facts (capital of France), not time-stamped, constantly updated. Procedural: implicit motor skills (riding a bike), recalled without conscious effort, resistant to forgetting. Evidence: HM (1953 hippocampus surgery) and Clive Wearing (herpes encephalitis) lost episodic memory while retaining procedural skills (piano playing) and some semantic knowledge - dissociation supporting distinct stores. Tulving's PET scans (1989) showed episodic retrieval activates the right prefrontal cortex, semantic the left - physical evidence. Evaluation: case studies involve widespread damage and may not generalise; the systems interact in everyday recall.
Evaluate the cognitive interview as a technique for improving the accuracy of eyewitness testimony. (10 marks)
Answer: The cognitive interview uses four techniques: context reinstatement (recreate the scene), report everything (all details, however trivial), reverse order (recall in a different sequence) and change perspective (view events from another witness's position). Evidence: Fisher et al. (1990) found detectives trained in the CI elicited 47% more correct details from real witnesses; Kohnken et al.'s (1999) meta-analysis of 50 studies found the CI produced substantially more correct information than the standard interview (average 34% increase). Strengths: strong empirical support and real adoption by UK police (enhanced CI); individual techniques work in isolation, so partial versions are useful; context reinstatement draws on the well-supported retrieval-failure explanation. Limitations: some techniques (especially change perspective) demand speculation and may increase recall of incorrect details; Kohnken et al. found increased errors alongside more correct information; the full CI is time-consuming and requires expensive training, so UK police typically use the enhanced CI with selected elements. Conclusion: research-supported and effective at increasing accurate detail, but practical constraints mean its full form is rarely used in real investigations.
Outline and evaluate interference as an explanation of forgetting. (8 marks)
Answer: Interference occurs when memories compete: proactive interference (old disrupts new, e.g. an old phone number intruding on a new one) and retroactive interference (new disrupts old). Evidence: McGeoch and McDonald (1931) - a second, semantically similar word list greatly reduced recall of the first, showing similarity drives interference; Baddeley and Hitch (1977) - rugby players who played every match recalled fewer recent games than those who missed matches, showing retroactive interference in real life; Müller and Pilzecker (1900) - interference is strongest when new learning closely follows old. Evaluation: lab evidence is reliable but uses artificial word lists (low ecological validity); interference explains only some LTM forgetting and cannot explain why expertise does not cause massive forgetting; Anderson (1974) showed interference effects depend on cueing - supporting the encoding-specificity account. Conclusion: interference is a valid partial explanation of LTM forgetting, especially where material is similar, best combined with retrieval failure.
Discuss the effects of anxiety on the accuracy of eyewitness testimony. (8 marks)
Answer: Weapon-focus studies (Loftus et al. 1987) show anxiety narrows attention onto the threat: participants' eyes fixated on a gun, reducing identification of the holder; Johnson and Scott found participants exposed to a bloody knife recalled the man's face less accurately than those exposed to a pen with grease. Deffenbacher et al.'s (2004) meta-analysis supports an inverted-U (Yerkes-Dodson) relationship: moderate arousal aids accuracy, extreme arousal impairs it. However, Christianson and Hubinette (1993) found real bank employees who were victims recalled more accurately than bystanders, and Yuille and Cutshall (1986) found real shooting witnesses were accurate months later - suggesting real-life emotional involvement can aid memory. Evaluation: lab studies use artificial stress (a fake weapon) and demand characteristics; real-life studies cannot manipulate anxiety, and victims differ from witnesses; the inverted-U resolves the contradiction but individual differences (trait anxiety, age) moderate effects.
