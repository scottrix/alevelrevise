Research Methods and Statistics

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA, Edexcel, OCR

A-Level Psychology revision on Research Methods and Statistics: experiments, designs, sampling, ethics, descriptive statistics, distributions, hypothesis testing, all seven inferential tests, type I/II errors, peer review and features of science.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Design experiments: lab, field, natural, quasi; operationalise IV/DV; control extraneous variables, demand characteristics and investigator effects (AQA 4.2.3)
  • Choose and justify experimental designs (independent measures, repeated measures, matched pairs) with counterbalancing and random allocation (AQA 4.2.3)
  • Describe non-experimental methods (observations, self-report, correlations, case studies) and sampling techniques (random, stratified, systematic, opportunity, volunteer) (AQA 4.2.3)
  • Apply BPS ethical guidelines and evaluate reliability (test-retest, inter-rater) and validity (internal, external, ecological, construct) (AQA 4.2.3)
  • Compute and interpret descriptive statistics, distributions and levels of measurement (nominal, ordinal, interval) (AQA 4.2.3)
  • Conduct hypothesis testing: probability, significance, critical values, type I/II errors; choose the correct inferential test from the seven (AQA 4.2.3)
  • Explain features of science (falsifiability, replicability, objectivity) and the peer review process (AQA 4.2.3)

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Operationalisation: Defining variables precisely so they can be measured - e.g. 'memory' operationalised as 'number of words recalled from a 15-word list in 60 seconds'.
Demand characteristics: Cues in a study that lead participants to guess the aim and change their behaviour (please-you or screw-you effects), reducing internal validity.
Counterbalancing: The ABBA technique: half the participants experience condition A then B, the other half B then A, spreading order effects (practice, fatigue) evenly across conditions.
Standard deviation: A measure of dispersion showing the average spread of scores around the mean; a large SD indicates values are spread out, a small SD that they cluster near the mean.
Levels of measurement: The type of data collected: nominal (categories/frequencies), ordinal (ranked/ordered data), interval (equal units with a true zero-free scale, e.g. seconds, temperature).
Probability and significance: Probability (p) is the likelihood results occurred by chance; results are significant if p is at or below the significance level (0.05 by convention - 5% or less chance of results being due to chance).
Type I error: A false positive: wrongly rejecting a true null hypothesis - claiming an effect exists when it does not; caused by a significance level that is too lenient (e.g. p < 0.10; the 'optimistic' error).
Type II error: A false negative: wrongly accepting a false null hypothesis - missing a real effect; caused by a significance level that is too stringent (e.g. p < 0.01 when 0.05 was acceptable; the 'pessimistic' error).
Peer review: Assessment of psychological research by independent experts before publication, checking validity, significance and originality to allocate research funding and maintain quality.
Falsifiability (Popper): A key feature of science: theories must be capable of being proven wrong by evidence; psychology's theories must generate testable (and therefore falsifiable) predictions.

📌 Key Points

Experiments: lab (control, low EV), field (natural setting, less control), natural (IV occurs naturally), quasi (IV pre-existing); operationalise IV/DV; control extraneous/confounding variables
Designs: independent measures (different people per condition, no order effects but participant variables), repeated measures (same people, no participant variables but order effects - counterbalance), matched pairs (matched on key variables)
Sampling: random (unbiased, needs target population list), stratified (proportional subgroups, most representative), systematic (every nth), opportunity (convenient, biased), volunteer (self-selecting, biased but willing)
Ethics (BPS): informed consent, deception (cost-benefit + debrief), protection from harm, right to withdraw, confidentiality, privacy; presumpptive consent, retrospective consent
Descriptive stats: mean (sensitive, uses all data), median (skewed data), mode (nominal); SD (precise spread), range (crude); distributions: normal (symmetrical, bell curve), positive/negative skew
Hypothesis testing: null/alternative hypotheses, p ≤ 0.05 significance, calculated vs critical value, one-/two-tailed; type I error (false positive, lenient) vs type II (false negative, stringent)
Choosing the test: difference vs correlation/association × related/unrelated design × nominal/ordinal/interval data → chi-square, sign test, Mann-Whitney U, Wilcoxon, related/unrelated t, Spearman's rho, Pearson's r
Features of science: theory construction, falsifiability (Popper), replicability, objectivity, empirical methods, paradigm shifts (Kuhn); peer review checks validity before publication

Experimental methods, variables and designs (AQA 4.2.3)

Experiments test cause and effect by manipulating an independent variable (IV) and measuring a dependent variable (DV). Both must be operationalised (defined precisely so they can be measured). Types of experiment: lab (artificial setting, high control, high internal validity but low ecological validity and demand characteristics), field (natural setting, higher ecological validity but less control and ethical issues - consent), natural (the IV occurs naturally, e.g. a disaster - high ecological validity, no manipulation, rare events, confounding variables) and quasi (the IV is pre-existing and not manipulated, e.g. gender, age - no random allocation, so causation is weaker).

Variables and controls: extraneous variables (any variable other than the IV affecting the DV - situational: noise, time of day; participant: age, intelligence) must be controlled or they become confounding variables (variables that systematically vary with the IV, making it impossible to tell which caused the DV change). Control techniques: standardisation (same procedure/materials for all), randomisation (random order of tasks/conditions), random allocation (assigning participants to conditions by chance, spreading participant variables evenly), counterbalancing (ABBA technique for order effects) and single/double-blind procedures (participants/researchers unaware of condition, reducing demand characteristics and investigator effects). Demand characteristics - cues letting participants guess the aim (Orne: please-you effects) - and investigator effects (researcher's behaviour influencing results, e.g. Rosenthal and Fode's 'clever Hans' effect) both threaten internal validity; single-blind and double-blind designs control them.

Experimental designs: independent measures (different participants in each condition - no order effects, but participant variables confound and more participants are needed; controlled by random allocation); repeated measures (same participants in all conditions - participant variables controlled, fewer participants needed, but order effects (practice, fatigue, boredom) confound - controlled by counterbalancing or leaving a gap between conditions); matched pairs (different participants matched on key variables such as age or IQ - controls participant variables without order effects, but matching is time-consuming and imperfect).

Evaluation (AO3):

  • Lab experiments: high control (internal validity, replicability) but low ecological validity, demand characteristics and artificiality; field experiments improve ecological validity at the cost of control and consent.
  • Repeated measures: participant variables controlled and economical, but order effects require counterbalancing; demand characteristics more likely (participants experience both conditions and may guess the aim).
  • Independent measures: no order effects and fewer demand characteristics, but needs more participants and participant variables threaten validity - random allocation controls this.
  • Matched pairs: best of both, but matching is never perfect and it is time-consuming.

Non-experimental methods and sampling (AQA 4.2.3)

Observations: watching and recording behaviour. Naturalistic (real environment, high ecological validity, low control) versus controlled (structured setting, control over variables); covert (participants unaware - no demand characteristics but ethical issues) versus overt (aware - consent obtained but reactivity); participant (researcher joins the group - rich insight but loss of objectivity) versus non-participant (researcher observes from outside - objective but less insight). Behavioural categories must be operationalised (specific, observable actions) and event sampling (counting every occurrence) or time sampling (recording at intervals) used; inter-rater reliability checks consistency between observers.

Self-report techniques: questionnaires (open questions - rich qualitative data, hard to analyse; closed questions - quantitative, easy to analyse but restricted; social desirability bias and response bias/acquiescence are key weaknesses) and interviews (structured - standardised questions, replicable; unstructured - flexible, rich data but interviewer bias and hard to replicate). Correlations: measuring the association between two co-variables (no IV manipulation, so no cause and effect - a third variable may explain the relationship); positive (both increase), negative (one increases, other decreases), zero (no relationship); presented as scattergrams with a correlation coefficient (-1 to +1). Case studies: in-depth study of one individual or small group (HM, Clive Wearing) - rich, detailed, unusual cases that cannot be generated experimentally, but small samples, researcher bias and poor generalisability.

Sampling techniques for selecting a representative sample from the target population: random (every member has an equal chance - unbiased and generalisable, but needs a full list and may still be unrepresentative by chance); stratified (subgroups in proportion to the population, randomly selected - most representative, but time-consuming); systematic (every nth person from a list - objective, but may coincide with a pattern); opportunity (whoever is available - quick and easy but biased towards the researcher's location and unrepresentative); volunteer (self-selecting - willing and engaged, but volunteer bias: more motivated, extroverted, may respond differently).

Evaluation (AO3):

  • Observations: naturalistic has high ecological validity but low control; inter-rater reliability and operationalised categories improve objectivity; covert observations raise consent issues.
  • Self-report: rich data and direct access to thoughts, but social desirability bias, response bias and dishonesty threaten validity; pilot studies and anonymity reduce these.
  • Correlations: useful for exploring relationships and generating hypotheses (basis of further research), but no causation, no IV manipulation, and a third-variable problem.
  • Sampling: random and stratified are most representative but impractical; opportunity and volunteer are practical but biased - exam answers must justify the choice for the study in question.
  • Pilot studies and content analysis: pilots improve the design before it matters (ecological validity of procedure), while content analysis converts rich qualitative material into analysable data - both are standard AQA application scenarios; content analysis's subjectivity in coding is the key limitation.

Further non-experimental techniques: a pilot study is a small-scale trial run before the main study, checking the procedure works, questions are understood and timings are right - saving money and time by catching design flaws early; it also trains observers for inter-rater reliability. Content analysis systematically converts qualitative material (documents, media, transcripts) into quantitative data using coding units (categories of content counted across the material) - non-invasive and high in external validity, but the researcher's interpretation of coding units risks subjectivity; thematic analysis (identifying themes rather than counting) keeps the qualitative richness but is harder to replicate. Case study defence: unusual cases (HM, Clive Wearing) generate data experiments cannot, but generalisability is limited - the standard A* trade-off in methods essays.

Ethics, reliability, validity, features of science and peer review (AQA 4.2.3)

Ethics (BPS Code of Ethics and Conduct): informed consent (participants must know the aim and agree; alternatives: presumptive consent - asking similar people, prior general consent, retrospective consent); deception (misleading participants - only acceptable if justified by the cost-benefit ratio and followed by debriefing); protection from harm (physical and psychological; participants must leave as they arrived); right to withdraw (during and after the study); confidentiality (no names, use numbers; the Data Protection Act); privacy (no observations in private spaces without consent).

Reliability (consistency): test-retest (same test on two occasions, high correlation ≥ 0.8 = reliable), inter-rater/inter-observer (two observers agree), split-half (two halves of a test correlate). Improved by: standardised procedures, operationalised behavioural categories, trained observers and pilot studies. Validity (accuracy): internal (did the IV cause the DV change? - threatened by demand characteristics, confounding variables, investigator effects); external (generalisable to other settings/populations/eras); ecological (real-life applicability); temporal (applicable across eras); construct (does the test measure the concept it claims to?); face (does it look valid?); concurrent (agrees with an established measure). Improved by: control groups, standardisation, single-blind procedures, natural settings and operationalisation.

Features of science (AQA 4.2.3): theory construction (hypotheses derived from theories), falsifiability (Popper - theories must be testable and capable of being disproven; psychology's theories must generate testable predictions), replicability (findings must be repeatable - the basis of reliability), objectivity (bias-free measurement, empirical methods), empirical methods (evidence from observation/experiment, not speculation) and paradigms and paradigm shifts (Kuhn - a science has a shared framework; psychology may be a 'pre-science' because it lacks a single paradigm). Peer review: research is assessed by independent experts before publication to check validity, significance and originality; it allocates research funding and maintains quality, but it is imperfect - reviewers may be biased (against unconventional findings, or in favour of their own field), anonymity can hide personal attacks, and publication bias favours positive results (the 'file drawer problem').

Psychological report structure (AQA 4.2.3): abstract (~150-word summary of aim, method, results, conclusions), introduction (literature review leading to aims and hypotheses), method (design, sample, materials/apparatus, procedure - detailed enough to replicate), results (descriptive and inferential statistics, tables/graphs; qualitative analysis if applicable), discussion (interpretation, evaluation, implications, further research), references (full APA format: Author, A. A. (Year). Title of article. Journal Name, Volume(Issue), pages.)

Descriptive statistics, distributions and levels of measurement (AQA 4.2.3)

Descriptive statistics summarise data. Measures of central tendency: mean (sum ÷ number - uses all data, most sensitive, but distorted by outliers); median (middle value - unaffected by outliers, best for skewed data, but ignores some values); mode (most frequent - usable for nominal data, but may be bimodal and unrepresentative). Measures of dispersion: standard deviation (average spread around the mean - precise, uses all values, but distorted by outliers and harder to calculate) and range (highest minus lowest - quick and easy, but crude: distorted by a single outlier and ignores the distribution in between). Percentages and ratios are also used for summarising frequencies.

Distributions: a normal distribution is symmetrical and bell-shaped, with the mean, median and mode all at the midpoint (e.g. height, IQ); a positive skew has a long tail to the right (most scores at the low end - the mean is pulled above the median; use the median); a negative skew has a long tail to the left (most scores at the high end - the mean is pulled below the median). Exam questions often ask you to identify the distribution and choose the appropriate measure of central tendency.

Levels of measurement: nominal (categories/frequencies - e.g. gender, blood type; mode only; chi-square, sign test), ordinal (ranked/ordered data, unequal units - e.g. Likert scales, rankings, ratings; median, range; non-parametric tests) and interval (equal units on a scale with no true zero - e.g. seconds, temperature in °C, test scores; mean, SD; parametric tests). The level of measurement determines which inferential test is appropriate - the standard first question when choosing a test.

Graphs and presentation: bar charts (categories, bars separated - nominal data), histograms (continuous data, bars touching - interval), scattergrams (correlations), line graphs (trends over time), tables (raw and summary data). The choice of graph matches the level of measurement - bar charts for nominal, scattergrams for correlations, histograms for interval distributions.

Hypothesis testing, significance and inferential statistics (AQA 4.2.3)

Hypotheses: the null hypothesis (H0) predicts no difference/relationship ('there will be no difference...'); the alternative/experimental hypothesis (H1) predicts a difference/relationship - directional (one-tailed) if it states which way ('music will improve recall' - only used when prior research justifies the direction) or non-directional (two-tailed) ('music will affect recall'). Hypotheses must be operationalised and testable.

Hypothesis testing and significance: the researcher sets a significance level (p ≤ 0.05 by convention - the probability of results occurring by chance is 5% or less), calculates the test statistic (calculated value) and compares it with the critical value from statistical tables. For most tests (t-tests, Mann-Whitney, Wilcoxon, Spearman, Pearson), results are significant if the calculated value equals or exceeds the critical value; for the sign test (and chi-square with one degree of freedom), significance requires the calculated value to be equal to or less than the critical value. The decision depends on: significance level (0.05 or 0.01), one- or two-tailed test, degrees of freedom (df = N-1 for related tests, N1+N2-2 for unrelated t-tests, (rows-1) × (columns-1) for chi-square) and N (sample size). Conclusions must state whether the null hypothesis is rejected or retained and report p (e.g. 'p ≤ 0.05, so the null hypothesis is rejected - music significantly improved recall').

Type I and type II errors: a type I error is a false positive - wrongly rejecting a true null hypothesis (claiming an effect exists when it does not); it occurs when the significance level is too lenient (e.g. using p < 0.10 instead of 0.05). A type II error is a false negative - wrongly accepting a false null hypothesis (missing a real effect); it occurs when the level is too stringent (e.g. using p < 0.01 when 0.05 would have shown significance). The 0.05 level balances the two risks; exam questions give a calculated value near the critical value and ask which error could be made.

Choosing the inferential test - three questions decide which of the seven tests to use: (1) test of difference, correlation or association? (2) related (repeated measures/matched pairs) or unrelated (independent measures) design? (3) level of measurement: nominal, ordinal or interval?

Parametric tests (related and unrelated t, Pearson's r) assume interval data and a normally distributed population - they are more powerful (more likely to detect a real effect) when assumptions are met; non-parametric tests (chi-square, sign test, Mann-Whitney, Wilcoxon, Spearman) make no distribution assumptions and suit ordinal/nominal data. The sign test (the simplest, and the one you must be able to compute): (1) record the direction of difference (+/-) between each pair of scores (ignore zeros, reducing N); (2) count the number of less frequent sign (S = the smaller total); (3) compare S with the critical value at p ≤ 0.05 for one/two-tailed; (4) significant if S ≤ critical value.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: AQA 4.2.3 (12-mark design question): A researcher wants to investigate whether background music affects memory recall. Design an experiment to investigate this and justify your design decisions.
Mark-scheme logic: Mark-scheme logic: design (8 marks) + justification (4 marks). Hypothesis (operationalised, non-directional): 'Participants will recall a different number of words from a 15-word list in a music condition compared with a silence condition.' Method: a lab experiment with a repeated measures design - the same 20 participants complete both conditions (15 words with music, 15 different words in silence), with <strong>counterbalancing</strong> (half do music first, half silence first - ABBA) to spread order effects (practice, fatigue) evenly. <strong>Random allocation</strong> to the two orders. Controls: same time of day, same room, same word lists (matched for word frequency and length), 60-second learning and 2-minute recall for both. IV: presence of background music (instrumental track vs silence); DV: number of words correctly recalled. Sampling: opportunity sample of sixth-form students (practical) - acknowledge the limitation (unrepresentative) or use random/stratified sampling for marks. Ethics: informed consent, right to withdraw, debrief, no deception needed, protection from harm. Analysis: descriptive statistics (mean and SD for each condition); inferential test - <strong>related t-test</strong> (test of difference, related design, interval data - number of words recalled) if the data are normally distributed; otherwise <strong>Wilcoxon</strong> (ordinal). Significance at p ≤ 0.05: if the calculated value exceeds the critical value, reject the null hypothesis. Justification: repeated measures controls participant variables and needs fewer participants; counterbalancing controls order effects; the related t-test is the most powerful test for interval data, minimising type II error risk.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

A researcher compares two independent groups using ordinal data. State which inferential test should be used and explain why. (4 marks)
Answer: The Mann-Whitney U test. Reasoning: it is a test of difference (not correlation or association), it is used with an independent measures (unrelated groups) design, and it is suitable for ordinal data because it is non-parametric - it makes no assumption of a normal distribution. If the data were interval and normally distributed, an unrelated t-test would be used instead.
Explain counterbalancing as a way of controlling order effects in a repeated measures design. (4 marks)
Answer: Half the participants experience condition A first and then condition B, while the other half experience B first and then A (the ABBA technique). This spreads order effects - practice, fatigue and boredom - evenly across both conditions, so they cannot confound the effect of the independent variable. Counterbalancing increases internal validity while retaining the advantages of repeated measures: fewer participants are needed and participant variables are controlled because everyone does both conditions.
A researcher has collected ordinal data on hours of sleep and exam scores and wants to test for a relationship. Explain which test of correlation should be used and outline one limitation of correlational analysis. (6 marks)
Answer: Spearman's rho should be used because it is a test of correlation/association between two co-variables designed for ordinal data, whereas Pearson's r requires interval-level data and a normal distribution. The researcher states a null hypothesis of no relationship and compares the calculated rho to the critical value at the 5% significance level (significant if rho equals or exceeds the critical value). Limitation: correlation does not establish cause and effect - a third variable, such as motivation or ability, could explain why sleep and exam scores are related, and the direction of the relationship cannot be inferred from a correlation coefficient alone.
Explain what is meant by a type I error and a type II error in hypothesis testing. (4 marks)
Answer: A type I error is a false positive: wrongly rejecting a true null hypothesis - claiming a significant effect exists when it does not; it occurs when the significance level is too lenient (e.g. p < 0.10 instead of 0.05). A type II error is a false negative: wrongly accepting a false null hypothesis - missing a real effect; it occurs when the significance level is too stringent (e.g. p < 0.01 when p < 0.05 would have shown significance). The 0.05 convention balances both risks; using p < 0.01 reduces type I error risk but increases type II risk.
Outline the BPS ethical guidelines a researcher must follow, and one way each issue can be dealt with. (8 marks)
Answer: Informed consent: participants must know the study's aim and agree to take part; dealt with by consent forms, or presumptive/prior general/retrospective consent when full disclosure would ruin the study. Deception: misleading participants is only acceptable if justified by the cost-benefit ratio; dealt with by debriefing at the end, explaining the true aim and restoring participants to their original state. Protection from harm: participants must not suffer physical or psychological damage beyond everyday experience; dealt with by screening, stopping the study if distress occurs, and counselling referrals. Right to withdraw: participants must be able to leave during and after the study, with data removed on request. Confidentiality: no personal details published; dealt with by numbering participants and using only anonymised data (Data Protection Act). Privacy: no observations in private spaces without consent. A cost-benefit analysis by the ethics committee weighs harm against the study's value before approval.
Explain the features of science and evaluate whether psychology meets them. (8 marks)
Answer: Features of science: theory construction (hypotheses derived from theories), falsifiability (Popper - theories must be capable of being disproven), replicability (findings must be repeatable), objectivity (bias-free measurement), empirical methods (evidence from observation/experiment) and paradigms/paradigm shifts (Kuhn - a shared framework of assumptions). Psychology meets several: it uses empirical lab experiments with objective measurement, its theories (e.g. multi-store model) generate falsifiable predictions, and replication is central (replication studies, meta-analyses). Evaluation: psychology lacks a single paradigm (Kuhn called it a 'pre-science') - approaches compete rather than share one framework; some theories (Freud's psychodynamic approach) are unfalsifiable; subjectivity threatens objectivity in case studies and observations. Conclusion: psychology is scientific in method but its status as a full science is debated because of competing paradigms and unfalsifiable theories.
