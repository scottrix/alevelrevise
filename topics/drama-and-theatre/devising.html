Devising Theatre

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC/Eduqas

Devising processes, stimuli, structuring and written evidence for AQA 7262 Component 2 (30%, working notebook) and Edexcel 9DR0 Component 1 (40%, portfolio) — how to hit the top band.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • State exactly what each board's devising component requires (AQA 7262 Component 2: devised performance + 3,000-word working notebook, 30%; Edexcel 9DR0 Component 1: devised piece from a set-play stimulus + 3,000-word portfolio, 40%).
  • Plan a devising process from stimulus to performance: research, practical exploration, selection, structuring, design integration and refinement.
  • Justify structural and stylistic choices (linear vs episodic, motifs, framing) by their intended effect on an audience.
  • Write notebook or portfolio entries that evidence development and collaboration rather than narrating the timetable.
  • Evaluate your own process analytically: intention vs outcome, problems solved, choices rejected.

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Devising: Collaborative creation of an original performance without a pre-existing script: the ensemble generates, selects and shapes material, usually from a stimulus and/or practitioner influence.
Stimulus: The starting point for devised work. In Edexcel 9DR0 Component 1 the stimulus must be a complete published play from the board's list (e.g. Woyzeck, Machinal, Metamorphosis in the Berkoff adaptation, Punk Rock); AQA Component 2 keys the work to a theatre practitioner's methods.
Working notebook: AQA 7262 Component 2's written evidence (3,000 words maximum): a record and analysis of how the devised piece developed, including the practitioner's influence on choices.
Devising portfolio: Edexcel 9DR0 Component 1's written evidence (3,000 words maximum) covering response to the stimulus and research, development and collaboration, and evaluation of the piece against intentions.
Ensemble: A company working as a creative unit — sharing responsibility for generating material, physical commitment and cohesion on stage rather than star individuals.
Practitioner-informed devising: Devising in which the methods of a named practitioner (Brecht, Frantic Assembly, Berkoff, Artaud-influenced physical work) demonstrably shape the piece's form, not just its decoration.
Workshop technique: A structured exploratory task used to generate material — hot-seating, image work, improvisation, task-based physical theatre — evidenced in written work as a source of choices.
Montage: Meaning built by juxtaposing short scenes, images or moments so the audience makes connections between them; the standard devised alternative to linear storytelling.

Boards, components and starting points

Know the rules before the first workshop. AQA 7262 Component 2, Creating Original Drama (30% of A-level), requires a devised piece performed as a group plus a working notebook of up to 3,000 words, teacher-assessed and moderated; the piece must show how the methods of a theatre practitioner informed the work. Edexcel 9DR0 Component 1, Devising (40%), starts from a different trigger: your stimulus is a complete published play selected from the board's list — e.g. Woyzeck, Machinal, Berkoff's Metamorphosis, Punk Rock, Cloud Nine — and is assessed as a 3,000-word portfolio (internally marked, externally moderated) plus the devised performance itself.

The starting point shapes everything. With an Edexcel play-stimulus, respond as interpreter as well as maker: research the play's context and themes, extract its preoccupations (in Machinal, mechanised womanhood; in Woyzeck, a body ground down by poverty and science), then break from the original — devised work must not become a retelling. With AQA's practitioner key, read the practitioner's methods as your stimulus: a Frantic Assembly-informed group builds from physical tasks and choreographic motifs; a Brecht-informed group builds from argument, demonstration and montage.

Evidence from day one. Both written documents reward decisions, so log them while they happen: what you tried, why you rejected it, what the stimulus or practitioner suggested. A notebook written retrospectively from memory always reads as narrative; one drafted after each session reads as analysis. Keep three columns in rough notes — what we did, why, what it produced — and you already have top-band sentences.

Choose your foundations strategically. Pick the practitioner (AQA) or stimulus play (Edexcel) you can evidence in at least three places — a generating workshop, a structural decision and a design element — not the one you find most interesting on paper. Group size matters: three to five performers keeps the ensemble legible on stage and the written evidence honest; larger groups dilute individual contribution, which both boards mark. Run the calendar backwards from the performance deadline: exploration and research in the first quarter, shaping and cutting by the midpoint, refinement and the written draft in the final stretch — and diary the process weekly, because reconstructed notebooks read like fiction. Decide the performance route early: performer or designer. A designer route (set, costume, lighting or sound realised as portfolio designs) is assessed with equal seriousness and rewards candidates who think visually; a performer route rewards physical commitment and ensemble disciplines. Whichever route, record rejected ideas deliberately — a photographed workshop improvisation you cut, with a sentence on why, is stronger evidence of judgement than three ideas that survived untouched.

Process and structuring the piece

Generate wide, choose hard. A disciplined process runs: stimulus response → research → workshops → selection → structuring → design integration → rehearsal refinement → editing. In the generation phase use varied workshop techniques so the written evidence shows range: hot-seating invented characters, role-on-the-wall for backstory, image work (freezing an idea from the stimulus, then re-staging it), task-based physical theatre (build a sequence of eight movements that says 'pressure'), and improvisation from the stimulus's tensions. Research should be fast and usable — context, a news story, an expert's vocabulary — and must feed a decision, not pad the portfolio.

Structure is the marks. Examiners reward devised pieces that are built, not accumulated. Decide early between a linear arc and an episodic/montage form; most strong devised work juxtaposes short scenes, images or motifs so the audience assembles meaning. Use recurring devices deliberately: a physical motif transformed each time it returns, a framing device (a courtroom, a game show, a vigil), a repeated sound or image that accrues meaning. Name the intended audience effect for each choice — disorientation, complicity, escalation — because that is the sentence your written work and any viva will demand.

Edit like a director. The top-band skill is deletion: kill scenes that interest you but do not advance the audience's understanding; compress exposition; end scenes on images rather than explanations. Integrate design from the workshops onwards — a Brechtian placard, a single costume item that transforms, a soundscape that carries transitions — so design reads as dramaturgy, not decoration. Finally, rehearse the ensemble disciplines: shared focus, safe physical practice, and consistent spacing, because devising is marked as group theatre-making, and a collapsing ensemble collapses the piece.

Document in the room, not afterwards. Nominate a rotating scribe: after each workshop, five minutes to log what was tried, what the group noticed, and what it suggests next. Those logs are raw material for the written evidence and prevent the retrospective-essay problem entirely. Select for the write-up the three or four moments where a decision changed the piece: a workshop that produced the central image, a test that failed, a disagreement resolved by evidence. Give design its own workshops — improvise with a single light source, build a costume from found objects, construct a two-minute soundscape — so design appears in the development story as dramaturgy rather than an afterthought bolted on at technical rehearsal. In rehearsal itself, choreograph the transitions: devised pieces die in the joins between episodes, and a rehearsed transition (a shared walk, a repeated sound, a crossfade) is what examiners read as polish. Finally, remember how the performance is marked: your individual contribution inside the ensemble. Rehearse the moments where you lead, and document them, because the written work must show the same contribution the examiner will see on stage.

The written evidence: notebook vs portfolio

Write analysis, not diary. AQA's working notebook and Edexcel's portfolio share one examiner question: show me the thinking. Describing sessions ('we brainstormed, then we improvised') is bottom-band material. Band 5 entries make claims about intentions and evaluate outcomes: 'We rejected naturalistic split-scenes because our Frantic Assembly research showed montage of physical images would communicate isolation faster; testing this, the audience (our peers) read the isolation in three images rather than two scenes, so we kept it.'

Map your 3,000 words onto the criteria. Roughly a third on the starting point: your response to the stimulus or practitioner, research, and the artistic aims that emerged. The largest block on development: key decisions, workshop evidence, structural and design choices, collaboration — including honest disagreement and how it was resolved. The remainder on evaluation: what the piece communicated against intentions, what you would change with more time. Weave the practitioner (AQA) or stimulus play (Edexcel) through all three blocks; a single 'influence' paragraph is the classic dropped-grade mistake.

Evidence the collaboration. Boards credit devising as ensemble work, so name your collective role honestly: what you led, what you contributed physically, where you were edited down. Conflict is a gift in written work if resolved: 'Our split over tone was solved by testing both cuts with an invited audience' is a top-band sentence because it shows process as problem-solving. Anchor every claim in a specific moment of the piece, and finish the document early enough to edit for that analytical voice — verbs like justified, rejected, refined, transformed — rather than the recollection voice of we did, then we did.

A redrafting checklist for the top band. Run every paragraph through four tests. Claim test: does it assert something arguable about the piece, or merely report activity? Test test: is there evidence — peer response, a workshop comparison, feedback wording — behind the claim? Choice test: does it name what was chosen over what was rejected? Effect test: does it end on the audience? Verbs to install: justified, rejected, refined, transformed, abandoned, tested. Quantify honestly — running times, image counts, the feedback phrase the invited audience actually used — because numbers are unarguable evidence. Write the document in instalments during the process, then leave two weeks at the end purely for editing: the most common grade-limiter is not thin experience but thin editing, with 'we did, then we did' narration surviving to the final draft. Cut biography drift ruthlessly: paragraphs about the practitioner's life, the stimulus playwright's career or your group's social dynamics earn nothing unless they terminate in a decision about your piece. And use media deliberately where permitted — a captioned image of a key moment can anchor an analysis paragraph — but never let images replace the analytical sentences that carry the marks.

Final submission discipline. Before hand-in, proofread against the assessment criteria line by line and annotate your own draft with the criterion each paragraph addresses — anything unannotated is probably narrative. Keep early drafts: they are evidence of development if your centre requires authenticated drafts, and they protect you if a moderator queries process. Sign nothing you cannot defend: both boards require authenticity declarations, so the voice in the notebook must be yours, and quotations from the stimulus play or practitioner sources must be brief and attributed. Submit early enough that a formatting error — word count overrun, missing section — is a correction, not a casualty. The written evidence is the component's memory; make it precise, evidenced and unmistakably about your piece.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: Evaluate how your response to the stimulus influenced the structure of your devised piece, justifying two significant changes made during the development process. (Edexcel 9DR0 Component 1 portfolio style)
Mark-scheme logic: Mark-scheme logic for the top band:<ul><li><strong>AO1 creating and developing ideas:</strong> identify the stimulus element that anchored the work (a theme, image or character type from the set play) and the artistic intention that grew from it.</li><li><strong>Justified decisions, not description:</strong> change 1 and change 2 each stated as a before/after (e.g. linear chronology replaced by montage of three images; a naturalistic kitchen scene cut and replaced by a physical-theatre sequence).</li><li><strong>Evidence of testing:</strong> workshops, peer-audience response, or rehearsal observation that motivated each change.</li><li><strong>Stimulus traced through:</strong> the response to the stimulus remains visible in the final structure — the marker can see the journey from source to stage.</li><li><strong>Evaluation register:</strong> intention vs outcome ('the montage did communicate X, but the final image needed one more transformation, which time did not allow').</li><li><strong>Conditions:</strong> changes must be significant (structural, not line-edits), connected to the stimulus, and the justification must reference intended audience effect, not personal preference alone.</li></ul>

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

How should you evidence a practitioner's methods in the AQA working notebook without it becoming a biography?
Answer: Open each reference with the method, not the life: 'Brecht's demonstration technique shaped how we presented the moneylender' earns marks; two sentences on Brecht's biography do not. For each influence, follow the three-step sentence pattern: method → what it changed in our piece → intended audience effect. E.g. 'Placards summarising each section prevented suspense, so our audience judged the debt-cycle instead of following it.' Show the influence in at least three places: a generating workshop, a structural decision and a design element, and include one influence you tested and rejected — proving engagement rather than imitation. Close the notebook by evaluating how far the practitioner's methods served your stimulus's ideas; that critical distance is what distinguishes band 5 from competent application.
Evaluate the use of two workshop techniques in developing your devised material.
Answer: Hot-seating an invented character (a refused asylum seeker) produced the backstory the piece eventually framed its montage around: her answers gave us the objective 'to be believed', which became the engine of the central monologue. Its strength was specificity — details that improvisation alone had not reached; its limit was verbal bias, and we corrected it by converting her answers into a wordless physical sequence, which tested better with a peer audience. Second, image work: freezing the stimulus photograph's composition, then re-staging it three times (domestic, courtroom, crowd) gave us a recurring motif that structured the whole piece; its strength was speed and theatricality, but the first version was too illustrative, so we transformed the image's spatial relationships until it suggested the idea rather than copying it. Evaluation of both should name the test applied (peer response against intention) — that is what lifts the answer above description.
Your group received conflicting responses to the stimulus. Evaluate how you resolved this.
Answer: Our set-play stimulus, <em>Machinal</em>, provoked two readings: some saw mechanised oppression demanding Brechtian distance, others saw an intimate psychological tragedy demanding empathy — a genuine artistic conflict, not a timetable dispute. We resolved it by testing rather than arguing: each camp staged the same moment (the office scene) in its own style for an invited audience of peers, who found the naturalistic version engaging but unsurprising, and the Brechtian version cold but memorable. The solution was hybrid structure — intimate playing inside an alienated frame: direct address and captions framing emotionally played scenes, so the audience both feels for and judges the protagonist. Evaluate the resolution honestly: it cost rehearsal time and one member still preferred the pure version, but the testing method converted conflict into evidence, and the final piece communicated the oppression <em>and</em> the person. The general lesson — resolve artistic disagreement by staged comparison against stated intentions — is the analytical point the portfolio rewards.
Explain how you structured your devised piece and justify why an episodic structure suited your stimulus.
Answer: The piece ran seven episodes — three wordless images, three scenes and a song — linked by a recurring motif of hands. Episodic structure suited our stimulus, the machinery imagery of <em>Machinal</em>, because the original play's own montage of workplace, home, courtroom and execution resists linear realism: our audience needed to compare institutions, not follow one plot. Justify the order by audience effect: we placed the two most naturalistic scenes second and fifth, so between them the images escalated in abstraction, holding attention through contrast; the song at the centre deliberately broke empathy at the piece's moral hinge. A linear alternative was tested and rejected — in workshop it read as melodrama, closing off the judgement our stimulus invited. Design carried the structure too: a single strip light reset each episode like a full stop, and a placard-style caption titled each one, keeping the audience analytical. Structure here is the meaning; that claim, evidenced, is the top-band point.
What makes written evaluation band 5 rather than merely descriptive?
Answer: Three shifts in register. First, claims with tests: not 'the physical theatre went well' but 'the eight-count lift communicated panic, confirmed when our invited audience used the word trapped in feedback, which matched our intention'. Second, intention versus outcome: state what you wanted the audience to understand or feel, then measure the piece against it, including where it fell short and what you would change — unexamined success reads as naive. Third, decisions over activities: every paragraph should contain a choice (this, rather than that) with its reason, because devising is assessed as theatre-making judgement. Also quantify where honest — running times, image counts, feedback wording — and name collaboration as process: who proposed, who challenged, what was cut. Descriptive writing retells a process; evaluative writing justifies an artistic product. Drafting early, then editing every 'we did' into 'we chose, because, so that', is the fastest route up the bands.
