Interpreting Set Texts

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC/Eduqas

Set plays for AQA 7262 (Section A and B) and Edexcel 9DR0 (Components 2 and 3), and how to build a director's interpretation: concept, staging and design justified by textual evidence and audience effect.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Map your set plays to the correct component: AQA 7262 Section A (dramatic and stylistic study) and Section B (staging); Edexcel 9DR0 Component 3 Section B (page to stage) and Section C (interpreting texts).
  • Construct a coherent director's concept and derive concrete staging decisions from it.
  • Justify interpretation with textual evidence — dialogue, stage directions, subtext and structure — rather than personal preference.
  • Apply practitioner lenses (Stanislavski, Brecht, Artaud) to generate performer, director and designer decisions for set texts.
  • Evaluate alternative interpretations and defend your chosen one by its intended effect on a contemporary audience.

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Interpretation: A coherent set of decisions about how a play should be realised in performance — period, design, playing style, key relationships — justified by the text and by intended audience meaning.
Director's concept: The single governing idea that unifies a production's decisions (e.g. 'Yerma as a closed box slowly pressurised'); it must be evidenced from the text, not imposed on it.
Performance text: The play as a score for performance — dialogue, stage directions, structure and silences to be realised — as opposed to a literary text read only for theme.
Subtext: The unspoken meaning beneath the dialogue that the actor's playing and the director's staging make visible to the audience.
Stage configuration: The spatial relationship of stage and audience (proscenium, thrust, traverse, in-the-round, promenade); a legitimate and examinable interpretive choice.
Semiotics: The study of signs; in theatre analysis, how set, costume, lighting and sound function as signs carrying meaning to the audience.
Blocking: The planned movement and positioning of actors on stage; in interpretation answers, a decision tool for power, focus and meaning.
Intention: The stated effect a production decision is designed to produce in an audience; every interpretation answer must connect choice to intention.

Set plays and where they are examined

Know your text's job in the exam. The same play is examined differently depending on which paper it serves, so revise the mode, not just the plot. Under AQA 7262 Component 1, your two set plays split roles: one from Section A is studied dramatically and stylistically (genres, structure, context, how it works on the page and in performance), one from Section B is studied for staging — you answer as a director realising scenes. Under Edexcel 9DR0, Component 3 Section B (page to stage) requires realising one set text from a performer's or designer's perspective (currently Lysistrata or That Face), while Section C (interpreting texts) asks for a director-level reinterpretation of a longer set text — e.g. Woyzeck or Machinal — for a contemporary audience. Lists are occasionally amended by the boards: always verify against the live specification your centre holds.

AQA Component 3 (Making Theatre) uses three extracts from published plays chosen by your centre, not a fixed list — the interpretation skill transfers directly.

Revision consequence: a Section A text needs annotations on genre and style; a Section B or Edexcel Section C text needs annotations on staging potential — entrances, props, levels, sound cues — because you will be asked to build scenes from it, not describe them.

Annotate in three passes. Pass one — structure: mark units, entrances, exits and turning points, so you know the scene's architecture before decorating it. Pass two — staging potential: code every prop, level, door, sound cue and direct address; these are the raw materials Section B and Edexcel Section C answers are built from, and scenes with rich staging potential (Yerma's doorway scene, Road's Scullery tour, the Interrogator scenes of Machinal) should be learned as fully built models. Pass three — interpretation: note where the text is underdetermined and therefore open (period, configuration, casting, playing style), because these gaps are where your original decisions earn marks. Then nominate five or six buildable scenes per text and prepare them like an athlete prepares moves: blocking, design, audience effect, one alternative rejected. For AQA Component 3 the same preparation transfers — your centre's three extracts are rehearsed and one performed, and the reflective work rewards exactly the annotation discipline above. The general law: the exam rewards candidates who have pre-decided, in detail, how specific moments play; anything improvised in the invigilation hall reads as improvised.

Building an interpretation: concept, evidence, justification

Concept first, devices second. A director's concept is one sentence that every staging decision obeys: 'Road as a guided tour of a street with no exit'; 'The Glass Menagerie as memory literally refracted through glass'. Without a concept, design choices look arbitrary; with one, the same choices read as interpretation. Derive the concept from the text itself — its dominant images, repeated motifs, structure — then test every decision against it: does this lighting state, this period update, this direct address serve the concept and the audience's understanding?

Evidence is the difference between opinion and interpretation. Quote or cite precisely: a stage direction, a line's subtext, a structural repetition. If you set Hedda Gabler in 2026, justify it from the text — the marriage of convenience, social surveillance, the pistols now inherited rather than inherited-and-anachronistic — and then show consequences: the drawing room becomes glass and screens, surveillance replaces gossip. If you keep Lorca's Yerma in its Andalusian setting, justify that too: fertility ritual and rural chorus are load-bearing. Both are valid; the marks sit in the justification chain textual evidence → decision → intended audience effect.

Consider, then choose. Higher-band answers acknowledge alternatives and dismiss them with reasons: 'An in-the-round configuration would suit the town's surveillance of Yerma, but a thrust keeps the shared surface of the bed and the field visible to all — chosen for that image.' Practitioner lenses are the fastest generators of justified options: a Stanislavskian reading pursues psychological truth; a Brechtian reading adds captions and demonstration; an Artaudian reading drives the scene with sound and ritual. Name the lens, state the decision it produces, attach it to the text, and the paragraph practically marks itself.

Updating a classic: what moves, what holds. When arguing for a transposition, first isolate the text's load-bearing elements — the ones the plot and theme cannot survive without. For Hedda Gabler: the marriage of convenience, the manuscript as rival's child, the pistols, social surveillance. A 2026 setting can keep all four (screens replace gossip; the pistols become an heirloom too conspicuous to sell), but a setting that discards them is not an interpretation, it is a different play. Conversely, some elements are negotiable: verse form, actual dates, specific domestic furniture. Higher-band answers show this analysis explicitly: name what you preserve and why, then what you change and what it costs. Attach the contemporary-relevance claim to a specific contemporary mechanism, not to the phrase itself — 'honour culture policed through group chats' earns more than 'it is relevant today'. And beware purposeless anachronism: an update with no argument behind it reads as a costume choice. The test is always the same sentence: this change, made because the text's concern X is differently visible in setting Y, producing effect Z in a modern audience.

Staging and design: realising the interpretation

Staging is interpretation made visible. Configuration, entrances, levels, proxemics and use of space are the director's grammar. Examples to imitate in answers: opening Road with Scullery addressing the audience from a thrust walkway implicates us in his tour; staging The Curious Incident's overwhelm through ensemble choreography on a grid floor turns Christopher's interiority into public spectacle; playing the Interrogator's scenes of Machinal from raised positions crushes the Young Woman spatially before the plot does. Each example obeys the same sentence law: decision → how it is staged → what the audience understands or feels as a result.

Design is interpretation's vocabulary. Set, costume, lighting and sound must be argued as meaning, not atmosphere: a rotating single lamp in The Glass Menagerie (Williams's own leg-of-mutton screen aside) makes memory selective; costume in That Face — Martha's silk dressing gown worn at noon — signs addiction as a performance of elegance; projected captions in a Brechtian Woyzeck convert episodes into evidence. Cite the designer's tools precisely (states, cues, textures) and always close the loop with intended meaning.

Answer architecture for the written papers. For AQA Section B (30 marks) and Edexcel Section C, plan four to five paragraphs: configuration and concept; two to three moments staged in detail (use the extract or act given); design integration; one alternative considered and rejected. Anchor each paragraph in the specified scene — examiners penalise answers that stage the whole play loosely — and keep the audience's understanding as the verdict of every paragraph. That architecture, rehearsed on your two texts until it is automatic, is the difference between knowing the play and banking the marks.

Two more design-driven models. For Lysistrata (Edexcel Section B), design carries the comedy's scale: an in-the-round or thrust configuration turns the acropolis seizure into a coup the audience is inside, with the chorus's two semi-choruses entering through the house itself, and costume doubling old and young through a single colour clash (grey drabness versus saturated, athletic brights) so the generational war is visible before the peace treaty. For That Face, a vertical set — the family apartment above, the school below — lets Martha's gravity over Mia be staged literally as descent, and the drug scene's haze is a lighting state, not fog: a slow crossfade to warm amber soft-focus that makes intoxication beautiful, then snaps cold for the come-down, teaching the audience that the production's glamour is the trap. Both models end with the justification sentence that examiners want: the design decision, its realisation, the audience meaning. Rehearse the architecture under time pressure — plan in five minutes, four to five paragraphs in forty — because Section B and C answers fail through sprawl, not ignorance: scene-specific detail, consistently justified, is the whole game.

Exam-day assembly. In the paper, read the extract twice, underline its given props, entrances and silences, and choose the interpretation you can sustain, not the most original one available. Five minutes of planning buys coherence: configuration and concept; three moments staged in detail; design thread; one alternative rejected. Write in director's language throughout — I would block, I would light, the audience reads — and attach a justification clause to every decision, because unjustified staging is the single most common reason capable candidates plateau at mid-band. Finish with the alternative: naming it, costing it, rejecting it for reasons grounded in the text. That closing move converts a competent answer into an evaluative one.

Exam-style: As a director, explain how you would realise your interpretation of the final scene of Yerma to communicate meaning to a twenty-first-century audience. (AQA 7262 Component 1 Section B style, 30 marks)

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

As a director, explain how you would stage the opening of Road by Jim Cartwright to establish the world of the play for the audience.
Answer: Configuration first: a thrust with a raised walkway as the road itself, so the audience sits either side of the street like residents — Scullery's tour must implicate us, and in-the-round risked diffuse focus in workshop terms. Scullery enters down the walkway in a gaudy, too-warm jacket, directly addressing the audience as neighbours, breaking the fourth wall from the first line so the evening reads as invitation into complicity. The houses are suggested by door frames and window frames hung in the dark, lit individually as each interior is visited — a Brechtian choice: the street as a series of exhibits rather than an illusionistic set. Sound design opens with distant music leaking from different doors, mixed under Scullery's patter, so poverty is heard as noise before it is stated. The intended effect: within five minutes the audience knows this is a guided tour they cannot refuse, and every later interior lands as an episode in the same walk.
As a director, explain how you would use the ensemble to stage Christopher's overwhelmed moments in The Curious Incident of the Dog in the Night-Time.
Answer: The concept: Christopher's interiority made spatial — the world happens to him on stage, so the ensemble performs the world's pressure. In station and street sequences the ensemble moves Christopher rather than around him: sustained lifts tilting his body as the platform tips, walking patterns that cross his sightlines at speed, a wall of bodies advancing that he negotiates like traffic. Vocal design belongs to the ensemble too — overlapping repetition of fragmentary announcements (timetable, tannoy, strangers' sentences) built to a literal roar, cut abruptly to silence on his discovery of the rat, the silence doing more expressive work than the noise. Lighting states shift with each wave: a cold white general wash snapping to isolating specials on Christopher, so the audience experiences focus as sensory survival rather than mood. Configuration remains a grid floor over a black box — the patterned world Christopher can only control by logic. Justification closes every paragraph: the audience does not learn that Christopher is overwhelmed; it is overwhelmed with him, and ensemble choreography is the instrument.
As a director, explain how you would stage the opening scene of That Face to present Martha and Mia's relationship for a contemporary audience.
Answer: The concept for the production: damage performed as elegance. The scene — hung-over morning chaos, the phone, the jeans that must be acquired — plays at pace in a bedroom whose luxury is decaying: silk bedding, an unmade wreck, empty bottles staged as still life, so the set signs wealth collapsing into neglect. Martha enters in a silk dressing gown at midday, and I would block her slow, unhurried crossing against Mia's frantic searching: proxemics as power, the mother controlling the room by refusing its urgency. When she cradles Mia's face, the tender gesture is held a beat too long — the audience should read both love and possession in the same physical sentence, which is Stenham's subtext made visible. Sound design is minimal: the phone's ring unrelenting until answered, an intrusion the century recognises instantly. An alternative — playing the scene for broad comedy — was rejected: laughter would set up the second act's violence as surprise rather than inevitability, and the text's structuring of control demands dread under the jokes.
As a director, justify a Brechtian interpretation of Woyzeck for a contemporary audience. (Edexcel 9DR0 Component 3 Section C style)
Answer: Buchner's fragment already thinks in montage — twenty-plus unnumbered scenes — so my interpretation formalises what the text implies: <em>Woyzeck</em> as a case file, not a tragedy. A frame of scaffolding holds projected scene titles ('The shave', 'The fairground'), each a piece of evidence in an institutional file, with harsh white light and visible stagehands refusing illusion. Woyzeck is demonstrated as much as played: the actor steps out of role after the Doctor's experiments to state, flatly, the going rate for a human body, converting the audience's pity into indictment of the institutions — army, science, church, economy — that the scenes parade. The horse fair's crowd, barker included, becomes a gestic chorus: consumption at every level. But one decision is deliberately Stanislavskian: in the murder by the pond, the house is held in a single cold special while Woyzeck plays the action truthfully and silently — the single crack in the alienation frame, justified as the point where a case file becomes a person. The contemporary meaning lands as system critique: the audience leaves judging mechanisms, not a madman.
As a performer, explain how you would apply Stanislavski's System to play Hedda Gabler in the burning of the manuscript.
Answer: Preparation begins with given circumstances: Hedda has learned the manuscript is Lovborg and Thea's 'child', the child her own marriage denied her; her super-objective — to control her life while appearing to submit — has just found its most destructive tactic available. In the unit, her objective is 'to destroy what I cannot have' pursued under the surface tactic 'to comfort Thea' — playing the comforting behaviour truthfully while the burning advances the true objective is the subtext the audience must read. The magic if personalises the stakes: what would I do if the one thing that made my life bearable were someone else's triumph? Tempo-rhythm carries the scene: her earlier brittle briskness slows into deliberate, almost ceremonial calm as she feeds the pages to the fire — the calm signals decision, not madness. Method of physical actions governs the playing: the small truthful doings (fanning the pages, warming her hands) generate the feeling an audience recognises as horror behind hospitality. The intended effect: we watch a woman commit vandalism as if it were a sacrament, and understand exactly why.
