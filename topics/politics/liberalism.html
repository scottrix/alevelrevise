Liberalism: Classical and Modern

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA, Edexcel

A* revision of liberalism for AQA 7152 (Paper 3) and Edexcel 9PL0 (Paper 1 core political ideas): core themes, classical vs modern strands, Locke to Friedan, and the tensions examiners reward.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • State the six core themes of liberalism (individualism, freedom, rationalism, equality and social justice, the state, liberal democracy) and attach a named thinker to each (AQA 3.3.1; Edexcel 9PL0 core political ideas) (AO1).
  • Distinguish classical from modern liberalism on the meaning of freedom, the role of the state and the nature of equality, citing Locke, Mill, Rawls and Friedan (AO1/AO2).
  • Explain why Hobbes is quoted in liberal debates for his state-of-nature and social-contract method despite illiberal conclusions (AO2).
  • Analyse tensions within liberalism - negative vs positive freedom, atomistic vs developmental individualism, minimal vs enabling state - and judge how fundamental they are (AO2/AO3).
  • Apply liberal ideas to liberal democracy: consent, constitutionalism, toleration, rule of law (AO2).
  • Construct balanced essay arguments with substantiated judgements that meet AQA 25-mark extract criteria and Edexcel 30-mark essay criteria (AO3).

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Individualism: The belief that the individual is the primary unit of politics and has priority over society and the state; 'atomistic' (classical) individuals are self-reliant, 'developmental' (modern) individuals seek self-realisation.
Foundational equality: Individuals are born with equal moral worth and natural rights (life, liberty, property) that no government may legitimately remove (Locke).
Formal equality: Equal legal and political status - equal rights under law, equal votes, equality of opportunity rather than of outcome.
Negative freedom: Freedom as the absence of external constraint on the individual; the classical liberal conception (Mill, Nozick).
Positive freedom: Freedom as self-mastery and possession of the resources to develop one's potential; the modern liberal conception (Green, Rawls).
Harm principle: Mill's rule that individual liberty may only be restricted to prevent harm to others; self-regarding actions are beyond legitimate interference (On Liberty, 1859).
Enabling state: The modern liberal state that uses welfare, education and regulation to expand individuals' capacity to help themselves, rather than to control them.
Social contract: The agreement by which individuals leave the state of nature and consent to government in exchange for protection of their rights; legitimate authority rests on consent (Locke).
Constitutionalism: The principle that government power should be limited and fragmented by a constitution, separation of powers and the rule of law.
Meritocracy: A society in which position and reward reflect ability and effort rather than birth or privilege; linked by liberals to equality of opportunity.
Veil of ignorance: Rawls' device for identifying a just society: choose its rules without knowing your own class, gender or talents, so the position of the least advantaged still satisfies the difference principle (A Theory of Justice, 1971).

📌 Key Points

Shared ground of all liberals: humans are rational; individuals have equal moral worth; the state must rest on consent, be limited by law and protect rights.
Classical strand (Locke, Mill, Nozick): negative freedom, minimal night-watchman state, laissez-faire economics, formal equality and meritocracy.
Modern strand (Green, Rawls, Friedan): positive freedom, enabling welfare state, managed capitalism, social justice as fairness.
The central tension is the meaning of freedom and the size of the state - crystallised in Nozick vs Rawls over redistribution ('forced labour' vs the difference principle).
Friedan applies liberal individualism to gender: legal equality is empty without tackling cultural conditioning ('the problem that has no name').

Core themes of liberalism (AQA 7152 3.3.1; Edexcel 9PL0 core political ideas)

Both specs fix the same six themes: individualism, freedom, rationalism, equality and social justice, the state and liberal democracy. Examiners reward theme-plus-thinker anchoring - never describe a theme generically without attaching a named liberal.

Liberal democracy is where the themes converge, and both boards explicitly test the link. Liberal democracy fuses consent with limitation: it is always constitutional democracy - hedged with rights, separation of powers and rule of law - because liberals fear untrammelled majorities as much as untrammelled rulers. Treat democracy as a means of protecting the individual, never as an end in itself: that framing answers half of all 'why do liberals support X?' questions.

Classical liberalism: negative freedom and the minimal state

Locke supplies the foundations (Two Treatises of Government, 1689). The state of nature is pre-political but not lawless: natural law grants every individual natural rights to life, liberty and property - foundational equality. Because rights are insecure where each person judges their own cause, rational individuals contract into government - but the contract is conditional: authority rests on consent, is limited to protecting property, and a government that destroys rights may be resisted and replaced. Locke's fingerprints are on the Glorious Revolution and the American Declaration of Independence ('life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness').

Mill radicalises individualism (On Liberty, 1859). Liberty is negative: the absence of constraint on self-regarding actions. The harm principle is the only legitimate ground for interference - preventing harm to others; 'over himself, over his own body and mind, the individual is sovereign'. Toleration follows: even one dissenting voice must be heard against apparent unanimity, because silenced opinion may be true and because truth needs challenge to stay alive. In Considerations on Representative Government (1861) Mill backs representative democracy - but proposes plural voting weighted to the educated, and in The Subjection of Women (1869) extends liberal individualism to gender. These later commitments are the transitional hinge to modern liberalism - exam gold for 'do liberals agree?' questions.

Nozick is the classical endpoint (Anarchy, State and Utopia, 1974). Self-ownership: individuals own their bodies, talents and labour, so redistributive taxation for welfare is 'on a par with forced labour'. The only just state is the minimal ('night-watchman') state - protection of person and property - and even that must arise by an 'invisible-hand' process from voluntary protective associations, not by design. Nozick targets the New Deal and Great Society settlements: roll back the welfare state, restore market liberty.

Hobbes sits in conservatism on both specs but is quoted in liberal debates for his method, not his conclusions (Leviathan, 1651). His state of nature is a war 'of every man against every man', so rational individuals contract to an absolute sovereign for order. The contractual, individualist reasoning seeds liberalism; the conclusion - no natural rights, no limited government, no right of rebellion - is precisely what Locke reverses. Use Hobbes to show what classical liberalism had to reject in order to be liberal.

Economically, classical liberalism aligns with laissez-faire: free markets, self-help and minimal regulation, on the argument that market liberty is individual liberty. Meritocratic reward reflects differing talent and effort - inequality of outcome is legitimate where opportunity is formally equal.

Modern liberalism: positive freedom and the enabling state

T.H. Green reframes freedom. Freedom is positive: the power to develop one's potential. Poverty, ignorance, disease and squalid housing are barriers to freedom, so removing them enlarges liberty rather than taxing it. Humans are developmental individualists - socially embedded, not atomised - and social institutions should enable self-realisation.

Positive freedom was enacted by the Liberal welfare reforms of 1906-14 (Old Age Pensions Act 1908, National Insurance Act 1911, school meals, labour exchanges): the state equips people to help themselves. The Beveridge Report (1942) - attacking the 'Five Giants' of want, disease, ignorance, squalor and idleness - and the post-war welfare state are modern liberalism's practical legacy.

Rawls gives modern liberalism its definitive theory (A Theory of Justice, 1971): 'justice as fairness'. Rational individuals in the original position, behind the veil of ignorance (not knowing their class, gender, race or talents), would choose two principles: (1) an equal claim to the most extensive basic liberties compatible with like liberty for all; (2) social and economic inequalities only if attached to positions open to all and to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged - the difference principle. Redistribution is thus rational self-insurance, and the enabling state - welfare, education, regulated capitalism - is what free and equal persons would consent to. Rawls' first principle is squarely Millian: modern liberalism claims continuity with Locke and Mill, not rupture.

Friedan applies liberal individualism to gender (The Feminine Mystique, 1963). Domesticity traps educated women in a role that blocks self-realisation - 'the problem that has no name'. Formal equality (the vote, legal status) is necessary but insufficient: cultural conditioning must also be dismantled, through legal reform (the National Organization for Women, founded 1966; the Equal Rights Amendment campaign) and changed expectations. Friedan tests whether liberalism's tools - rights, opportunity, rational argument - can reach private-sphere inequality.

Keynes supplies the economics: the Great Depression showed markets can fail, so managed capitalism - countercyclical spending rather than laissez-faire - is compatible with, indeed supportive of, liberty. Modern liberals therefore accept state regulation and spending as freedom's infrastructure, not its enemy.

Tensions within liberalism: classical vs modern (AO2/AO3)

Examinable tensions are differences within a shared framework. State the tension, cite thinkers on both sides, then judge.

  • Freedom: negative (Mill, Nozick) vs positive (Green, Rawls). Is the welfare state liberty's protector or its destroyer?
  • The state: minimal night-watchman vs enabling welfare state - crystallised in Nozick vs Rawls over redistribution: 'forced labour' vs the difference principle. The sharpest clash in the whole ideology.
  • Equality: formal equality and meritocracy (classical) vs social justice and fairer outcomes (modern). Classical worry: redistribution punishes success and violates self-ownership. Modern worry: unequal starts make formal equality hollow.
  • Individualism: atomistic and self-reliant (classical) vs developmental and socially embedded (modern).
  • Democracy: classical fear of the 'tyranny of the majority' (Mill's plural voting) vs modern acceptance of universal suffrage as enabling participation.

Judgement options. Differences are significant but not fundamental: moderns claim to extend the tradition - the enabling state widens the sphere of real choice, Rawls' first principle protects equal basic liberties, and both strands retain individual primacy, consent, constitutionalism and toleration. Alternatively, argue the differences are fundamental at the extremes: on the size and purpose of the state, Nozick and Rawls are irreconcilable. Either judgement scores - provided it is argued, evidenced with thinkers, and stated as a conclusion, not a hedge.

Liberal democracy, thinker comparison and essay technique

Both strands have governed in practice: the 1906-14 Liberal government (New Liberal reform) and Attlee's 1945-51 settlement (Beveridge, positive freedom). The features of liberal democracy - consent, limited government, toleration, rule of law, protection of rights - are the baseline for both boards' questions asking how liberal ideas shape liberal democracy.

Exam technique: AQA 9-markers, AQA 25-mark extracts, Edexcel 30-markers

AQA 9-markers ('Explain and analyse three ways in which...') need three distinct points, each a claim + named thinker + analysis - three marks per point, no introduction or conclusion needed. AQA 25-mark items are extract-based: use the extract's words, then evaluate with thinkers - explicit agreement or disagreement with the extract's author is rewarded. Edexcel Paper 1 ideology questions are 30-markers ('To what extent do liberals agree about...'): balanced paragraphs, counter-arguments, a substantiated judgement, and at least three key thinkers used accurately. Universal trap: narrating thinkers' lives. Analysis means explaining why a claim follows from a liberal premise - not what happened to Mill.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: Explain and analyse three ways in which liberal thinkers have supported the concept of the state. [9 marks] (AQA 7152 Paper 3 style) --- AND --- To what extent do liberals agree about the role of the state? [30 marks] (Edexcel 9PL0 Paper 1, core political ideas)
Mark-scheme logic: 9-marker: three points, each claim + thinker + analysis. (1) Locke - the state is created by consent through the social contract to protect natural rights (life, liberty, property); without it, rights are insecure in the state of nature. (2) Mill - the state is justified by the harm principle: it may restrain only other-regarding actions, leaving self-regarding liberty untouched. (3) Rawls - the enabling state is what rational individuals behind the veil of ignorance would consent to: equal basic liberties plus the difference principle. The 30-marker additionally needs: agreements (the state as a necessary evil, limited by law, resting on consent, protecting property); disagreements (minimal vs enabling state; negative vs positive freedom; Nozick's 'forced labour' claim vs Rawls' difference principle); a substantiated judgement (e.g. the strands agree the state must be consent-based and limited but diverge radically on how large it should be - or that moderns extend Locke rather than contradict him); balanced paragraphs with counter-arguments and dated key works.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Explain and analyse three ways in which liberal thinkers have supported the idea of freedom. [9 marks]
Answer: (1) Negative freedom (Mill): liberty means absence of constraint; the harm principle confines interference to preventing harm to others, so self-regarding actions - speech, belief, lifestyle - must be left free. Analysis: freedom is protected by limiting the state, not empowering it. (2) Natural liberty (Locke): in the state of nature individuals enjoy natural liberty under the law of nature; government exists to preserve life, liberty and property, and may be resisted if it destroys them. Analysis: freedom precedes the state and grounds a right of rebellion. (3) Positive freedom (Green/Rawls): freedom is the real capacity to develop one's potential; behind the veil of ignorance rational individuals secure equal basic liberties first, then conditions that make liberty meaningful for the least advantaged. Analysis: welfare is freedom's infrastructure. Marking: 3 marks per point - claim + thinker + analysis; dated key works earn credit.
Explain and analyse three ways in which liberal ideas have influenced liberal democracy. [9 marks]
Answer: (1) Consent (Locke): government's authority rests on the governed's agreement - expressed through regular, competitive elections and, historically, the right to replace a government that breaks the contract. (2) Limited government (Locke/Mill): constitutionalism fragments power through constitutions, separation of powers and rule of law, because concentrated power threatens the individual; Mill's 'tyranny of the majority' shows even democratic power must be hedged. (3) Toleration (Mill): the defence of free speech and minority rights underpins civil liberties, pluralism and religious tolerance within liberal-democratic states. Marking: three distinct mechanisms, each with a named thinker; analysis must connect the idea to a specific institutional feature of liberal democracy.
To what extent do modern liberals differ from classical liberals? [25 marks] (AQA-style essay practice - in the real paper this is extract-based)
Answer: Differences: freedom (negative vs positive), state (minimal vs enabling), equality (formal equality/meritocracy vs the difference principle), individualism (atomistic vs developmental); the extremes are Nozick vs Rawls. Continuities: rationalism, individual primacy, foundational equality, consent, constitutionalism, toleration; moderns claim continuity - Green builds on Mill's developmental hints (The Subjection of Women, 1869; support for worker cooperatives), and Rawls' first principle of equal basic liberties is Millian. Judgement: differences of degree within a shared framework - the same measure (the individual), a different account of what freedom requires; at the extremes, however, the state's size and purpose differ so radically (night-watchman vs difference principle) that the split looks fundamental. Structure: intro defining the strands; paragraphs on freedom, the state and equality, each giving both sides; conclusion with a substantiated judgement.
To what extent do liberals agree about human nature? [30 marks]
Answer: Agreements: humans are rational and of equal moral worth; reason is not gendered (Wollstonecraft); individuals are capable of progress through debate and reform rather than force. Disagreements: atomistic, self-reliant egoists (classical) vs developmental, socially embedded beings (modern); whether reason needs enabling conditions - education, health, income (modern liberals) - or operates best when the state leaves individuals alone (Nozick). Mill sits astride the divide: plural voting implies reason is unevenly distributed, yet his education optimism and case for women's equality imply universal capacity. Judgement: core rationalism is shared - the disagreement is about the social conditions under which reason flourishes; significant but reconcilable, because both sides invoke rationalism to justify their politics. Structure: agreement paragraph, disagreement paragraphs with counter-argument, judgement with criteria.
To what extent do liberals support democracy? [30 marks]
Answer: Support: consent grounds authority (Locke's social contract) and elections are its mechanism; rational individuals can participate in public reason (Mill's representative government); democracy protects rights better than monarchy or aristocracy. Qualifications: fear of the 'tyranny of the majority' (Mill); liberal democracy must be constitutional - rights, separation of powers and rule of law hedge democratic power; Mill's plural voting shows support for representation rather than unfiltered majoritarianism. Judgement: liberals support democracy as a means of protecting the individual - democracy legitimates, but liberty limits; a democracy without constitutionalism ceases to be liberal. Structure: support, qualify, judgement with explicit criteria (consent, limitation, toleration).
