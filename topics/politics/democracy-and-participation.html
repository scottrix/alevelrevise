Democracy and Participation

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA, Edexcel

A* revision on democracy and participation for AQA 7152 and Edexcel 9PL0: direct versus representative democracy, the franchise since 1832, the participation crisis and reform — with full 9-, 25- and 30-mark model answers.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Distinguish direct from representative democracy and assess the advantages of each with UK examples (AQA 3.1.2.1; Edexcel P1 Topic 1)
  • Evaluate claims that the UK suffers a participation crisis, using turnout, party membership and partisan dealignment data (AO2/AO3)
  • Trace the development of the franchise from the Great Reform Act (1832) to the present, with the roles of the Chartists, Suffragists and Suffragettes (AO1)
  • Analyse the influence of pressure groups, think tanks, lobbyists and corporations on government and Parliament (AQA 3.1.2.4; Edexcel P1 Topic 1.4)
  • Evaluate democratic reforms since 1997 — devolution, the HRA, referendums, Lords reform, recall — and remaining deficits (AO2/AO3)
  • Construct top-band judgements on franchise extension, compulsory voting and the health of UK democracy (AO3)

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Direct democracy: Citizens make decisions themselves without intermediaries — through referendums, initiatives and e-petitions; used sparingly in the UK (1975 EEC, 2011 AV, 2016 EU referendums).
Representative democracy: Citizens elect representatives (MPs, peers, councillors) who take decisions on their behalf — Burkean trustees rather than delegates — and are accountable at elections.
Democratic deficit: The gap between democratic ideals and practice: low turnout, unaccountable institutions (the Lords), safe seats and executive dominance reduce the legitimacy of decisions.
Participation crisis: The claim that declining turnout, collapsing party membership and partisan dealignment show citizens disengaging from formal politics — contested by evidence of referendum turnout and online activism.
Suffrage / franchise: The right to vote in public elections; extended from roughly one adult male in seven in 1832 to universal adult suffrage at 18 by 1969.
Universal suffrage: The franchise held by all adult citizens regardless of property, gender or class — achieved in stages: 1918 (all men, women over 30), 1928 (equal franchise), 1969 (age 18).
Partisan dealignment: The long-term decline in stable class-based party loyalty, making voters floating and elections more volatile — a key indicator used in participation-crisis arguments.
Pluralist democracy: A system in which power is dispersed among competing groups — parties, pressure groups, media — whose competition approximates the public interest (AQA 3.1.2.4's 'pluralism').
Insider pressure group: A group with regular access to government decision-making (e.g. the BMA, NFU, CBI); effective but dependent on political favour.
Outsider pressure group: A group without access to government that campaigns externally through media and direct action (e.g. Just Stop Oil, Fathers 4 Justice); attention-grabbing but rarely consulted.
Think tank: A research body generating policy ideas outside parties and government (e.g. IPPR, Demos, Policy Exchange, IEA) — an 'other influence' on government named in both specs.
Recall election: Under the Recall of MPs Act 2015, an MP convicted of an offence or suspended for at least 10 sitting days faces a by-election if 10% of constituents sign a recall petition.

📌 Key Points

Turnout collapsed to 59.4% in 2001 but recovered to 67.3% (2019) — the participation crisis is contested, not settled.
2016 EU referendum: 72% turnout, 52–48 — the biggest act of direct democracy in UK history.
Franchise milestones: 1832 → 1867 → 1884 → 1918 → 1928 → 1969 (age 18); Chartists, Suffragists, Suffragettes drove change.
Recall of MPs Act 2015 triggered two by-elections (Onasanya and Davies, both 2019) — first successful recalls in UK history.
Other influences named in both specs: think tanks (Policy Exchange, IPPR), lobbyists (Transparency of Lobbying Act 2014), corporations (CBI) and the media.

Types of democracy and the state of UK democracy

Direct democracy means citizens decide issues themselves. UK use: UK-wide referendums on EEC membership (1975, 67% Yes), the Alternative Vote (2011, 68% No on a 42% turnout) and EU membership (2016, 52–48 on a 72% turnout — the largest act of direct democracy in UK history); devolved referendums (Scottish independence 2014, 55–45 on an 84.6% turnout — the highest for any UK vote since universal suffrage); and e-petitions, where 100,000 signatures make a petition eligible for consideration for debate — the 2019 'revoke Article 50' petition reached 6.1m signatures, the largest ever, and was debated but not acted on. Representative democracy means electing intermediaries: MPs, devolved and local representatives, under Burkean trustee rather than delegate logic. Advantages of representation: expertise, protection of minorities from majority tyranny, feasibility for complex government, and stable accountable government; advantages of direct democracy: legitimacy for constitutional questions, engagement, and decisions immune to party spin. A* answers use both: direct democracy suits issues of principle (Brexit, Scottish independence), representation suits technical governance — few voters can audit a budget.

The participation crisis debate is the topic's core evaluation. Evidence for: general election turnout collapsed from ~84% (1950) to 59.4% (2001) and fell again to 59.7% (2024); party membership fell from millions in the 1950s (when around one in ten adults belonged) to under 500,000 combined today; partisan dealignment has made class voting unreliable and elections volatile; the 2009 expenses scandal damaged trust; and the majority of constituencies are safe seats where individual votes rarely matter. Evidence against: 2016 (72%) and 2017 (68.8%) turnout spikes; the Scottish referendum's 84.6%; surging online participation — 38 Degrees, Change.org, #MeToo and Black Lives Matter; and pressure-group membership that dwarfs party membership (the National Trust and RSPB each claim more members than all UK parties combined). The judgement that scores: 'crisis' overstates engagement collapse — participation has migrated from parties and elections to causes, campaigns and referendums.

Political participation: pressure groups, think tanks, lobbyists, corporations

Both specs require analysis of participation beyond voting and parties. Pressure groups divide by access and motive. Insider groups hold regular access to government: the BMA shaped NHS policy and its 2023–24 strike ballot moved ministers; the NFU and CBI (a business group) are consulted routinely. Outsider groups campaign externally: Just Stop Oil (road blockages, 2022–23) and Insulate Britain generated headlines and pushed energy policy up the agenda but achieved little direct policy change; Snowdrop (after Dunblane, 1996–97) won the firearms ban — proof outsider groups can succeed when public sympathy is overwhelming. Promotional groups campaign for a cause (Liberty, Amnesty); interest groups defend members (unions, professional bodies). Success factors: resources and organisation, government sympathy, opposition status, public opinion and method fit.

Think tanks generate the policy ideas parties borrow: New Labour drew on IPPR and Demos; Cameron's modernisation on Policy Exchange; Truss's 2022 mini-budget drew heavily on the Institute of Economic Affairs' free-market agenda — influence that can outlast any one government. Lobbyists broker access: the Transparency of Lobbying, Non-Party Campaigning and Trade Union Administration Act 2014 created a register of consultant lobbyists — criticised as narrow (it misses in-house lobbying); the 'cash for questions' scandal (1994, Neil Hamilton) and 'cash for access' sting (2012, Peter Cruddas) show why regulation matters, and subsequent governments have promised a broader statutory register. Corporations exert structural influence: the CBI and Institute of Directors advise ministers directly, large employers lobby on regulation and tax, and post-Brexit trade policy has multiplied corporate access points. The media frames participation: endorsements matter at the margin (the Sun's switch of support has been credited with swing effects since 1992), while online platforms — from the 2016 campaign's targeted advertising to COVID-era activism — have shifted participation from membership to mobilisation.

For evaluation: these 'other influences' (AQA 3.1.2.4; Edexcel 1.4) cut both ways for democracy — pluralism enriches debate and represents minorities, but resources and access skew influence toward the wealthy and well-organised, so pressure-group politics is a fair-weather democracy: excellent at amplifying organised voices, weak at the unorganised.

The franchise: from 1832 to today

Both specs require the development of suffrage 'from the Great Reform Act (1832) to the present'. Learn the milestones as a chain:

ReformWhat it didSignificance
Great Reform Act 1832Abolished rotten boroughs, enfranchised the middle classes; electorate ~650,000First step — about one adult male in five could vote
Second Reform Act 1867Enfranchised urban working men; electorate ~2.2mDoubled the electorate; the working class in towns got the vote
Third Reform Act 1884Extended the franchise to rural working men; ~two-thirds of adult menFranchise roughly matched between town and country
Representation of the People Act 1918All men 21+; women 30+ with property qualificationsUniversal male suffrage; 8.4m women enfranchised after WWI
Equal Franchise Act 1928Women 21+ on the same terms as menUniversal adult suffrage achieved
Representation of the People Act 1969Voting age lowered to 18 (first used 1970)The last major extension — 'Votes at 18' won by WWI-era campaigners' successors

The campaigns behind the acts: the Chartists (People's Charter 1838: universal male suffrage, secret ballot, no property qualification, paid MPs, equal constituencies, annual parliaments) failed in their lifetime but five of six demands were later adopted; the Suffragists (NUWSS, Millicent Fawcett) campaigned constitutionally for decades; the Suffragettes (WSPU, the Pankhursts) used militancy — window-smashing, arson, hunger strikes and the 'cat and mouse' policy — whose contribution to 1918 is debated: disruption publicised the cause, but historians argue WWI service and changing attitudes delivered the vote. Suffrage is now treated as a human right (free elections under ECHR Protocol 1, Art 3) — Hirst v UK (2005) ruled the blanket ban on prisoner voting unlawful; Parliament defied it, showing the franchise remains under parliamentary control.

Modern debates: Votes at 16 — already used for Scottish Parliament, Senedd and local elections in Scotland and Wales and by 16-year-olds in the Isle of Man and Jersey; the government elected in 2024 has committed to lowering the voting age to 16 for UK general elections. For: 16-year-olds pay tax, work and serve; consistency across UK elections. Against: maturity and engagement; few countries do it. Compulsory voting (Australia: turnout ~90%+): for — ends free-rider abstention, forces parties to campaign broadly; against — ill-informed votes, liberty, the ballot box is not the only participation route. Prisoner voting — Parliament's defiance of Hirst is itself a franchise debate. EU citizens lost no general-election franchise (they never had one) but the 2016 referendum excluded most EU nationals resident in the UK — an exclusion critics called arbitrary.

Democratic reform since 1997: achievements and deficits

Since 1997, reforms have targeted the deficits the franchise alone cannot fix:

  • Devolution (Scotland 1999, Wales, Northern Ireland) — power brought closer to voters, though England's own devolution (metro mayors, abolished EVEL in 2021) remains partial — the 'English question' persists.
  • Human Rights Act 1998 — positive, justiciable rights for every citizen (see the judiciary topic for Miller I/II and A v SSHD).
  • Freedom of Information Act 2000 — transparency that exposed the 2009 expenses scandal: participation's biggest trust shock.
  • House of Lords Act 1999 — removed most hereditary peers; 92 remained, and the government elected in 2024 committed to removing the rest — an unelected second chamber remains the largest single democratic deficit.
  • Recall of MPs Act 2015 — constituents can force a by-election after conviction or serious suspension; first successful recalls: Fiona Onasanya (Peterborough, 2019) and Christopher Davies (Brecon and Radnorshire, 2019).
  • Referendums and the 2011 AV vote — direct democracy used and, on AV, rejected (68% No): voters defended FPTP.
  • Fixed-term Parliaments Act 2011 — set five-year terms to stop PMs calling opportunistic elections; repealed in 2022 (Dissolution and Calling of Parliament Act) — a reform undone.

Deficits that remain: the unelected Lords; FPTP disproportionality — UKIP won 3.9m votes and 1 seat in 2015, and the Conservatives took a majority of seats on 43.6% in 2019 — safe seats and wasted votes persist; turnout dips (59.7% in 2024); executive dominance of a largely ceremonial Commons; money in politics (party-funding scandals; the 2014 lobbying act's narrow register); and online disinformation in the 2016 and 2019 campaigns. The A* evaluation: reform has widened the franchise's exercise (devolution, recall, petitions, rights) but left the distribution of power between institutions largely untouched — democracy has become more participatory without becoming much more representative.

Essay strategy and synoptic links

This topic's essay questions are predictable: the participation crisis (AQA 25 / Edexcel 30), direct versus representative democracy, franchise extension (votes at 16, compulsory voting) and pressure groups and democracy. Top-band structure:

  1. Define the standard, then judge against it: 'participation crisis' only means something if you set a threshold — turnout below X, membership below Y — then test the data against it.
  2. Both directions, each with data: turnout 59.4% (2001) vs 72% (2016); party membership collapse vs 6.1m-signature petition; safe seats vs 2017 turnout surge.
  3. Use the spec's named examples: Chartists, Suffragists, Suffragettes (AQA 3.1.2.1); one insider and one outsider group in detail (AQA 3.1.2.4); think tanks, lobbyists, corporations, media.
  4. A judgement that answers the question: participation has migrated (from parties to causes and referendums) rather than collapsed; direct democracy legitimises constitutional questions but cannot replace representative government; franchise extension is a one-way trend whose pace — 16-year-olds, prisoners — is the live debate.

Synoptic links: the constitution (codification and entrenchment would protect reforms such as the HRA from reversal — the FTA's 2022 repeal is the proof); Parliament (safe seats and executive dominance link to the Commons' limited influence); the executive (referendums as Foley's plebiscitary leadership — Cameron's 2016 gamble); and the judiciary (Miller I reasserted parliamentary sovereignty — representative democracy — at the moment of maximum direct-democracy pressure; Hirst shows the franchise remains parliamentary). One-line judgement bank for the top band: 'The UK is a mature representative democracy with an active direct-democracy supplement and a stubborn institutional deficit — the Lords, FPTP and money in politics — that reform since 1997 has narrowed but not closed.'

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: Evaluate the view that the UK is now suffering from a participation crisis (AQA 7152, 25 marks) | Evaluate the extent to which democracy in the UK would be improved by further reform (Edexcel 9PL0 Paper 1, 30 marks)
Mark-scheme logic: Mark-scheme logic for a top-band answer. AO1: accurate data and examples — turnout (84% in 1950, 59.4% in 2001, 72% in 2016), party membership (millions in the 1950s to under 500,000), partisan dealignment, referendums (1975, 2011, 2016, Scottish 2014), e-petitions (2019 revoke petition, 6.1m), franchise acts (1832–1969), reforms since 1997 (devolution, HRA, FOIA, Lords Act 1999, Recall Act 2015). AO2: analysis in both directions — for crisis: turnout, membership, trust post-expenses, safe seats; against: referendum turnout surges, online activism, pressure-group membership. AO3: sustained judgement — participation has migrated from parties and elections to causes and referendums rather than collapsed; 'crisis' requires a threshold the data does not uniformly meet; for reform versions, conclude reform widened participation but left institutional power (Lords, FPTP, executive dominance) largely intact.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Explain and analyse three features of direct democracy in the UK (AQA, 9 marks)
Answer: First, referendums: citizens decide issues directly — UK-wide votes in 1975 (EEC, 67% Yes), 2011 (AV, 68% No) and 2016 (EU, 52–48 on a 72% turnout), plus the 2014 Scottish independence referendum (55–45, 84.6% turnout); they provide legitimacy for constitutional questions that representatives alone cannot supply. Second, e-petitions: petitions with 100,000 signatures are eligible for consideration for parliamentary debate — the 2019 'revoke Article 50' petition gathered 6.1m signatures, the largest ever, showing mass engagement but also the limits of direct democracy: it was debated and ignored. Third, initiatives and online mobilisation: platforms such as 38 Degrees and Change.org let citizens set the agenda without party intermediaries — direct participation that bypasses both Parliament and parties, though it lacks the decision-making power that would make it binding. The features combine engagement with weakness: UK direct democracy advises and legitimises but rarely decides, which is why representative government remains the default.
Analyse how suffrage has changed since the Great Reform Act of 1832 (AQA, 9 marks)
Answer: Suffrage has extended in stages, each driven by campaign pressure and war. The Great Reform Act 1832 abolished rotten boroughs and enfranchised the middle classes (electorate ~650,000 — about one adult male in five); the 1867 Act brought in urban working men (~2.2m) and the 1884 Act rural workers (two-thirds of adult men). The Representation of the People Act 1918 delivered universal male suffrage and enfranchised 8.4m women over 30 — the culmination of the Suffragists' constitutional campaigning (NUWSS, Fawcett) and the Suffragettes' militancy (WSPU, the Pankhursts), whose disruption publicised the cause while WWI service changed attitudes; the Equal Franchise Act 1928 equalised the franchise at 21. The Representation of the People Act 1969 lowered the voting age to 18, completing universal adult suffrage. The pattern is one-way extension driven by extra-parliamentary campaigns — the Chartists' six demands failed in 1838–48 but five were later adopted — while the remaining debates (votes at 16, prisoner voting) show the frontier is now age and conduct rather than class or gender.
Evaluate the extent to which the UK suffers from a participation crisis (Edexcel, 30 marks)
Answer: Evidence for a crisis is substantial. Turnout: general election turnout fell from about 84% in 1950 to 59.4% in 2001 and again to 59.7% in 2024 — well under two-thirds in most modern elections. Membership: party membership collapsed from millions in the 1950s (around one in ten adults) to under 500,000 across all parties, so fewer citizens are embedded in formal politics. Dealignment: partisan dealignment has weakened class loyalties, producing volatile, transactional voting. Trust: the 2009 expenses scandal, Brexit division and disinformation in the 2016 and 2019 campaigns depressed trust in representatives, and the majority of seats are safe, so most votes do not affect outcomes. Against the crisis thesis: turnout is cyclical rather than secular — 72% in the 2016 referendum, 68.8% in 2017, and 84.6% in the Scottish referendum show voters turn out when stakes are high. Participation has migrated, not vanished: online platforms (38 Degrees, Change.org), movements (#MeToo, Black Lives Matter) and pressure-group membership — the National Trust and RSPB each exceed all party memberships combined — reveal cause-based engagement. Direct democracy is increasingly used and demanded. Judgement: 'crisis' overstates — the threshold it implies (broad disengagement from politics as such) is not met; what has happened is a shift from institutional participation (parties, elections) to issue participation (referendums, campaigns, online mobilisation). The real deficits are structural — safe seats, the unelected Lords and money in politics — and they depress the value of participation rather than the amount of it.
Evaluate the view that pressure groups enhance democracy in the UK (AQA, 25 marks)
Answer: Pressure groups enhance democracy in a pluralist model. They represent minorities and single issues that parties aggregate away: Liberty and Amnesty defend civil liberties between elections; Snowdrop (after Dunblane, 1996–97) won a firearms ban that parties had avoided; the BMA's 2023–24 strike ballot forced NHS pay onto the agenda. They educate and mobilise — insider groups (BMA, NFU, CBI) provide expertise that improves policy, outsider groups (Just Stop Oil, Insulate Britain) force neglected issues into debate — and they give participation a channel between elections, deepening democracy beyond the five-yearly vote. Against: pluralism is skewed by resources and access. Insider influence depends on government favour — the NFU is powerful because ministers need it, not because its case is strongest; outsider success is rare and often symbolically hollow (Just Stop Oil generated headlines and arrests but little policy change). Wealthy business groups and corporations lobby continuously through channels the Transparency of Lobbying Act 2014's narrow register does not capture (it lists consultant lobbyists only, not in-house lobbying), and 'cash for questions' (1994) and 'cash for access' (2012) show access can be bought. Direct-action groups can also substitute intimidation for deliberation. Judgement: pressure groups enhance democracy for the organised — they widen participation, expertise and minority representation — but the system is not genuinely pluralist, because access tracks resources and political favour; democracy is enhanced at the margins and distorted at the centre, which is why regulation of lobbying and party funding, not more groups, is the binding constraint.
Evaluate the extent to which the franchise should be extended to 16-year-olds (Edexcel, 30 marks)
Answer: The case for extension is strong on consistency and practice. Sixteen-year-olds already vote in Scottish Parliament and Senedd elections (Scotland since 2017, Wales since 2021) and in the Isle of Man and Jersey, and the government elected in 2024 committed to lowering the voting age for UK general elections — so the question is consistency, not novelty. Arguments for: 16-year-olds pay income tax and National Insurance, can work full-time, join the armed forces (with consent) and marry; no taxation without representation is a coherent principle; the UK's own 1969 decision to lower the age from 21 to 18 followed the same logic. Early voting also builds a habit: evidence from Scotland suggests first-time voters at 16 engage more reliably over a lifetime. The case against is maturity and engagement. Critics argue most 16-year-olds lack the financial independence and life experience — housing, pensions, taxation across a lifetime — that much policy determines, and that the minimum ages for other civic acts (jury service, standing for Parliament) remain 18 or higher, so votes at 16 is inconsistent in the opposite direction. Turnout data from devolved elections shows 16–17-year-olds turn out at lower rates than older first-time voters, and opponents argue parties would target schools rather than argue policy. Wider context: the franchise has always expanded — 1832, 1867, 1884, 1918, 1928, 1969 — and each extension was resisted on the same maturity grounds, then accepted as normal; suffrage is now framed as a human right (ECHR Protocol 1, Art 3). Judgement: extension to 16 is justified by consistency, civic contribution and the one-way historical trend of the franchise — but its success depends on political education, since the evidence on youth turnout is mixed and a low-turnout extension would weaken rather than strengthen democracy.
