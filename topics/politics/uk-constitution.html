The UK Constitution

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA, Edexcel

A* A-level Politics revision on the UK constitution for AQA 7152 and Edexcel 9PL0: nature and sources, reforms since 1997, devolution and the codification debate — with full 25- and 30-mark model answers.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Describe the nature of the UK constitution — uncodified, unentrenched, unitary — and explain parliamentary sovereignty and the rule of law (AQA 3.1.1.1; Edexcel P2 1.1)
  • Identify the five sources of the constitution and illustrate each with statutes, cases or conventions (AO1)
  • Analyse the constitutional reforms of 1997–2010 and 2010 to the present, assessing their significance (AQA 3.1.1.2; Edexcel P2 1.2)
  • Explain the role and powers of the Scottish Parliament, Senedd and Northern Ireland Assembly (AQA 3.1.1.3; Edexcel P2 1.3)
  • Evaluate the impact of devolution on the UK, including the West Lothian Question and quasi-federalism (AO2/AO3)
  • Construct balanced arguments for and against codification and further reform (AQA 3.1.1.4; Edexcel P2 1.4)

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Uncodified constitution: A constitution not contained in a single authoritative written document; its rules come from multiple sources, some written (statutes, cases) and some unwritten (conventions).
Entrenchment: Special legal protection making a constitutional rule hard to amend or repeal; the UK constitution is unentrenched, so Parliament can change it by simple majority.
Parliamentary sovereignty: The principle that Parliament is the supreme law-making body: it can legislate on any matter, cannot bind its successors and no court can strike down an Act (A V Dicey).
Rule of law: Dicey's principle that no one may be punished without a fair trial, all are equal before the law, and rights result from the ordinary law rather than a written constitution.
Unitary state: A state in which legal sovereignty rests with one central parliament; devolved bodies exercise delegated, not sovereign, power.
Statute law: Law made by Parliament in Acts; the most important source of the constitution (e.g. Human Rights Act 1998, Scotland Act 1998).
Common law: Judge-made law developed through precedent, including the royal prerogative and fundamental rights (e.g. Entick v Carrington 1765).
Constitutional conventions: Unwritten rules of behaviour regarded as binding but not legally enforceable — e.g. royal assent is always granted and the prime minister must sit in the Commons.
Works of authority: Respected scholarly texts that clarify constitutional practice — Dicey's Introduction to the Study of the Law of the Constitution, Bagehot's The English Constitution, Erskine May's Parliamentary Practice.
Devolution: The transfer of powers from central government to subordinate elected bodies (Holyrood, Senedd, NI Assembly) without transferring sovereignty.
Quasi-federalism: A unitary state that in practice operates with federal-style features — powerful, asymmetrical devolved legislatures alongside a sovereign Westminster.
West Lothian Question: The anomaly that Scottish MPs at Westminster can vote on English matters while English MPs cannot vote on devolved Scottish matters.

📌 Key Points

Uncodified + unentrenched = flexible: any constitutional rule can be amended by a simple parliamentary majority.
Five sources: statute law, common law, conventions, works of authority, treaties.
Labour 1997–2010: devolution, HRA 1998, Lords Act 1999, FOI Act 2000, Constitutional Reform Act 2005 (Supreme Court 2009).
2010 onwards: FTPA 2011 (repealed 2022), Scotland Acts 2012/2016, EU Withdrawal Acts 2018/2020, DCPA 2022.
Miller II (2019): prorogation unlawful — courts willing to police the executive inside an uncodified constitution.
Devolution is asymmetric (Scotland strongest); England has no parliament — quasi-federal, not federal.

Nature and sources of the UK constitution (AQA 3.1.1.1; Edexcel P2 1.1)

The UK constitution is uncodified: there is no single, authoritative document setting out the rules of the political system. Its rules are scattered across statutes, court judgments, conventions and works of authority. It is also unentrenched — no rule enjoys special legal protection, so any element can be amended or repealed by a simple parliamentary majority. This makes the constitution flexible: major changes (devolution, the Human Rights Act) were enacted without special procedures, in contrast to the cumbersome amendment processes of codified constitutions.

Three principles anchor the system. Parliamentary sovereignty (A V Dicey): Parliament is the supreme legal authority — it can make or unmake any law, cannot bind its successors, and no court may strike down an Act of Parliament. The rule of law: no one may be punished without a fair trial, everyone is equally subject to the law, and rights arise from the ordinary law. The unitary state: legal sovereignty rests at Westminster; devolution (1998 onwards) delegated power to Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland without sharing sovereignty — although in practice the UK now displays quasi-federal features.

The contrast with codified constitutions is a favourite examiner's line. In the USA the constitution is entrenched: amendment needs supermajorities and the Supreme Court can strike down Acts of Congress as unconstitutional. In the UK, sovereignty means judges can interpret Acts and declare incompatibility but cannot invalidate primary legislation — so constitutional change is political, not judicial. Note too that much of the UK constitution is written: the HRA 1998, the Scotland Act 1998 and the Ministerial Code are all textual. What is missing is a single hierarchy of rules with higher-law status.

Why conventions matter in essays

Conventions show both the constitution's flexibility and its fragility. Because they are not legally enforceable they can be broken — as Johnson attempted with prorogation in 2019, when the Supreme Court ruled the advice to the monarch unlawful (Miller II), and as the Dissolution and Calling of Parliament Act 2022 demonstrated by reversing the statutory fixed-term rules. Use conventions to evaluate whether an uncodified constitution adequately constrains the executive.

Constitutional reform under Labour, 1997–2010 (AQA 3.1.1.2; Edexcel P2 1.2)

Labour entered office in 1997 with a programme of democratic renewal. The reforms devolved power, strengthened rights protection and separated the judiciary from the executive — but they were piecemeal rather than codifying.

  • Devolution (Scotland Act 1998, Government of Wales Act 1998, Northern Ireland Act 1998 alongside the Good Friday Agreement): created elected bodies in Edinburgh, Cardiff and Belfast with legislative and (in Scotland) tax-varying powers — the most transformative reform.
  • Human Rights Act 1998: incorporated the ECHR into UK law. Judges must interpret legislation compatibly with convention rights (s3); where that is impossible, courts may issue a declaration of incompatibility (s4), to which Parliament may respond — rights protection without overriding sovereignty. Public authorities are bound to act compatibly (s6).
  • House of Lords Act 1999: removed all but 92 hereditary peers, modernising the second chamber's composition.
  • Freedom of Information Act 2000: gave citizens a qualified right of access to information held by public bodies.
  • Constitutional Reform Act 2005: replaced the Law Lords with a UK Supreme Court (opened 2009) in a separate building, created an independent Judicial Appointments Commission and redefined the Lord Chancellor's role — a fuller separation of powers.

Evaluation: the reforms fragmented executive power and made rights justiciable in UK courts, but stopped short of codification, left Lords reform unfinished (the Wakeham Royal Commission 2000 proposed a largely appointed chamber; nothing was enacted) and made the constitution more complex and more litigious. Whether they strengthened democracy depends on the benchmark chosen: devolution clearly did; the HRA's reliance on parliamentary goodwill did not fully. The courts' use of the HRA is instructive: declarations of incompatibility in the Belmarsh case (A and others v SSHD, 2004) and Bellinger v Bellinger (2004) prompted legislative change, and Parliament responded in each — rights protection working as designed, if indirectly.

Impact detail: the reforms reshaped party politics too — the SNP's rise in Scotland, a distinctive Welsh Labour government, and power-sharing in Belfast. The Freedom of Information Act changed accountability culture: the MPs' expenses scandal (2009) was exposed through FOI requests. These effects make the Labour reforms a strong case study in 'most significant reform' questions — but they also show the constitution becoming more litigious, with the courts' role expanding.

Reform, 2010 to the present: coalition and Conservative governments (AQA 3.1.1.2; Edexcel P2 1.2)

The 2010–15 coalition produced the first statutory change to the electoral timetable: the Fixed-term Parliaments Act 2011 (FTPA), setting five-year terms and replacing the PM's dissolution prerogative with a two-thirds Commons vote or a loss of confidence. It was used once — the 2017 early election — and tested unsuccessfully in 2019, when May's third attempt at an early election required the separate Early Parliamentary General Election Act 2019 instead; its repeal in 2022 restored the prime minister's traditional advantage over dissolution timing. The EU Act 2011 required a referendum for any further transfer of power to the EU. Devolution deepened: the Scotland Act 2012 and Wales Act 2014 devolved significant tax powers.

From 2015 Conservative governments continued reshaping the constitution: the Recall of MPs Act 2015 allowed constituents to trigger a recall petition — and a by-election — for MPs sentenced to prison or suspended from the House; the Scotland Act 2016 devolved income tax rates and bands and declared Holyrood's permanence; the European Union Withdrawal Act 2018 and EU (Withdrawal Agreement) Act 2020 ended the supremacy of EU law in the UK, returning competences to Westminster and the devolved legislatures. Ministers relied on wide delegated powers ('Henry VIII clauses') to amend retained EU law without full parliamentary scrutiny — a standard criticism linking the constitution to executive power. In 2022 the Dissolution and Calling of Parliament Act 2022 repealed the FTPA and restored the prerogative — a reversal illustrating the constitution's flexibility in both directions.

Judicial–executive friction sharpened during Brexit. In R (Miller) v Secretary of State for Exiting the EU (Miller I, 2017) the Supreme Court held that triggering Article 50 required parliamentary authorisation; in R (Miller) v The Prime Minister (Miller II, 2019) it ruled the 2019 prorogation unlawful because it had the effect of frustrating Parliament's ability to carry out its constitutional functions without reasonable justification. These cases show judges policing the boundary between prerogative and sovereignty — key evidence in debates on whether an uncodified constitution still protects against executive overreach. Plans to replace the HRA with a British Bill of Rights were announced and then abandoned in 2023, leaving the reform agenda unfinished.

Devolution: role, powers and impact (AQA 3.1.1.3; Edexcel P2 1.3)

Scotland: the Scottish Parliament at Holyrood (129 MSPs elected by the Additional Member System) holds primary legislative competence over devolved matters — health, education, justice, the environment, transport — under a reserved matters model: everything not reserved to Westminster is devolved. Since the Scotland Acts 2012 and 2016 it sets Scottish income tax rates and bands and controls land and transaction taxes. The 2014 independence referendum (55%–45%) tested the settlement, and the Scotland Act 2016 declared the parliament permanent — though s28(7) of the 1998 Act explicitly preserves Westminster's sovereignty.

Wales: the Senedd (renamed from the National Assembly in 2020) began in 1999 with only secondary, delegated powers; a 2011 referendum and the Wales Acts 2014/2017 converted it into a primary legislature with income tax powers. It expanded from 60 to 96 members from the 2026 election. Northern Ireland: the Assembly is a consociational power-sharing body — key decisions require cross-community consent, reflecting the Good Friday Agreement 1998. It has twice collapsed (2017–2020 and 2022–2024), showing devolution's dependence on local consent.

England: has no parliament of its own. English Votes for English Laws (EVEL) (2015) gave English MPs a veto stage over English-only bills; it was abolished in 2021 as complex and largely ineffective. Instead, England is devolved functionally: a directly elected London Mayor (from 2000) and metro mayors with combined authorities (Greater Manchester, West Midlands) control transport, skills and some budgets. Further county-level deals and a devolution framework continue to extend this model, and the Barnett formula still distributes much devolved funding by population — a further sign the settlement is pragmatic rather than principled.

Impact: devolution has produced policy divergence (free tuition and personal care in Scotland; a differently organised NHS in Wales), built a quasi-federal pattern of asymmetrical power, and raised the West Lothian Question. It has arguably strengthened the union by giving the nations legitimate democratic outlets — but it has also created rival centres of legitimacy and kept constitutional pressure alive (the 2014 referendum, demands for indyref2). Whether devolution strengthens or weakens the union is the classic essay judgement: mark schemes reward both sides plus a supported verdict.

Debates on further constitutional reform (AQA 3.1.1.4; Edexcel P2 1.4)

Codification is the central debate. Arguments for: a codified constitution would clarify the rules (citizens and courts could find them in one authoritative place); strengthen rights protection (rights could not be repealed by ordinary statute); limit executive power (courts could strike down unconstitutional acts, as in the USA); and deepen civic understanding. Arguments against: flexibility allows rapid responses to crises and changing values; parliamentary sovereignty would remain, so Parliament could override judicial rulings anyway; codification would transfer power to unelected judges and invite judicial activism; and the current system has demonstrably adapted — devolution, the HRA and EU exit show an evolving constitution.

Consider also partial codification — the route the UK has effectively taken: a written rights charter (the HRA), a statute-protected devolution settlement, and a codified procedure for treaties (Constitutional Reform and Governance Act 2010) — without a single higher-law document. Essays can argue the UK is drifting towards codification piecemeal, or that these are exceptions proving the flexibility rule.

Other live debates for evaluation:

  • House of Lords reform: should the second chamber be fully elected? Legitimacy versus expertise; the hereditary by-elections and crony peerage criticisms (see the Parliament topic).
  • HRA vs a British Bill of Rights: would a domestic bill of rights protect rights better, or weaken protection and damage the UK's relationship with the ECHR?
  • English devolution: a federal UK with an English parliament, or deeper metro-mayor devolution?
  • Electoral reform: FPTP versus PR (the 2011 AV referendum rejected change) — link to representation and legitimacy.
  • The voting age: lowering it to 16 (already used in Scottish and Welsh devolved elections; proposed for UK-wide elections in 2025) links the constitution to democracy and participation.
  • Parliamentary terms: the FTPA's repeal in 2022 reopened the debate on dissolutions and prime ministerial advantage.

Essay logic: high-mark answers weigh clarity against flexibility, protection against sovereignty, and constitutional stability against democratic renewal — then commit to a judgement with evidence (the Miller cases, the devolution record, the FTPA's rise and fall).

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: AQA Paper 1: 'Analyse the arguments in favour of a codified constitution for the UK.' [25 marks] Edexcel Paper 2: 'Evaluate the view that the UK's constitution should be codified and entrenched.' (30 marks)
Mark-scheme logic: Mark-scheme logic (AQA 25 marks: AO1 12 / AO2 13): AO1 — accurate knowledge of the uncodified, unentrenched nature of the constitution; the amendment process (simple majority, no referendum required); examples of flexibility (devolution, HRA 1998, the FTPA's repeal in 2022). AO2 — developed arguments FOR codification: clarity and accessibility of the rules; stronger entrenchment of rights (contrast the HRA's s3/s4 with judicial enforcement in the USA); limiting executive power (Miller II showed the limits of courts policing conventions); democratic legitimacy. Then counter-arguments: flexibility allows rapid response to events; parliamentary sovereignty means any judicial ruling could be overridden; risk of judicial activism; 'if it ain't broke' — the constitution has adapted well (1997–2022 record). Conclusion: a supported judgement (e.g. codification is desirable in principle but politically unlikely, or flexibility is a strength rather than a weakness). Edexcel (30 marks) additionally requires: alternative viewpoints (codify vs keep uncodified, plus partial options such as a statutory Bill of Rights), contemporary examples since 1997, and a sustained final judgement that weights the evidence rather than listing both sides.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Explain the term 'uncodified constitution'. [5 marks]
Answer: An uncodified constitution is one that is not contained in a single authoritative written document (2 marks for the core definition). Its rules are instead drawn from several sources: statute law (e.g. the Human Rights Act 1998), common law and the royal prerogative, constitutional conventions (e.g. that royal assent is always granted), works of authority and treaties. The UK's is the leading example — unlike the USA's codified constitution, no single document sets out the rules of government, although significant parts are written (2 marks for developed description with examples; 1 mark for accurate use of terminology).
Analyse the impact of devolution on the UK. [25 marks]
Answer: AO1: devolution from 1998 (Scotland Act 1998, Government of Wales Act 1998, Northern Ireland Act 1998 / Good Friday Agreement) transferred legislative and tax powers to elected bodies without transferring sovereignty — a reserved-matters model in Scotland, a consociational assembly in Northern Ireland. Holyrood controls health, education, justice and, since 2017, Scottish income tax rates. AO2 analysis: (1) Policy divergence — Scotland abolished tuition fees and introduced free personal care; Wales runs its own NHS organisation; devolution delivers different democratic choices. (2) Constitutional structure — the UK has become quasi-federal, with asymmetrical, powerful legislatures; the West Lothian Question prompted EVEL (2015, abolished 2021). (3) Legitimacy and sovereignty — devolution created rival centres of authority: the 2014 independence referendum (55%–45%) and indyref2 demands show centrifugal pressure, yet Westminster retains sovereignty (Scotland Act 1998 s28(7)) and the Scotland Act 2016 declared Holyrood permanent. Counter-analysis: devolution arguably strengthened the union by legitimising national identities, and its impact is uneven — England has no parliament, and the NI Assembly has collapsed twice. Conclusion: devolution has profoundly changed the UK's constitutional structure (quasi-federalism) while leaving sovereignty formally intact — its ultimate impact, strengthening or dissolving the union, remains unresolved.
Evaluate the view that devolution has been the most significant constitutional reform since 1997. (30 marks)
Answer: In support: devolution is the most transformative reform — it redistributed power across the whole state, created three legislatures with primary law-making and (in Scotland) tax powers, and changed the UK from unitary to quasi-federal. Its effects are visible daily (policy divergence on tuition fees and care) and it reshaped UK politics (EVEL, the 2014 referendum, the rise of the SNP). Against: (1) The Human Rights Act 1998 could be judged more significant — it imported justiciable rights, transformed the judiciary's role (s3 interpretive duty; s4 declarations of incompatibility) and changed how every public body operates. (2) The Constitutional Reform Act 2005 created a UK Supreme Court — a structural change to the separation of powers that fed directly into Miller I (2017) and Miller II (2019). (3) The EU Withdrawal Acts (2018/2020) ended the supremacy of EU law — arguably the largest sovereignty shift of all. Counter-evaluation: the HRA depends on parliamentary goodwill (Parliament can repeal it; the 2023 Bill of Rights plan came close), whereas devolution created legislatures that cannot lawfully be abolished without consent (Scotland Act 2016 s1) — a deeper protection. Nor has any reform so visibly changed citizens' experience of government as devolution. Judgement: devolution remains the most significant reform because it altered the territorial distribution of power and created new centres of democratic legitimacy — but a strong answer recognises the rival claims of the HRA and EU exit.
Evaluate the view that the Human Rights Act 1998 has strengthened the protection of rights in the UK. (30 marks)
Answer: In support: the HRA made ECHR rights justiciable in UK courts for the first time — citizens no longer need to go to Strasbourg; public authorities must act compatibly (s6); courts interpret legislation compatibly where possible (s3) and can issue declarations of incompatibility (s4), which have prompted change (e.g. Bellinger v Bellinger 2004 on marriage rights). The Act empowered judges to check executive excess — most notably A and others v SSHD (2004, the Belmarsh case), where indefinite detention of foreign nationals without trial was declared incompatible with Article 5, forcing a change in the law. Against: (1) The HRA does not entrench rights — Parliament may legislate incompatibly and could repeal the Act (the proposed British Bill of Rights proved the point); sovereignty ultimately limits protection. (2) Rights were protected before 1998 through common law, Parliament and the ECHR's direct route — the HRA added a mechanism, not rights themselves. (3) Critics argue the HRA invites judicial activism, letting unelected judges frustrate elected governments (a charge levelled after Miller II). Counter-evaluation: the Belmarsh ruling forced legislative change and Parliament complied — evidence the HRA does bite; the s3 interpretive power has quietly reshaped many statutes; the ECHR fallback remains. Judgement: the HRA has strengthened rights protection procedurally and judicially, but because it is not entrenched the improvement is institutional rather than guaranteed — landing on this distinction is what earns the top levels.
Analyse how the constitution has changed since 2010. [25 marks]
Answer: AO1: the coalition (2010–15) passed the Fixed-term Parliaments Act 2011 (five-year terms; a two-thirds dissolution vote), the EU Act 2011 (referendum lock) and devolved further tax powers (Scotland Act 2012; Wales Act 2014). Conservatives from 2015 added the Recall of MPs Act 2015, the Scotland Act 2016 (income tax rates; Holyrood's permanence), the EU Withdrawal Acts 2018/2020 (ending EU law supremacy) and the Dissolution and Calling of Parliament Act 2022 (repealing the FTPA). AO2: (1) Flexibility cuts both ways — the FTPA's codification of dissolution was reversed within a decade, showing rules can be remade by simple majority. (2) EU exit produced the largest sovereignty shift since 1997, returning powers to Westminster and the devolved bodies and reopening 'retained EU law' questions. (3) Judicial–executive friction intensified: Miller I (2017) and Miller II (2019) show the courts policing the prerogative and Parliament's ability to sit — evidence the uncodified constitution still constrains the executive. Counter-analysis: some change was consolidation rather than transformation (devolution deepened existing settlements), and the abandonment of the British Bill of Rights in 2023 shows reform fatigue. Conclusion: since 2010 the constitution has changed most through Brexit-related statutes and the courts' enlarged role, while the underlying architecture — an uncodified constitution with a sovereign Parliament — remains.
