Political Parties

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA, Edexcel

A* revision on political parties for AQA 7152 and Edexcel 9PL0: party functions and systems, funding and organisation, Conservative and Labour ideological development, the Liberal Democrats, minor parties and left-right convergence — with full 9-, 25- and 30-mark model answers.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Outline the functions performed by political parties — representation, participation, policy formulation, leadership recruitment, mobilisation and government formation — and classify the UK party system (AQA 3.1.2.3; Edexcel P1 Topic 2)
  • Explain how the main parties are organised and funded, and evaluate the case for and against state funding of political parties (AQA 3.1.2.3; Edexcel P1 Topic 2)
  • Trace the ideological and policy development of the Conservative Party from one-nation conservatism through the New Right to modernisation and beyond (Edexcel P1 Topic 2)
  • Trace the ideological and policy development of the Labour Party from democratic socialism through New Labour's Third Way to Corbynism and Starmerism (Edexcel P1 Topic 2)
  • Explain the origins, policies and influence of the Liberal Democrats and of minor parties, including the SNP, Greens, UKIP/Brexit Party and Reform UK (AO1/AO2)
  • Evaluate the view that the main parties have converged on the centre ground, and assess the effects of convergence and divergence on choice, mandates and the party system (AO2/AO3)
  • Construct balanced judgements on the emerging UK party system and on the factors that affect party success (AO3)

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Party system: The pattern of party competition in a state — classified as one-party dominant, two-party, two-and-a-half-party or multiparty — judged by vote and seat shares and by how governments are formed.
Two-party system: A system in which two parties dominate votes and seats and alternately form single-party governments — the Westminster pattern from 1945 to the 1970s (Labour and Conservative won 97% of votes in 1951).
Dominant-party system: A system in which one party wins election after election over a long period while others still compete — the Conservatives 1979–97 (four consecutive majorities); the SNP in Scotland 2007–24.
Duverger's law: The observation that first-past-the-post mechanically wastes votes cast for third parties and psychologically discourages split voting, tending to produce two-party systems — weakened in the UK by regional parties and tactical voting.
Catch-all party: A party that broadens its appeal beyond its traditional base to capture the median voter, producing ideological moderation and left-right convergence between the main parties.
Left-right convergence: The narrowing of policy and ideological distance between the main parties — 'Butskellism' after 1945, and New Labour's acceptance of the market economy and Tory spending plans after 1994.
One-nation conservatism: Disraeli's paternalistic tradition: the privileged have a duty of care to the poorer classes and national unity matters more than class conflict — invoked by modernisers and Cameron's Big Society.
New Right: The Thatcherite synthesis of economic liberalism (privatisation, deregulation, low tax, weak unions) and social authoritarianism (tough law and order, strong defence, traditional values).
Clause IV: Labour's 1918 constitutional commitment to common ownership of the means of production; rewritten by Blair in 1995 to champion enterprise and the market alongside social justice.
Third Way: New Labour's settlement between old state socialism and the New Right: a market economy combined with active government investment in education, health and welfare-to-work.
PPERA 2000: The Political Parties, Elections and Referendums Act 2000: created the Electoral Commission, required disclosure of donations, banned foreign and most anonymous donations, and set campaign spending limits.
Short money: State funding (from 1975) paid to opposition parties in the Commons in proportion to seats and votes won, to support parliamentary work — Cranborne money (1996) extends this to the Lords.

📌 Key Points

Functions of parties: representation, participation, policy formulation, leadership recruitment, electoral mobilisation, government formation and opposition.
UK party systems: two-party (1945–70s), Conservative dominance (1979–97), two-and-a-half party (1997–2010), multiparty era since 2010 (coalition, SNP surge, DUP confidence-and-supply, Reform 2024).
Funding: membership, donations, union affiliation fees (Labour), Short money and Cranborne money; PPERA 2000 governs disclosure and spending; scandals fuel the state-funding debate.
Conservative development: Tamworth Manifesto (1834) → one-nation → Thatcherism/New Right → Cameron modernisation → Johnson's levelling up → Truss → 2024 defeat.
Labour development: Clause IV (1918) → Attlee's welfare state → SDP split (1981) → New Labour Third Way → Corbynism → Starmer's fiscal rules.
Convergence narrows voter choice and weakens exclusive mandates; divergence returned over Brexit and in 2024 (tax, immigration, net zero).

Party functions and the UK party system (AQA 3.1.2.3; Edexcel P1 Topic 2)

Parties perform six linked functions. They represent social and regional interests in Parliament; they enable participation through membership, campaigning and voting; they engage in policy formulation, offering voters a coherent programme rather than a list of unrelated issues; they recruit and train leaders — every prime minister and most ministers come through party ranks; they mobilise the electorate by organising campaigns and getting out the vote; and they form governments and an official Opposition, sustaining the scrutiny and accountability that make ministers answerable between elections.

Party systems classify the pattern of competition:

  • Two-party system (1945–70): Labour and Conservative together won over 90% of the vote (97% in 1951); governments alternated with single-party majorities, giving strong but exclusive mandates.
  • Dominant-party pattern: the Conservatives won four consecutive elections 1979–97; the SNP governed Scotland from 2007 to 2024.
  • Two-and-a-half-party system (1997–2010): the Liberal Democrats polled 18–23% — a third force in votes but not a government-maker until the 2010 coalition.
  • Multiparty system (since 2010): coalition (2010–15), the SNP's 56 seats (2015), DUP confidence-and-supply (2017–19), and the fragmentation of 2024 — Labour 411 seats on just 33.7% of the vote, Reform UK on 14.3%, with the Greens and Liberal Democrats advancing.

Duverger's law explains why first-past-the-post pulls Westminster back towards two parties: votes for third parties are 'wasted' unless geographically concentrated, and voters tactically desert them. Yet devolution, European elections (pre-2020) and strong regional parties fragmented the system. The 2024 result — a Labour landslide won on a smaller vote share than Corbyn's 2017, with Reform UK second in many seats — feeds the debate that the UK now shows an emerging multiparty system in voting behaviour while remaining two-party in Westminster government formation.

Funding, organisation and policy development (AQA 3.1.2.3; Edexcel P1 Topic 2)

Sources of funding. Membership subscriptions; individual donations; corporate and union money. Labour still depends heavily on trade union affiliation fees (Unite, GMB, USDAW) — historically the source of its conference majorities; the Conservatives rely on wealthy individual donors and business. State support supplements both: Short money (1975) funds opposition parties in the Commons in proportion to seats and votes won, and Cranborne money (1996) does the same in the Lords.

PPERA 2000 created the Electoral Commission, requires disclosure of reportable donations, banned foreign and most anonymous donations and capped national campaign spending. Large donations remain legal, however: scandals — cash-for-questions (1994), cash-for-honours (2006, loans for peerages) and repeated claims that major donors buy access to party leaders — sustain the argument that private funding corrupts politics and distorts policy influence.

The state funding debate. Hayden Phillips's cross-party review (2006–08) proposed capping donations and increasing state funding but collapsed when Labour would not cap union money and the Conservatives would not cap individual donations. The case for state funding: cleaner politics, independence from big money, a level playing field against incumbent advantage. The case against: taxpayers would subsidise parties they oppose; parties should raise their own support; state funding entrenches existing parties and could freeze out new movements.

Organisation. Central headquarters (CCHQ, Labour's Southside, Lib Dem HQ) runs strategy, communications and national campaigns; local associations (Conservative) and Constituency Labour Parties select candidates and fight local campaigns; regional structures bridge the two. Leadership elections shape party direction: Conservative rules give MPs the nomination shortlist and members the final vote (2022: Sunak won among MPs only after Truss resigned; 2024: Badenoch), while Labour's electoral college of MPs, members and affiliated unions delivered Corbyn's 2015 and 2016 wins against the will of most of his MPs — a standing illustration of how organisation affects outcomes.

Membership and policy development. Membership has collapsed from 1950s peaks (Labour over 1 million in 1953; Conservatives around 2.8 million) to roughly 370,000 for Labour and around 130,000 for the Conservatives by 2024 — pushing parties towards professionalised, donor- and media-driven campaigns. Policy is developed at annual conferences, through Labour's National Policy Forum, the Conservative Research Department and allied think tanks (IPPR for Labour; Policy Exchange and Demos for the Conservatives), then distilled into manifestos drafted under tight leadership control — 'Get Brexit Done' (2019) and 'Change' (2024) show leaders prioritising a few memorable pledges over detailed programmes.

The Conservative Party: one-nation, the New Right and modernisation

Origins and one-nation tradition. The Tory party rebranded under Peel, whose Tamworth Manifesto (1834) accepted the 1832 Reform Act — conservatism adapts in order to conserve. Disraeli's one-nation conservatism preached paternalistic obligation to the poorer classes and national unity above class conflict (Public Health Act 1875). After 1945 the party accepted the welfare state and mixed economy — the bipartisan 'post-war consensus'.

Thatcherism (1979–90) was a deliberate departure. The New Right fused economic liberalism with social authoritarianism: privatisation (British Telecom 1984, British Gas 1986), deregulation and the City 'big bang' (1986), tax cuts, sale of council houses (Housing Act 1980) and union control (miners' strike 1984–85), alongside tough law and order, strong defence and traditional values. Thatcher repudiated the consensus but defended the NHS and kept welfare fundamentals — one-nation elements inside an otherwise radical project.

Major to Cameron. Major's government was split by Europe (Maastricht ratification, ERM exit 1992), and modernisation under Cameron (2005–16) detoxified the brand: A-list candidate selection, environmental rebranding, the Big Society and same-sex marriage (2013) — one-nation liberalism reasserted, though the coalition (2010–15) forced compromise (the AV referendum was lost in 2011).

May, Johnson, Truss, Sunak. May promised grammar schools and 'burning injustices' but was consumed by Brexit. Johnson won 2019 on 'Get Brexit Done' and 'levelling up', taking Red Wall seats with a more state-interventionist, one-nation posture. Truss's mini-budget (2022) revived pure New Right tax-cutting and collapsed within weeks; Sunak restored fiscal discipline. The 2024 defeat (121 seats), with Reform UK splitting the right, reopens the classic debate: does the party's future lie in one-nation moderation or in harder New Right positioning?

The Labour Party: from democratic socialism to the Third Way and beyond

Origins and old Labour. Founded as the Labour Representation Committee (1900) to give the unions a parliamentary voice, becoming the Labour Party (1906); shaped by the Fabian Society and trade unions, its 1918 constitution inserted Clause IV, committing it to common ownership of the means of production. Attlee's 1945 government built the NHS (1948), the welfare state and nationalised coal, rail and steel — democratic socialism in practice. Wilson and Callaghan modernised selectively but were caught by stagflation and the 1976 IMF loan.

Crisis and modernisation. The SDP split (1981) — the Gang of Four (Williams, Rodgers, Jenkins and Owen) — handed the Liberals the Alliance. Kinnock modernised (expelling Militant, softening unilateralism) but lost 1987 and 1992; Smith's One Member One Vote (1993) weakened union block voting.

New Labour (1994–2007). Blair rewrote Clause IV (1995) and articulated the Third Way: acceptance of the market economy and inherited Conservative spending plans combined with active government — Bank of England independence (1997), the national minimum wage (1999), devolution, welfare-to-work and large NHS and schools investment after 2000, financed partly through PFI and accompanied by tuition fees and the Iraq War (2003), which split the party and damaged trust. Brown's premiership was overwhelmed by the financial crisis (2008–09).

Corbynism and Starmerism. Corbyn (2015–19) returned Labour to democratic socialism — renationalising rail, energy and water, free tuition, higher corporate tax — surging to 40% of the vote in 2017 but collapsing in 2019. Starmer (2020–) reasserted discipline (suspensions of rebels), kept fiscal rules, dropped the £28bn-a-year Green Prosperity Plan and won 2024's 'Change' landslide (411 seats on 33.7%) — a cautious, catch-all programme critics call Blairism without the growth. Policy development runs through the National Policy Forum, conference and joint policy consultations, tightly managed from the leadership.

The Liberal Democrats, minor parties and the convergence debate

Liberal Democrats. The Liberals' new liberalism (Asquith, Lloyd George) produced the People's Budget (1909) and National Insurance (1911); inter-war decline followed Labour's rise as the progressive party. The SDP–Liberal Alliance (1981–87) polled 25.4% in 1983; the Liberal Democrats merged in 1988. Ashdown stabilised the party; Kennedy opposed the Iraq War (2003), winning 62 seats in 2005; Clegg's coalition (2010–15) delivered the AV referendum (lost 2011) and the tuition-fee U-turn (£9,000) that wrecked its student support. The Orange Book (2004) — Clegg, Cable, Huhne — pulled the party towards classical-liberal, market-orientated policy against its social-liberal majority. Under Davey it took 72 seats in 2024 (its best since 1923) by targeting Remain-voting, rural and incumbent-vulnerable constituencies.

Minor parties. The SNP has governed Scotland since 2007 and took 56 of 59 Scottish seats in 2015, but fell to 9 seats in 2024 amid independence fatigue. Plaid Cymru holds Welsh nationalism's ground; the Greens won Brighton Pavilion (2010, Caroline Lucas) and four seats in 2024; UKIP topped the 2014 European elections and polled 3.9 million votes for a single seat in 2015, forcing Cameron's EU referendum; the Brexit Party won the 2019 European elections; Reform UK took 14.3% of the vote and 5 seats in 2024 and now pressures the Conservatives from the right. The DUP sustained May's government by confidence-and-supply (2017–19); Sinn Féin abstains from Westminster. Minor parties set agendas (Brexit), act as kingmakers in hung parliaments, govern in devolved bodies and expose FPTP's disproportionality — yet their Westminster impact still depends on geographic concentration of votes.

Left-right convergence. Convergence narrows the distance between the main parties: after 1945 ('Butskellism'), and again from 1997 to 2010, when Labour accepted the market economy, privatisation and inherited spending plans while the Conservatives accepted NHS and welfare spending. Explanations: catch-all strategy (chasing the median voter), the decline of class voting and globalisation. Effects: weaker exclusive mandates, falling turnout, partisan dealignment and space for minor parties to differentiate. Divergence returns at moments of crisis: Brexit (2016–19) split both main parties, and 2024 differentiated them on tax (Labour's employer national insurance rise against Reform's tax cuts), immigration and net zero. A* judgement: convergence is real but partial — economic management has converged while cultural and constitutional policy has not, and the emerging multiparty system grows on exactly the issues where the main parties still disagree.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: Evaluate the view that the UK is moving towards a multiparty system (AQA 7152, 25 marks) | Evaluate the extent to which the policies of the Conservative and Labour parties have converged since 1997 (Edexcel 9PL0, 30 marks)
Mark-scheme logic: Mark-scheme logic for a top-band answer. AO1: accurate account of party functions and system classification (two-party 1945–70s, Conservative dominance 1979–97, multiparty since 2010), funding rules (PPERA 2000, Short money) and party development with named leaders and policies (Thatcher's privatisation, Blair's Clause IV revision, Starmer's fiscal rules). AO2: analysis of convergence — Butskellism, New Labour's acceptance of spending plans and PFI, Cameron's NHS pledge and Big Society, Starmer's fiscal rules — against divergence — Brexit, the 2019 and 2024 manifestos, Labour's employer NI rise versus Reform's tax cuts; analysis of multiparty evidence (2010 coalition, SNP 56 seats, DUP confidence-and-supply, Reform and Green gains) against FPTP's two-party pull (Duverger's law; Labour and Conservative still forming governments). AO3: justified judgement with criteria — voter choice, mandates, representation and accountability — e.g. voting has fragmented but government formation remains two-party, so the UK is best seen as a two-party system with an emerging multiparty electorate; convergence has narrowed economic policy but cultural and constitutional issues now carry the differentiation, sustaining minor-party growth.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Explain and analyse three functions performed by political parties in UK politics. [AQA 7152, 9 marks]
Answer: One: policy formulation — parties distil demands into coherent manifestos, giving voters a programme to accept or reject; without parties, elections would offer a list of individual candidates' personal views, weakening mandate clarity (e.g. the 2019 'Get Brexit Done' manifesto). Two: leadership recruitment — all prime ministers since 1902 have been party leaders, and parties train ministers through shadow and frontbench roles and local government; this ensures governments have experienced personnel. Three: government formation and accountability — parties organise government and sustain the official Opposition, which scrutinises ministers between elections (Leader's Questions, select committee minorities); without organised opposition, executive accountability would depend on ad hoc coalitions of backbenchers. Also credit: representation of interests, enabling participation, mobilising turnout.
Explain and analyse three ways in which party funding creates problems for UK democracy. [Edexcel 9PL0 Paper 1, 12 marks]
Answer: One: reliance on large donations raises suspicion of buying influence — major donors gain access to leaders and honours nominations (cash-for-honours inquiry, 2006), so policy may bend to funders rather than voters. Two: asymmetric dependence distorts competition — Labour's union affiliation fees (Unite, GMB) and the Conservatives' wealthy individual donors each expose their party to capture, and in the 2024 campaign the Conservatives drew heavily on donor funding while Reform UK relied on a single large donor. Three: the current framework is partial and contested — PPERA 2000 requires disclosure and caps spending, but large personal donations remain legal, and the failure of the Hayden Phillips review (2006–08) means no consensus on state funding; opponents argue state funding would tax voters to subsidise parties they reject, so the problem of trust has no settled solution.
Evaluate the extent to which the Labour Party remains a socialist party. [Edexcel 9PL0, 30 marks]
Answer: Arguments that it remains socialist: origins and doctrine — founded by the unions and Fabians, Clause IV (1918) committed it to common ownership; Attlee nationalised coal, rail and steel and built the NHS; Corbyn (2015–19) pledged renationalisation of rail, energy and water, free tuition and higher corporate tax, and won 40% in 2017. Arguments that it is not: New Labour rewrote Clause IV (1995), accepted the market economy, privatisation (PFI, air traffic control) and inherited spending plans; the Third Way prioritised welfare-to-work over ownership; Blair's governments relied on private finance and introduced tuition fees. Under Starmer the party kept fiscal rules, dropped the £28bn Green Prosperity Plan, courted business support and renationalises little beyond rail operators as franchises expire — catch-all caution, not socialism. Judgement: Labour's socialism is now contingent — democratic socialist ambitions return in opposition (Corbyn) but governable programmes since 1994 accept the market; the party is best described as a social-democratic, catch-all party whose socialist wing resurfaces when electorally safe, so to a limited and declining extent it remains socialist in instinct but not in policy.
Evaluate the view that minor parties have little influence on UK politics. [Edexcel 9PL0, 30 marks]
Answer: Arguments for the view: FPTP wastes minor-party votes — UKIP won 3.9 million votes for one seat in 2015 and Reform UK 14.3% for 5 seats in 2024; Westminster government formation has remained two-party since 1945 apart from the 2010 coalition; minor parties rarely hold power and national agendas are still set by Labour and the Conservatives. Arguments against: minor parties set national agendas — UKIP forced the EU referendum (2016) and Brexit dominated 2016–19; the SNP's 56 seats (2015) made Scotland's constitutional status a UK question and sustained coalition-era leverage; the DUP's confidence-and-supply agreement (2017–19) sustained May's government and shaped Brexit negotiations; the Greens shaped the climate agenda (net zero by 2050, 2019) and the Liberal Democrats governed 2010–15 and took 72 seats in 2024. Judgement: Westminster seats understate influence — minor parties influence when they are geographically concentrated (SNP, DUP) or agenda-setting (UKIP/Reform on Europe and immigration); 'little influence' is only true for parties with diffuse support at general elections, and multiparty devolved bodies plus the 2024 fragmentation suggest their influence is growing, not shrinking.
Evaluate the extent to which the Conservative Party remains committed to one-nation conservatism. [AQA 7152, 25 marks]
Answer: Arguments for continued one-nation commitment: the tradition recurs at moments of repair — Peel's Tamworth Manifesto (1834) and Disraeli's social reform (Public Health Act 1875); Cameron's modernisation detoxified the brand with the Big Society, environmental targets and same-sex marriage (2013); Johnson's levelling up pledged state investment in left-behind towns, and Sunak's rhetoric of integrity and fiscal responsibility echoes paternalistic duty. Arguments against: Thatcherism (1979–90) is the governing ideology — privatisation, deregulation, tax cuts, union control and council-house sales repudiated paternalism; Truss's mini-budget (2022) revived pure New Right tax-cutting and was abandoned only after market collapse; austerity after 2010 cut welfare and local government spending, and the 2024 campaign was fought more against Reform UK's immigration stance than for one-nation social policy. Judgement: one-nation rhetoric wins elections while New Right policy tends to govern — the party is committed to one-nation conservatism as a campaign position but, in office, market liberalism and social authoritarianism dominate; its commitment is therefore rhetorical rather than substantive, and Reform UK's rise will decide which wing holds the party next.
