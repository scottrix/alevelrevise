The Prime Minister and Cabinet

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA, Edexcel

A* revision on the Prime Minister and Cabinet for AQA 7152 and Edexcel 9PL0: prime ministerial powers, collective and individual responsibility, premierships from Thatcher to Starmer, and full 9-, 25- and 30-mark model answers.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Describe the roles and powers of the Prime Minister and of the Cabinet, using key concepts — core executive, primus inter pares, inner cabinet, cabinet committees (AQA 3.1.1.3; Edexcel P2 Topic 3)
  • Explain the difference between individual and collective ministerial responsibility, supported by named examples such as Crichel Down (1954) and Iain Duncan Smith (2016) (AO1/AO2)
  • Analyse the factors that shape the relationship between the Prime Minister and the Cabinet: majority size, party unity, cabinet management style and events (AO2)
  • Deploy case studies of prime ministerial power — one from 1945–1997 (e.g. the poll tax, 1990) and one from 1997 to the present (e.g. the invasion of Iraq, 2003) — as the AQA specification requires (AO1)
  • Evaluate the 'presidentialism' debate (Foley) using evidence for and against prime ministerial dominance (AO3)
  • Link prime ministerial power to the other branches — Parliament, the judiciary (Miller II) and devolution — for synoptic credit (AO2/AO3)

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Core executive: The network of bodies at the apex of government — the Prime Minister, Cabinet, Cabinet committees and the Treasury — where power over policy and resources is contested and negotiated (Hennessy).
Primus inter pares: 'First among equals': the constitutional position of the Prime Minister as the most senior but nominally equal member of the Cabinet.
Inner cabinet: The small group of the most senior ministers (e.g. Prime Minister, Chancellor, Foreign Secretary, Home Secretary) who take the biggest decisions, often outside full Cabinet.
Cabinet committee: A sub-group of ministers, chaired usually by a senior minister or the Prime Minister, that settles policy in a defined area before it reaches full Cabinet — a key mechanism of delegation.
Patronage: The Prime Minister's power to appoint and dismiss ministers and to distribute other offices; it compels loyalty because careers depend on the Prime Minister's favour.
Royal prerogative: Residual powers now exercised in practice by ministers without parliamentary approval, including deploying the armed forces, treaty-making, granting honours and, since the Dissolution and Calling of Parliament Act 2022, calling general elections.
Collective responsibility: The convention that Cabinet decisions are binding on all ministers, who must defend them publicly and resign or be dismissed if they cannot support them; includes confidentiality of Cabinet proceedings.
Individual responsibility: The convention that each minister is answerable to Parliament for their personal conduct and the errors and policy failures of their department, traditionally resigning for serious failures.
Ministerial Code: The rulebook for ministerial conduct, built on the seven Nolan principles; breaches are investigated by the independent adviser on ministers' interests — revised in 2022 under Johnson, softening the expectation that ministers resign for breaches.
Presidentialism: Michael Foley's thesis (2000) that UK prime ministers increasingly resemble presidents: spatial leadership, a personalised mandate and direct media appeals to the people over the party.
Sofa government: The Blair-era style of decision-making through informal chats with advisers and favoured ministers in the Prime Minister's study rather than through the formal Cabinet system (identified by the Butler Review, 2004).
Vote of no confidence: A Commons motion that, if lost by the government, triggers resignation or a general election; the Fixed-term Parliaments Act 2011 codified this and its 2022 repeal restored the convention.

📌 Key Points

AQA 3.1.1.3 requires two examples of the PM and Cabinet dictating events: one from 1945–1997 (e.g. poll tax, 1990) and one from 1997–present (e.g. Iraq, 2003).
Collective responsibility has agreed exceptions — the 1975 EEC referendum and the 2016 Brexit referendum allowed Cabinet free votes.
Power depends on circumstance: an 80-seat majority (Johnson 2019) empowers; a hung parliament (May 2017) weakens.
Miller II (2019) struck down prorogation — judicial constraint on the prerogative is now essential evidence in essays.
The 2022 mass ministerial resignations that ended Johnson's premiership show party, not Parliament, is the PM's real check.

The roles and powers of the Prime Minister

The Prime Minister combines several roles: head of the executive and chair of Cabinet, leader of the largest party (giving a personal mandate after an election win), chief foreign-policy spokesman on the world stage (G7, NATO and summit diplomacy), the public face of government through weekly PMQs, and patron-in-chief. Patronage is the foundation of power: the PM appoints and dismisses around 100 paid ministerial posts — the Cabinet, junior ministers and whips — so careers depend on the PM's favour. Thatcher promoted loyalists in 1983; Sunak's 2022 and 2024 reshuffles rewarded unity. Patronage can also fail: ministers with independent power bases (a faction, a media profile, a big department) are hard to move.

Ministers also exercise royal prerogative powers in the PM's name: deploying the armed forces (Blair committed troops to Iraq in 2003 without a binding vote before the conflict), negotiating treaties (the Brexit Withdrawal Agreement, 2019–20), granting honours (resignation honours lists) and — since the Dissolution and Calling of Parliament Act 2022 repealed the Fixed-term Parliaments Act 2011 — choosing the date of general elections. Prerogative power is not unlimited: Miller II (2019) made a five-week prorogation unlawful, and statute can override prerogative (Miller I, 2017 — Article 50 required an Act of Parliament).

Conceptually, the PM sits within the core executive (Hennessy): a network — PM, Cabinet, Cabinet committees, the Treasury and senior advisers — in which resources and policy are negotiated, not commanded. The inner cabinet (PM, Chancellor, Foreign and Home Secretaries) takes the biggest decisions, and cabinet committees settle most business before full Cabinet. This supports the view that the PM is primus inter pares — first among equals — rather than a president. Yet prime ministerial resources have grown: the Cabinet Office and No.10 policy units, the pool of special advisers, and control of the government's media narrative all give the modern PM levers Attlee lacked.

Collective and individual ministerial responsibility

Collective responsibility has three strands: unanimity (Cabinet decisions bind every minister, who must defend them publicly), confidentiality (internal argument stays private, protected even from select committees) and confidence (the government resigns or seeks a dissolution if it loses a confidence vote). Enforcement is by resignation or sacking: Robin Cook (2003) and Clare Short (2003) resigned over Iraq; Iain Duncan Smith (2016) resigned over welfare reform cuts — both AQA-cited examples. The convention bends when agreed: Wilson allowed a free campaign in the 1975 EEC referendum, and Cameron suspended collective responsibility for the 2016 Brexit referendum — letting Johnson, Gove and Leadsom campaign Leave from the Cabinet table. It can also be weakened in effect: Johnson's 2022 revision of the Ministerial Code relaxed the expectation that ministers resign for breaches, before party revolt forced him out anyway.

Individual responsibility makes each minister answerable to Parliament — through oral and written questions, statements and departmental select committees — and responsible for departmental failure and personal conduct. The classic precedent is Crichel Down (1954): Sir Thomas Dugdale resigned even though officials, not the minister, were at fault. Modern examples split into departmental failure (Lord Carrington after the Falklands invasion, 1982; Estelle Morris, 2002; Amber Rudd over the Windrush scandal, 2018) and personal misconduct (Priti Patel's 2020 bullying findings, which Johnson defended; Gavin Williamson's 2021 sacking over leaks; Suella Braverman's two resignations, 2022 and 2023, over security breaches and inflammatory comments). Whether ministers must resign is contested — the Ministerial Code has no automatic sanction — so strong Edexcel answers argue accountability now depends more on the PM's political judgement than on convention.

Factors that affect prime ministerial power

The relationship between the Prime Minister and Cabinet is not fixed; it varies with four main factors. 1. Size of the majority. Landslide majorities make the Commons near-irrelevant and the PM unchallengeable in Cabinet — Thatcher after 1983 (144) and Blair after 1997 (179) could govern by decree. Minority or small majorities shift power to Parliament and to recalcitrant ministers: May's 2017 loss of her majority made the DUP and Cabinet Brexit factions decisive — Davis and Johnson both resigned over the Chequers plan. 2. Party unity. A united party lets the PM dominate; a divided one paralyses them. Europe destroyed Thatcher (Howe's 1990 resignation speech triggered Heseltine's challenge) and May (repeated Commons defeats); the 2022 resignations — Sunak and Javid first, then 50+ ministers — destroyed Johnson. 3. Cabinet management style. Thatcher was domineering but initially let ministers run their departments; Blair bypassed Cabinet through sofa government with advisers and a few trusted colleagues (Butler Review, 2004); May was inclusive but indecisive; Truss excluded the Treasury and cabinet committees from the mini-budget — with catastrophic results.

4. Events and circumstances. War and crisis centralise power in No.10: the Falklands (1982) and Ukraine (2022) boosted incumbent PMs; COVID-19 gave Johnson emergency control of the timetable — but also exposed weak cabinet management (Cummings's account of chaotic decision-making). Economic crises constrain: Truss's mini-budget (September 2022) was reversed by markets, the Bank of England and her own MPs within weeks — the starkest modern proof that 'events, dear boy' (Macmillan) govern premierships. 5. Media and public standing. Blair's media mastery and Johnson's press celebrity sustained them through crises that would have felled others; Sunak's poor poll ratings weakened his authority from 2023.

The presidentialism debate (Foley, 2000) synthesises these factors: spatial leadership (the PM as a brand apart — 'Blair's Britain'), a personalised mandate (leader-centred campaigns, televised debates) and plebiscitary devices (referendums, direct appeals over the party's head). The counter-case: Brown, May, Truss and Sunak all reached No.10 without winning a general election as leader; collective Cabinet proved indispensable in COVID COBRA meetings; and Parliament humiliated May and Johnson alike. The A* judgement: prime ministerial power is contingent — patronage, media and majority explain dominance; party division, events and, since Miller II, the courts explain subordination.

Case studies: premierships in action

The AQA specification requires one example of the PM and Cabinet dictating events from 1945–1997 and one from 1997 to the present. The safest pair: the poll tax (1990) and the invasion of Iraq (2003).

  • Poll tax (Thatcher). Thatcher imposed the community charge against Cabinet and Treasury warnings, using her 1987 majority and patronage to overrule dissent. Riots (Trafalgar Square, 1990), mass non-payment and Conservative MPs' panic forced Heseltine's challenge; Howe's resignation speech sealed her fall. It shows a PM dictating policy — and the limits when policy fails.
  • Iraq (Blair). Blair committed Britain to the 2003 invasion through sofa government and close alignment with George W. Bush, with Cabinet discussion minimal and Parliament voting after the decision was effectively taken. The Butler Review (2004) criticised the use of intelligence. 139 Labour MPs rebelled — the largest revolt on a major issue since 1945 — and the fallout defined his legacy.

Essay strategy: presidential or cabinet government?

Most questions on this topic are 'evaluate the view' essays (AQA 25 marks; Edexcel 30 marks) — typically presidentialism, PM-Cabinet power or ministerial accountability. Structure for the top band:

  1. Thesis in the introduction: state a qualified judgement up front, e.g. 'Prime ministers are presidents in presentation only — power depends on majority, party unity and events, as Thatcher's fall and Truss's 49 days show.'
  2. Three analytical paragraphs for the view (patronage; media and spatial leadership; majority control of Parliament), each with a named example.
  3. Three against: core executive negotiation (Hennessy); collective Cabinet in crises (COVID COBRA); constraints from party revolt (2022 resignations), Parliament (May's 432–202 defeat) and the courts (Miller II).
  4. A judgement that answers the question: not 'it depends' but what it depends on — the variables above.

Synoptic links for the top band: Parliament (select committees and the Liaison Committee questioning the PM); the judiciary (Miller II on prorogation; Miller I on the prerogative-statute boundary); devolution (COVID showed No.10 cannot dictate to Holyrood and Cardiff — four-nations divergence); and the location of sovereignty (prerogative now bounded by both statute and judicial review). Common traps: narrating premierships without analysis; using only pre-2016 examples (Truss, Sunak and the 2022 resignations are fresh, examiner-friendly evidence); ignoring the counter-case that weak-mandate PMs (Brown, May, Truss, Sunak) disprove the presidential thesis as decisively as Thatcher and Blair support it.

Exam-style: Evaluate the view that the Prime Minister and Cabinet have the power to dictate events and determine policy making (AQA 7152, 25 marks) | Evaluate the extent to which the prime minister is able to dominate the cabinet (Edexcel 9PL0 Paper 2, 30 marks)
Mark-scheme logic: Mark-scheme logic for a top-band answer. AO1 (knowledge, ~40%): accurate account of PM powers — patronage, prerogative, media leadership, chairing Cabinet — and the core executive concept; two case studies matching the AQA period requirement (poll tax 1990 pre-1997; Iraq 2003 post-1997). AO2 (analysis, ~30%): factor analysis — majority size (Thatcher 1983/Blair 1997 vs May 2017), party unity (Howe 1990; the 2022 resignations), cabinet management (sofa government vs Truss's exclusion of the Treasury), events (Falklands, COVID, the 2022 markets). AO3 (evaluation, ~30%): a sustained judgement — power is contingent, not presidential: dictation succeeds with landslide majorities and unity (Thatcher, Blair, Johnson 2019) and fails against party (Truss), Parliament (May's 432–202 defeat) and courts (Miller II). Strong answers end by weighing Foley's presidentialism against Hennessy's core executive and concluding on operational variables rather than a flat 'it depends'.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Analyse the difference between individual and collective responsibility (AQA, 9 marks)
Answer: Collective responsibility binds all ministers to defend Cabinet decisions publicly and keep internal disagreement confidential; individual responsibility makes each minister answerable to Parliament for their own conduct and their department's failures. They operate differently: collective responsibility is enforced within the executive (resignation or dismissal — Robin Cook over Iraq, 2003; Iain Duncan Smith over welfare reform, 2016), while individual responsibility is enforced by Parliament (questions, select committees) and has historically required resignation for departmental failure — Sir Thomas Dugdale over Crichel Down (1954), Lord Carrington after the Falklands (1982), Amber Rudd over Windrush (2018). They can conflict: a minister who cannot defend a Cabinet decision must resign (collective), yet ministers frequently survive departmental failure when the PM declines to enforce individual responsibility — showing both conventions depend on prime ministerial judgement rather than automatic sanction.
Explain and analyse three factors that affect the power of the prime minister (Edexcel, 12 marks)
Answer: First, the size of the government majority: Thatcher (144 after 1983) and Blair (179 after 1997) dominated Parliament and Cabinet because rebels could not cost them anything, whereas May's minority government after 2017 made the DUP and every Cabinet faction decisive. Second, party unity: a united party underwrites the PM's authority, but division destroys it — Howe's 1990 resignation speech triggered Thatcher's fall, and in July 2022 the resignations of Sunak and Javid, followed by 50+ ministers, forced Johnson's exit despite his 80-seat majority. Third, events and circumstances: crisis centralises power in No.10 (Falklands 1982; COVID-19 from 2020; Ukraine 2022), but adverse events reverse it — Truss's mini-budget (September 2022) was reversed within weeks by markets, the Bank of England and her own MPs, ending a 49-day premiership. The factors interact: a large majority only empowers if the party stays united, and events test both.
Evaluate the view that the UK prime minister is now effectively a president (Edexcel, 30 marks)
Answer: The presidential thesis (Foley, 2000) has strong support. Spatial leadership: modern PMs present themselves as brands apart from their parties — 'Blair's Britain', 'Get Brexit Done' under Johnson. Personalised mandates: leader-centred campaigns, televised debates and media interviews fought directly with the public. Patronage and prerogative: control of 100+ posts, deployment of forces (Iraq 2003), treaty-making and the calling of elections restored by the Dissolution and Calling of Parliament Act 2022. Strong-majority PMs have dominated Cabinet like chief executives: Thatcher dictated the poll tax; Blair's sofa government sidelined the formal Cabinet. Yet the counter-case is substantial. Constitutional structure denies the label: the PM is primus inter pares, answerable weekly in PMQs, with no separation of powers — accountable to Parliament in a way presidents are not. Four of the last eight PMs (Brown, May, Truss, Sunak) took office without winning a general election as leader, puncturing the 'personal mandate' claim. Real checks bind: collective Cabinet proved essential in COVID COBRA decision-making; Parliament defeated May 432–202 and forced her exit; party, not presidential politics, removed Johnson; and the courts bound the prerogative in Miller I (2017) and Miller II (2019), voiding prorogation. Judgement: 'effectively a president' overstates — the PM is presidential in presentation and resources but parliamentary in power. Dominance is contingent on majority, unity and events, and Miller II confirms that even the outward trappings of presidential power remain subject to courts and conventions.
Evaluate the view that prime ministerial power is determined more by circumstances than by personality (AQA, 25 marks)
Answer: Circumstances carry most weight. Structural factors set the arena: majority size (Thatcher's 144 in 1983 versus May's minority after 2017), party unity (the 2022 resignations ended Johnson regardless of his personality), and events — the Falklands transformed Thatcher's authority, COVID-19 centralised Johnson's, and markets destroyed Truss's in 49 days. Institutional constraints are impersonal too: Miller II (2019) would have checked any PM's prorogation, and devolution meant No.10 could not impose one COVID rule on four nations. Yet personality still matters at the margin. Blair's media mastery and Johnson's celebrity sustained unpopular positions longer than circumstances alone would allow, while Thatcher's domineering style — not just the poll tax — alienated ministers whose resignations proved fatal. Cabinet management style (sofa government versus May's inclusiveness) is a personal choice with structural consequences. Judgement: circumstances define the limits of power — majority, unity and events decide whether a PM can dictate policy, as the AQA-cited examples of the poll tax (1990) and Iraq (2003) each show — but personality determines how close to those limits a PM operates and whether they survive crossing them.
Explain and analyse three ways in which Parliament can hold the prime minister to account (Edexcel, 12 marks)
Answer: First, Prime Minister's Questions and statements: the weekly PMQ session and urgent statements force the PM to answer publicly — May was visibly damaged by PMQs after the 2017 defeat, and Johnson faced direct questioning over Partygate. Second, select committees: departmental committees question ministers and officials, and the Liaison Committee questions the PM directly several times a year — the Privileges Committee investigation into Johnson's Partygate evidence shows committee scrutiny can reach the PM's own conduct, though government whips influence committee membership. Third, votes and legislative control: the Commons can defeat government business — May's 432–202 meaningful-vote defeat in January 2019, the largest modern government defeat, stripped her Brexit strategy of authority — and can seize control of the timetable, as the 2019 'Letwin amendment' did. The mechanisms are strongest against weak-majority PMs and weakest when a landslide majority makes votes a formality.
