The Judiciary and Civil Liberties

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA, Edexcel

A* revision on the judiciary and civil liberties for AQA 7152 and Edexcel 9PL0: composition and appointments, the Supreme Court, Miller I and Miller II, the Human Rights Act 1998 and judicial independence — with full 9-, 25- and 30-mark model answers.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Describe the composition of the judiciary and the appointments process — the Constitutional Reform Act 2005, the Supreme Court (2009) and the Judicial Appointments Commission (AQA 3.1.1.4; Edexcel P2 Topic 4)
  • Distinguish judicial independence from judicial impartiality (neutrality) and evaluate how each is protected or threatened (AO1/AO2)
  • Explain the role and powers of the Supreme Court: judicial review, ultra vires, statutory interpretation under HRA ss.3 and 4 (AO1/AO2)
  • Evaluate the impact of Miller I (2017) and Miller II (2019) on the executive, legislature and the policy process (AO2/AO3)
  • Assess how far the Human Rights Act 1998 protects civil liberties, using cases such as A v SSHD (2004) and PJS v News UK (2016) (AO2/AO3)
  • Construct balanced judgements on the politicisation of the judiciary and on further reform, linking to the location of sovereignty (AO3)

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Judicial independence: The principle that judges decide cases free from pressure by the executive, Parliament, parties or the media — protected by security of tenure, paid salaries from the Consolidated Fund, sub judice rules and a ban on political activity.
Judicial impartiality: The principle (AQA's term; also 'neutrality') that judges apply the law without personal bias or political sympathy — assessed through the judiciary's social composition and case outcomes.
Separation of powers: Montesquieu's principle that legislative, executive and judicial power should be held by different bodies; the Constitutional Reform Act 2005 moved the UK closer to it by separating the judiciary from the Lord Chancellor and the Lords.
Ultra vires: 'Beyond the powers': the doctrine that public bodies acting outside the powers given to them by Parliament (or natural justice) can be struck down by the courts.
Judicial review: The process by which the High Court and Supreme Court review the lawfulness of executive action and delegated legislation — a check on the executive, not on Acts of Parliament.
Declaration of incompatibility: Under s.4 of the Human Rights Act 1998, a court statement that legislation is incompatible with the ECHR; it does not strike the law down — Parliament chooses whether to respond, preserving parliamentary sovereignty.
Human Rights Act 1998: The Act that incorporated the European Convention on Human Rights into UK law: s.3 requires compatible interpretation of statutes where possible, s.4 allows declarations of incompatibility, s.6 makes it unlawful for public authorities to act incompatibly.
European Convention on Human Rights: The Council of Europe treaty (1950) whose rights — life (Art 2), liberty (Art 5), fair trial (Art 6), privacy (Art 8), expression (Art 10) — are enforced in UK courts through the HRA, not by EU membership.
Constitutional Reform Act 2005: The Act that ended the Law Lords' seat in the House of Lords, created the Supreme Court (opened 2009), curtailed the Lord Chancellor's combined role and created the Judicial Appointments Commission.
Judicial Appointments Commission: The independent commission (est. 2006) that selects judges through open competition on merit, replacing the Lord Chancellor's secretive 'secret soundings' of candidates.
Sub judice: The rule that MPs and peers may not debate cases active before the courts — one safeguard of judicial independence from Parliament.
Residual liberties: Dicey's view of UK rights: everything is permitted unless the law forbids it, and rights result from ordinary law and court judgments rather than a written charter — contrasted with the positive, listed rights of the HRA.

📌 Key Points

Miller I (2017, 8–3): triggering Article 50 required an Act of Parliament — the prerogative cannot override statute.
Miller II (2019, 11–0): the five-week prorogation was unlawful, 'void and of no effect' — a direct check on the executive.
A v SSHD (2004, Belmarsh): detention of foreign terror suspects was incompatible with ECHR Arts 5 and 14 — a s.4 declaration that forced the Prevention of Terrorism Act 2005.
Declarations of incompatibility do not strike down Acts — parliamentary sovereignty survives Miller I/II.
The Judicial Review and Courts Act 2022 limited quashing orders and abolished Cart judicial reviews — executive push-back against judicial power.

Composition and appointments: independence by design

Before 2005 the most senior judges sat as Law Lords in the House of Lords — legislators and judges — while the Lord Chancellor combined executive, legislative and judicial roles in one office. The Constitutional Reform Act 2005 unpicked this: it created the Supreme Court (opened October 2009 in the Middlesex Guildhall, Parliament Square), ended the Lord Chancellor's headship of the judiciary (transferred to the Lord Chief Justice) and established the Judicial Appointments Commission (2006), which selects judges through open competition on merit rather than the 'secret soundings' by which past Lord Chancellors picked judges who shared the government's politics. Security of tenure (judges cannot be removed for their rulings, under the Act of Settlement 1701), salaries paid from the Consolidated Fund, the sub judice rule and a bar on judges' political activity all protect judicial independence — the absence of pressure from the executive, Parliament, parties or media.

Impartiality (neutrality) is harder to guarantee by design because it depends on who the judges are. Critics point to the composition of the senior judiciary: most Supreme Court justices were educated at Oxford or Cambridge, many privately schooled, and the bench has historically been dominated by white men — Lady Hale became the first female President of the Supreme Court only in 2017, and ethnic-minority and state-school representation at the top remains thin, though women now make up around a third of judges overall and diversity below the top has improved. Defenders reply that judicial diversity has increased, that appointments are merit-based and transparent since the JAC, and that judgments themselves (unanimous 11–0 rulings, decisions against governments of both parties) demonstrate impartiality in practice. For essays, keep the two concepts distinct: independence = freedom from pressure (institutional), impartiality = absence of bias (compositional and behavioural).

The role and powers of the Supreme Court

The Supreme Court is the final court of appeal for all UK civil cases and for criminal cases from England, Wales and Northern Ireland, and it hears devolution disputes. It only decides points of law of general public importance — typically 60–80 appeals a year — but its impact on government, the legislature and the policy process is disproportionate (AQA 3.1.1.4). Its main weapons:

  • Judicial review of executive action. Under the doctrine of ultra vires, ministers and public bodies acting beyond the powers Parliament gave them — or contrary to natural justice — can be struck down. R (UNISON) v Lord Chancellor (2017) quashed employment tribunal fees: the Court held that access to justice is a constitutional right, not a commodity, forcing the government to repay £27m+ in fees.
  • Statutory interpretation under the HRA. Section 3 requires statutes to be read compatibly with the ECHR where possible (Ghaidan v Godin-Mendoza, 2004); where impossible, s.4 permits a declaration of incompatibility — which does not strike the law down. Parliament usually responds: after A v SSHD (2004) — the Belmarsh case, where detaining foreign terror suspects without trial was held incompatible with ECHR Arts 5 and 14 — the government replaced detention with control orders (Prevention of Terrorism Act 2005).
  • Constraining the prerogative. Miller I and Miller II (below) proved that even the executive's most traditional prerogative powers are reviewable — a shift in the location of sovereignty in all but name.

Note the limit: the Court cannot strike down Acts of Parliament. Miller I (2017, 8–3) held that triggering Article 50 required an Act of Parliament because the prerogative cannot override statute; it also confirmed the Sewel convention (no legislation on devolved matters without consent) is political, not justiciable. The Court polices the boundary between prerogative and statute — it does not overrule Westminster.

Miller I and Miller II: the Supreme Court versus the executive

Miller I — R (Miller) v Secretary of State for Exiting the European Union (2017). The government argued its prerogative powers over treaty-making let it trigger Article 50 without Parliament. The Supreme Court disagreed, 8–3: ministers cannot use prerogative powers to remove rights established by Parliament (the European Communities Act 1972) — primary legislation was required, and the European Union (Notification of Withdrawal) Act 2017 followed. Significance: the Court reasserted parliamentary sovereignty at the moment of the biggest constitutional change since 1945, and MPs were given a vote ministers wanted to avoid. The press reaction mattered as much as the ruling: the Daily Mail's 'Enemies of the people' front page (November 2016, after the High Court stage) crystallised the accusation that judges had entered politics.

Miller II — R (Miller) v The Prime Minister; Cherry v Advocate General for Scotland (2019). Boris Johnson prorogued Parliament for five weeks in September 2019 — the longest prorogation in recent history, in the final stages of the Brexit deadline. All 11 justices ruled the prorogation unlawful, void and of no effect: a decision with 'such an extreme effect' on the fundamentals of parliamentary democracy is not beyond judicial review. Parliament resumed within days; the Brexit deadline was then extended by the Benn Act. Significance: a unanimous Court set a justiciable limit on even the prerogative of prorogation — the strongest modern assertion that no minister is above the law. The executive responded by legislating: the Dissolution and Calling of Parliament Act 2022 restored full prerogative control of dissolution, and the Judicial Review and Courts Act 2022 limited quashing orders and abolished Cart judicial reviews — evidence for essays that executive push-back against judicial power is itself a live constitutional issue.

For A* evaluation: Miller I and II show the Court shaping the policy process (forcing Acts, voiding executive action) without acquiring American-style power — the sovereignty of Parliament survived intact because declarations and procedural rulings leave the last word to Westminster. Compare AQA's alternative phrasing: the Supreme Court 'interacts with and influences' the legislative process rather than controlling it.

Civil liberties and the Human Rights Act 1998

The HRA 1998 incorporated the European Convention on Human Rights into UK law, converting Dicey's residual liberties (everything legal unless forbidden) into positive rights that public authorities must actively respect (s.6). Rights with heavy exam use: liberty of the person (Art 5), fair trial (Art 6), privacy (Art 8), freedom of expression (Art 10). The Act works through courts, not repeal:

  • s.3 interpretation: courts read legislation compatibly wherever possible — Ghaidan v Godin-Mendoza (2004) read 'surviving spouse' to include same-sex partners without amending the statute.
  • s.4 declarations of incompatibility: A v SSHD (2004) — Belmarsh — held detention of foreign terrorism suspects incompatible with Arts 5 and 14; Parliament replaced the regime with control orders. Hirst v UK (2005) ruled the blanket ban on prisoner voting unlawful; Parliament, defying the judgment, kept the ban — proof that incompatibility judgments are advisory pressure, not commands.
  • Arts 8 v 10 conflicts: PJS v News Group Newspapers (2016) upheld a privacy injunction against tabloid reporting of an extramarital affair — the Court prioritised privacy, though the story spread online, showing the limits of court orders in the internet age.

Evaluation for essays: the HRA is the most consequential rights reform since 1997 — ordinary citizens can enforce rights in UK courts without going to Strasbourg, and the courts have checked counter-terror legislation (Belmarsh), Home Office policy (Windrush-era disputes) and press power. Against: rights remain under parliamentary sovereignty — incompatibility can be ignored (Hirst), recent governments have sought repeal or replacement (the Conservative Bill of Rights Bill 2022, introduced then abandoned), and the government elected in 2024 pledged to keep the HRA while reforming its operation. The civil-liberties verdict is therefore conditional: the HRA protects rights through parliamentary sovereignty, and its protection is only as strong as Parliament's willingness to respond.

Independence under pressure: politicisation and reform debates

Judicial independence survives in structure, but its perception has been battered. After Miller I, tabloids branded judges 'Enemies of the people'; after Miller II, ministers and commentators attacked a ' politicised' court; Home Secretaries and Justice Secretaries have publicly criticised rulings and sentencing, and the Johnson-era Ministerial Code changes and Judicial Review and Courts Act 2022 showed executive willingness to trim judicial power. Structural rebuttals for essays: judges cannot be sacked for their rulings (Act of Settlement 1701); appointments are independent of ministers since the JAC (2006); the Court rules against governments of both parties (UNISON 2017 against the Conservative fee regime; Miller I/II; A v SSHD against Labour); and unanimity (11–0 in Miller II) signals law, not politics. The scholarly case that composition undermines impartiality — elite education, under-representation of women and ethnic minorities at senior levels — is best used to concede neutrality concerns while distinguishing them from independence.

Reform debates for evaluation: (1) Should the Supreme Court have power to strike down legislation, as in the USA? Against: it would end parliamentary sovereignty and politicise appointments still further; for: s.4 declarations are weak pressure when Parliament ignores them (Hirst). (2) Should judicial review be restricted? The Judicial Review and Courts Act 2022 did restrict it (suspended quashing orders, abolition of Cart reviews) — weigh government efficiency and the separation of powers against access to justice (UNISON). (3) Should appointments include ministers again, or diversify further? JAC merit-based appointment v the old secret soundings; diversity v judicial neutrality debates.

Synoptic links: the location of sovereignty (Parliament still supreme — s.4; but prerogative now judicially bounded); devolution (the Court resolves devolution disputes, making it a quasi-constitutional arbiter); the constitution topic (Miller I/II as key evidence for codification and entrenchment arguments); and the executive topic (prerogative constraints on the PM). A top-band judgement: the judiciary has grown from the least to a leading branch since 2005 — Miller I and II made it a genuine check on the executive — yet it remains deliberately powerless against Acts of Parliament, by design.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: Evaluate the view that the UK Supreme Court is now the most powerful branch of government (AQA 7152, 25 marks) | Evaluate the extent to which the UK judiciary is independent and impartial (Edexcel 9PL0 Paper 2, 30 marks)
Mark-scheme logic: Mark-scheme logic for a top-band answer. AO1: accurate account of composition and appointments (CRA 2005, JAC 2006, Supreme Court 2009), powers (judicial review, ultra vires, HRA s.3/s.4) and key cases — Miller I (2017), Miller II (2019), A v SSHD (2004), UNISON (2017), PJS (2016). AO2: analysis of the Court's impact on the executive and legislature (forcing the EU (Notification of Withdrawal) Act 2017; voiding prorogation; Belmarsh forcing new terror legislation) balanced against its limits (cannot strike down Acts; Sewel non-justiciable; Parliament ignored Hirst). AO3: sustained judgement — the Court is a genuine but deliberately limited check: 'most powerful branch' overstates because parliamentary sovereignty survives (s.4, Miller I) and the executive pushed back legislatively (DCPA 2022, JRCA 2022); on independence/impartiality versions, conclude that independence is institutionally robust (tenure, JAC, rulings against both parties) while impartiality concerns from elite composition are real but weakening.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Analyse the importance of ultra vires and judicial review (AQA, 9 marks)
Answer: Ultra vires ('beyond the powers') is the doctrine that public bodies can be struck down for acting outside the powers Parliament gave them or contrary to natural justice; judicial review is the process that enforces it. Its importance is threefold. First, it polices the executive: R (UNISON) v Lord Chancellor (2017) quashed employment tribunal fees, establishing access to justice as a constitutional right. Second, it upholds parliamentary sovereignty: judicial review enforces the limits Parliament itself has set, rather than substituting judges' policy views — the courts are applying statute, not overruling it. Third, it disciplines administration: the threat of review forces consultation, reason-giving and lawful procedure across government. Its limits frame the evaluation: it checks ministers and delegated legislation, not Acts of Parliament — Miller I (2017) is the boundary case, holding the prerogative cannot override statute, so judicial review protects the Westminster balance of power rather than undermining it.
Explain and analyse three ways in which judicial independence is protected (Edexcel, 12 marks)
Answer: First, security of tenure: under the Act of Settlement 1701 judges cannot be removed for their rulings, so no minister can threaten a judge's job — the foundation of independence. Second, appointments reform: the Constitutional Reform Act 2005 created the Judicial Appointments Commission (2006), replacing the Lord Chancellor's 'secret soundings' with open, merit-based competition, and the 2005 Act also separated the judiciary from the legislature (ending the Law Lords' seat in the Lords) and curtailed the Lord Chancellor's combined role. Third, financial and behavioural safeguards: judges' salaries are paid from the Consolidated Fund without annual parliamentary vote, the sub judice rule stops MPs debating active cases, and a convention bars judges from active politics — reinforced by practice such as the Supreme Court's unanimous, cross-party record (Miller II was 11–0, UNISON 2017 struck down a Conservative policy, A v SSHD 2004 a Labour one). Together these show independence is protected by statute, institution and convention — though tabloid attacks ('Enemies of the people') and executive criticism after Miller I/II show independence of perception remains contested.
Evaluate the impact of the Supreme Court on government, legislature and the policy process (AQA, 25 marks)
Answer: The Court's impact on the executive is now decisive at key moments. Miller I (2017, 8–3) forced the EU (Notification of Withdrawal) Act 2017 — Parliament got a vote ministers wanted to avoid; Miller II (2019, 11–0) voided the five-week prorogation, forcing Parliament's recall and shaping the Brexit timetable; UNISON (2017) reversed employment tribunal fees; A v SSHD (2004, as the Law Lords) forced the replacement of Belmarsh detention with control orders. These show the Court determining the policy process, not merely interpreting it. Its impact on the legislature is real but bounded: s.4 declarations pressure Parliament (Belmarsh produced the Prevention of Terrorism Act 2005) yet can be ignored (Hirst v UK 2005 — prisoner voting), and Miller I confirmed the Sewel convention is political, not justiciable. The Court therefore shapes — it does not control — legislation. Evaluation: 'most powerful branch' claims overstate, because parliamentary sovereignty survived Miller I/II intact and the executive has legislated to trim judicial power (Dissolution and Calling of Parliament Act 2022; Judicial Review and Courts Act 2022). Judgement: since 2005 the Court has moved from the weakest to a genuinely powerful check — it can void ministerial action and force statutes — but it remains, by design, the servant of parliamentary sovereignty: its power is the power to police the boundary between prerogative and statute, not to govern.
Evaluate the extent to which the UK judiciary is both independent and impartial (Edexcel, 30 marks)
Answer: Independence is institutionally robust. Security of tenure (Act of Settlement 1701) means judges cannot be removed for their rulings; salaries come from the Consolidated Fund; the sub judice rule and the ban on judicial political activity separate the courts from Westminster; and since the Constitutional Reform Act 2005 the Supreme Court (2009) sits outside the legislature and appointments go through the independent Judicial Appointments Commission (2006) on merit. Behaviour confirms structure: the Court rules against governments of both parties — A v SSHD (2004) against Labour's Belmarsh detention, UNISON (2017) against Conservative tribunal fees, Miller I (2017) and Miller II (2019) against Conservative Brexit strategy — and Miller II's 11–0 unanimity signalled legal, not political, reasoning. Impartiality is weaker on paper. The senior judiciary remains socially elite: most Supreme Court justices are Oxbridge-educated, many privately schooled, and until Lady Hale's appointment as the first female President in 2017 the Court had never had a woman at its head; ethnic-minority representation at the top remains poor. Critics argue this monoculture produces blind spots (rights of migrants, prisoners, protesters), and high-profile conflicts — 'Enemies of the people' after Miller I, ministerial attacks after Miller II — fuel the perception of politicisation even when rulings are legally sound. Counter-arguments: composition is improving (around a third of judges are now women; JAC open competitions widen the pool), judges' decisions routinely favour the unpopular and the powerless, and impartiality is best judged by outcomes, which cut against both parties. Judgement: independence is strong — protected by statute, institution and a record of rulings against government — while impartiality is a live but narrowing concern rooted in composition rather than conduct; conflating the two, as tabloid and ministerial criticism often does, is the central error in this debate.
Evaluate the view that the Human Rights Act 1998 has done more to protect civil liberties in the UK than any other constitutional reform since 1997 (Edexcel, 30 marks)
Answer: The case for the HRA is strong. It converted Dicey's residual liberties into positive, enforceable rights: public authorities must act compatibly (s.6), courts must interpret statutes compatibly (s.3 — Ghaidan v Godin-Mendoza 2004), and citizens can assert ECHR rights in UK courts rather than spending years in Strasbourg. Its record includes direct vindication: A v SSHD (2004) struck at Belmarsh detention of foreign suspects — Parliament replaced it with control orders; UNISON (2017) protected access to justice; PJS v News UK (2016) upheld privacy against tabloids. No other single reform since 1997 has generated a comparable body of judicial protection for individual rights. Alternatives, however, deserve weight. Devolution embedded rights in the Scotland Act 1998 and gave national institutions whose existence the courts now defend; the Freedom of Information Act 2000 created transparency rights enabling scrutiny of government (expenses scandal 2009); and common law rights, vindicated in Miller II (2019), operate independently of the HRA. The case against the HRA itself: rights remain subordinate to parliamentary sovereignty — s.4 declarations are advisory and were defied after Hirst v UK (2005) on prisoner voting; s.3's 'reading down' has been attacked as judicial legislation; and repeated attempts to replace the Act (the Conservative Bill of Rights Bill 2022, abandoned) show its protection is politically fragile. Judgement: the HRA is the most consequential rights-protection reform since 1997 because it is justiciable by ordinary citizens in UK courts and has reshaped counter-terror, privacy and Home Office practice — but it protects liberties through, not against, parliamentary sovereignty, so its strength ultimately depends on Parliament's willingness to respond to the courts.
