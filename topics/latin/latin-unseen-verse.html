Unseen Verse Translation and Scansion

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level OCR, WJEC/Eduqas

OCR H443 unseen verse: hexameter scansion (dactyls, spondees, elision), Ovid and Virgil translation technique, with a worked example and metre rules.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Scan a Latin hexameter line correctly (feet, elision, ictus)
  • Apply scansion to resolve ambiguities in translation
  • Translate Ovidian and Virgilian verse with awareness of word order and style
  • Recognise verse style markers (chiasmus, hyperbaton, golden line)

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Hexameter: The epic metre: six feet, each dactyl (long-short-short) or spondee (long-long), the fifth usually a dactyl, the sixth always a spondee (or trochee).
Dactyl: A foot of one long plus two short syllables (−∪∪); the hexameter's most common foot.
Spondee: A foot of two long syllables (−−); slower and weightier, used for emphasis and solemnity.
Elision: A vowel or -m at a word's end is elided before a word beginning with a vowel or h: 'ave atque' scans 'av' atque'; the elided syllable does not count.
Caesura: A word-break within a foot, usually after the first syllable of the third foot (penthemimeral); the line's sense-break point.
Golden line: A hexameter patterned A-B-A-B (adjective-noun-verb-adjective-noun); a Virgilian style marker.

Scanning the hexameter: the rules

The foot pattern. A Latin hexameter = six feet: feet 1-4 dactyls (−∪∪) or spondees (−−), foot 5 usually a dactyl, foot 6 always two longs (−− or −∪ with the final syllable counted long — the trochee). Syllable quantity: a syllable is long by nature (a long vowel or a diphthong) or by position (a short vowel followed by two or more consonants, or by x/z). Elision: a word ending in a vowel or -m loses that syllable before a vowel-initial (or h-) word — 'atque' before 'omnes' scans as one elided unit; a monosyllable ending in -m sometimes resists (the rare 'ecthlipsis').

Scanning method. (1) Mark all elisions first; (2) mark long-by-nature syllables (diphthongs: ae, oe, au; long vowels); (3) mark long-by-position (vowel + 2 consonants); (4) fill the remaining feet from the left: each foot = − or −∪∪, with the fifth dactyl and the sixth spondee forced; (5) mark the caesura (usually the third foot's break). If the pattern breaks, check for an missed elision or a misjudged quantity — the metre is a self-checking system.

Translating Ovid and Virgil: style and technique

Word order. Verse word order is free — the metre's requirements rearrange the syntax. Translate from the MAIN VERB outwards as in prose, but expect: hyperbaton (separated adjective-noun pairs: 'magnanimum... Aenean' split across the line), chiasmus (A-B-B-A patterns), the golden line (A-B-A-B). Identify the verb and its subject first, then rebuild the sentence in English word order.

Style markers as translation aids. Ovid's style (Metamorphoses, Amores): witty, balanced, antithetical — expect paired phrases and rhetorical questions; his elegiac couplets (the Amores) alternate hexameter + pentameter. Virgil's style (Aeneid): elevated, solemn — expect spondaic lines at moments of weight, the golden line, enjambment (the sense running past the line's end). Enjambment matters for translation: the sentence's sense may complete in the NEXT line — always translate in sentence units, not line units.

Worked example and exam technique

Worked mini-example (adapted Virgil): 'Arma virumque cano, Troiae qui primus ab oris | Italiam fato profugus Laviniaque venit | litora.' Scansion: line 1 — Ar-ma (−∪) vi-rum-que (−∪) ca-no (−−)... the elision check: no elisions; the penthemimeral caesura after 'cano'. Translation: 'I sing of arms and the man who first came from the coasts of Troy to Italy and the Lavinian shores, an exile by fate' — the hyperbaton ('fato profugus' inserted) and enjambment ('litora' completing the sense in line 3) rebuilt in English order.

Exam technique. (1) Scan the line FIRST — the metre confirms quantities and reveals elisions; (2) find the main verb and subject; (3) translate in sentence units (not line units — enjambment); (4) mark the style markers (chiasmus, hyperbaton) and rebuild in English order; (5) check quantities at the end (a 'long' vowel misjudged distorts the foot). OCR marks: the verse unseen = translation (c. 30 marks) + scansion (c. 10) + comprehension; accuracy of metre confirms the translation's correctness — the two feed each other.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

What are the rules for syllable quantity in Latin verse?
Answer: A syllable is long by NATURE (a long vowel — marked macron — or a diphthong: ae, oe, au, ei, ui sometimes) or by POSITION (a short vowel followed by two or more consonants, or by x/z; the doubled consonant counts twice). A vowel before another vowel is normally short (except in diphthongs); h does not lengthen. The final syllable of a hexameter line counts as long regardless.
How does elision work, and how do you mark it?
Answer: A word ending in a vowel or -m loses its final syllable before a word beginning with a vowel or h: 'monstrum horrendum' scans 'monstr' horrendum; 'atque' + 'omnes' = one elided unit. Mark all elisions FIRST when scanning — an unmarked elision breaks the foot pattern and misleads the whole line. Muta cum liquida (tr + pl + cr clusters) may keep the preceding vowel short — check the context.
How would you translate a line with heavy hyperbaton?
Answer: Find the main verb and its subject first, then pair the separated adjective-noun combinations: Virgil's 'magnanimum Aenean' may be split across the line ('magnanimum... Aenean') — rebuild in English order ('great-hearted Aeneas'). The hyperbaton is a style marker (emphasis through separation), not a syntax change — translate the MEANING, and note the stylistic effect where the comprehension questions ask.
What is a golden line and why does it matter?
Answer: A hexameter patterned A-B-A-B (adjective-noun-verb-adjective-noun, with the verb central): a Virgilian elegance marker. It matters for translation (the verb's central position requires rebuilding the English order) and for the literary-technique questions (recognising the style as deliberate artistry). Example: 'aurea prima satas... aetas' patterns.
How should enjambment affect your translation?
Answer: Translate in SENTENCE units, not line units: Virgil's enjambment runs the sense past the line's end ('litora' completing in the next line), so a line-by-line translation produces non-English. Read to the sentence's full stop, then translate; the line breaks are metre's business, not syntax's — the comprehension questions may ask for the effect (emphasis, surprise) of the enjambment.
