Unseen Prose Translation

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level OCR, WJEC/Eduqas

OCR H443 unseen prose translation technique: vocabulary triage, finding the main verbs, subjunctives, participles and indirect statement — with a worked example and exam method.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Apply a translation method to an unseen prose passage (vocab triage, main verbs, clause structure)
  • Identify and translate subjunctive clauses, participles and indirect statement in context
  • Manage unseen vocabulary by recognising derivatives and compound verbs
  • Allocate exam time correctly and produce a fluent, accurate translation

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Subjunctive: The mood of hypothesis, purpose, result and reported thought; in unseen prose its main uses are purpose, result, indirect questions and cum-circumstantial clauses.
Indirect statement: Accusative + infinitive after verbs of saying, thinking and perceiving; the infinitive's tense is relative to the main verb (present = same time, perfect = before, future = after).
Participle: A verbal adjective: present (-ns, active), perfect (-tus/-sus, passive), future (-turus/-surus); translate as a clause ('when/after/because X had been...') where a literal rendering fails.
Ablative absolute: A noun + participle in the ablative expressing attendant circumstances, cause or time: 'when X had been done...' — always translate as a separate clause.
Gerundive: The passive verbal adjective in -ndus; with esse it expresses obligation ('must be done'); in the gerundive of obligation the gerundive agrees with the noun.
Historic infinitive: An infinitive standing alone in narrative ('to march and to fight...'), expressing vivid continuing action.

The method: how to attack an unseen passage

First pass: the shape of the sentence. Latin prose (adapted Livy, Cicero, Caesar) is highly periodic: the main verb often comes LAST. Do not translate word by word from the start. Instead: (1) read the sentence to its end; (2) find the main verb(s) and the subject (nominative); (3) bracket the subordinate clauses (quod/ut/cum/si + indicative or subjunctive); (4) mark the participles and ablatives absolute — each becomes a separate clause in English; (5) only then translate, from the main clause outwards.

Vocabulary triage. In the OCR unseen, roughly 5-10% of words are glossed; the rest you should know or recognise: (1) compounds: adversus (ad + versus), appropinquare (ad + prope); (2) derivatives: expugnare → to fight out fully = to storm; (3) the core function words (nam, igitur, tamen, quidem) carry the argument's logic — translate them first for the sentence's drift. Never spend two minutes on one word: mark it, guess from context, move on, return at the end.

Time management. The unseen translation paper allows roughly 1.5-2 minutes per line. Budget: first pass 30% of the time, translation 60%, checking 10%. Write the translation in fluent English — a literal rendering that produces non-English loses the accuracy marks; examiners mark for sense conveyed accurately.

The grammar traps: subjunctives, participles, indirect statement

TrapHow to spot itHow to translate it
Purpose clauseut/ne + subjunctive, no main-verb tense dependency'in order that...', 'so that... not'
Result clauseut + subjunctive, TAM/talis/sic in the main clause'with the result that...', 'so... that'
Indirect questionquestion word (quis, ubi, cur, num) + subjunctive'asked where/whether/why...' — backshift the tense
Cum-circumstantialcum + subjunctive (imperfect/pluperfect)'when/while/after...'; cum + indicative = 'since/although'
Indirect statementaccusative + infinitive after dico, puto, scio, video'that X was/would be...' — tense relative to the main verb
Ablative absolutenoun + participle in the ablative, unconnected'when/after/since X had been...'
Gerundive of obligation-ndus + form of esse'must be...', 'needs to be...'
Conditionssi/nisi + indicative or subjunctiveopen ('if X happens'), future less vivid ('if X were to happen'), unreal ('if X had happened, would have...')

Participles in detail. The perfect passive participle with a preceding noun usually needs a time clause: 'urbe capta' = 'when the city had been captured'. The present participle is continuous ('while doing'); the future participle is intention ('about to do', or purpose with a verb of coming). The ablative absolute is the most common unseen-prose structure — find them all before translating.

Worked example and exam technique

Worked mini-example (adapted Livy): 'Urbem sine pugna tradidissent hostes, ni consul, spe victoriae adductus, copias in castris recitavisset.' Method: (1) main verbs — tradidissent (pluperfect subjunctive, 3rd plural) and recitavisset (pluperfect subjunctive); (2) the structure: ni + subjunctive = unreal condition; ablatives absolute: 'spe victoriae adductus'? — no: 'adductus' modifies consul (nominative); (3) translation: 'The enemy would have surrendered the city without a fight, if the consul, led on by hope of victory, had not drawn up his forces in camp.' Check: the subjunctives agree (unreal condition in past time), the ablative ('spe victoriae') is cause ('led on BY hope'), the word order reversed for emphasis.

Exam technique. (1) Read the passage's first and last sentences for its subject matter; (2) identify the narrative's subject (a general? an embassy?) and keep pronouns resolved; (3) translate in fluent, natural English — prefer 'the enemy surrendered' to 'the enemies they surrendered'; (4) mark unknowns with a dash and return; (5) check number/person agreement and tense backshift in indirect speech at the end. OCR marks: 45 marks for the translation, marks lost per error category (tense, case, sense) — accuracy of SENSE beats literalism every time.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

How would you translate a cum-clause with a PLUPERFECT subjunctive, and why does the tense matter?
Answer: 'After/when X had [verb]ed...' — the pluperfect subjunctive marks the clause's action as BEFORE the main verb's action, so the English must show the sequence: 'when the city had been captured, the general returned to Rome'. With an imperfect subjunctive, 'while/when X was [verb]ing' — simultaneous with the main verb. The tense choice is the difference between 'when he returned' and 'after he had returned' — marks are lost when the sequence is flattened.
Translate: 'Dux, urbibus munitis, legiones in hiberna deduxit.' What structures do you spot?
Answer: Ablative absolute: 'urbibus munitis' = 'when the towns had been fortified' (noun + perfect passive participle in the ablative). Main clause: 'the general led the legions into winter quarters' ('deduxit' = perfect active, dux + in hiberna = where). Fluency check: 'After fortifying the towns, the general led his legions into winter quarters' — natural English; the ablative absolute rendered as a time clause.
How do you handle an indirect statement after a historic present main verb?
Answer: The infinitive's tense is relative to the MAIN verb: after a historic present, the present infinitive = same time ('dicit hostes venire' = 'he says that the enemy are coming' — or in narrative: 'he says the enemy are coming'), the perfect infinitive = before, the future = after. In narrative translation, keep the historic present ('he says', 'he reports') OR convert to past throughout — but never mix; consistency of tense is an accuracy mark.
What is the fastest way to find the sense of an unknown compound verb?
Answer: Split it: the preposition prefix (ad-, in-, ex-, con-, ob-, prae-) + the base verb you know. 'Expugnare' = ex + pugnare = 'to fight out' = 'to storm/capture'; 'appropinquare' = ad + prope = 'to approach'; 'conlocare' = con + locare = 'to place together/arrange'. Combined with context (the sentence's subject and object), the compound's sense is usually recoverable in seconds — far faster than a glossary search.
How should time be allocated in the OCR unseen translation?
Answer: Roughly 1.5-2 minutes per line: 30% of the time on the first pass (main verbs, clauses, participles, ablatives absolute), 60% translating from the main clause outwards, 10% checking tense sequences, agreement and pronoun resolution. Unknowns get a mark and a context guess, never a two-minute stall — sense conveyed accurately beats one perfect word.
