Metaethics: Cognitivism, Realism and Moral Language

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA

AQA 7172 Spec 3.2.3 metaethics: the two axes (cognitivism vs non-cognitivism, realism vs anti-realism), moral naturalism, Moore's open question argument, intuitionism, Hume's is-ought gap, Mackie's error theory, emotivism and Hare's prescriptivism — with 25-mark essay technique and full mark-scheme answers.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Distinguish the two metaethical axes — cognitivism vs non-cognitivism and realism vs anti-realism — and locate each theory on both axes (Spec 3.2.3).
  • Explain the possible origins of moral principles in reason, in emotion/attitudes or in society (Spec 3.2.3).
  • Explain moral naturalism, including naturalist forms of utilitarianism (Bentham) and of virtue ethics, and evaluate it using Moore's open question argument and the naturalistic fallacy (Spec 3.2.3).
  • Explain moral non-naturalism — Moore's intuitionism and Ross's prima facie duties — and evaluate the challenges from Hume's fork, the is-ought gap and the motivation argument (Spec 3.2.3).
  • Explain Mackie's error theory — the arguments from relativity and from queerness — and assess the nihilism charge (Spec 3.2.3).
  • Explain and evaluate emotivism (Ayer, Stevenson) and prescriptivism (Hare), including whether anti-realism can account for moral reasoning, disagreement and moral progress (Spec 3.2.3).
  • Analyse and evaluate metaethical arguments to form reasoned judgements in 25-mark essays (AO2, 'arguing with intent').

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Cognitivism: The view that moral judgements express beliefs, state propositions and are truth-apt: 'murder is wrong' is true or false and can be known. Shared by naturalism, non-naturalism and (in modified form) error theory.
Non-cognitivism: The view that moral judgements express non-cognitive mental states (emotions, prescriptions) and state nothing, so they are not truth-apt. Emotivism and prescriptivism are non-cognitivist theories.
Moral realism: There are mind-independent moral properties or facts: some moral judgements are true or false independently of what anyone thinks, feels or prescribes.
Moral anti-realism: There are no mind-independent moral properties or facts — either because moral judgements were never in the business of stating facts (emotivism, prescriptivism) or because, although they aim at objective truth, they systematically fail (error theory).
Moral naturalism: Cognitivist realism: moral properties are natural properties, reducible to or identical with properties studied by the sciences — e.g. naturalist utilitarianism (Bentham: good = pleasure, right = maximising it) and naturalist virtue ethics (good = traits enabling human flourishing).
Open question argument: Moore (Principia Ethica, 1903): for any proposed natural property N, the question 'but is N good?' remains open and significant, not self-answering like 'are bachelors unmarried?'; so good is not identical with any natural property — an argument against all reductive metaethical theories.
Is-ought gap: Hume: no 'ought' follows validly from premises stating only 'is' — a moral conclusion requires a moral premise, so sets of pure facts never entail obligations.
Error theory: Mackie (Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong, 1977): moral judgements purport to state objective, mind-independent facts, but there are no objective values, so every positive moral judgement is false; values are projected, not discovered.
Emotivism: Ayer: moral judgements fail the verification principle (neither analytic nor empirically verifiable), so they are not truth-apt statements but expressions of emotion — 'stealing is wrong' amounts to 'stealing — boo!' (the boo/hurrah theory); Stevenson adds emotive meaning and a persuasive function.
Prescriptivism: Hare (The Language of Morals, 1952): moral judgements are universalisable prescriptions — imperatives ('do this!') that commit the speaker to prescribing the same for any relevantly similar situation, including their own.

📌 Key Points

Two independent axes: cognitivism (moral judgements truth-apt) vs non-cognitivism (not truth-apt); realism (mind-independent moral facts) vs anti-realism (none). Every theory sits on both.
Moral principles may originate in reason (Moore, Ross), emotion/attitudes (Ayer) or society (Mackie: 'inventing' right and wrong).
Naturalism: good = natural property — Bentham: pleasure; naturalist virtue ethics: flourishing. Attacked by Moore's open question argument and the naturalistic fallacy.
Non-naturalism (Moore, Ross): good is simple, non-natural, known by intuition; Ross adds plural, self-evident prima facie duties. Attacked by Hume's fork, the is-ought gap, the motivation argument and Mackie's queerness.
Error theory (Mackie): judgements aim at objective truth but all fail — arguments from relativity and queerness; nihilism charge. Emotivism (boo/hurrah, persuasion) and prescriptivism (universalisable prescriptions) deny truth-aptness; attacked via embedded contexts, disagreement, the fanatic and moral progress.

The two axes and the origins of moral principles (Spec 3.2.3)

Second-order questions. First-order ethics asks 'Is abortion wrong?'; metaethics (AQA 7172 Spec 3.2.3) asks what such a judgement itself is: does it state a fact and aim at truth, or express an attitude and issue a command? Two independent axes organise the theories; a strong answer locates each theory on both.

Axis 1 — cognitivism vs non-cognitivism. Cognitivism: moral judgements express beliefs, assert propositions and are truth-apt — 'murder is wrong' is true or false and can be known. Non-cognitivism: moral judgements express non-cognitive states (emotions, prescriptions); they state nothing and are not truth-apt. Both camps agree moral sentences look declarative; the debate is what the utterance does.

Axis 2 — realism vs anti-realism. Moral realism: there are mind-independent moral properties or facts, so some moral judgements are objectively true. Moral anti-realism: there are none — either because moral judgements were never in the truth business (emotivism, prescriptivism) or because, though they aim at truth, they systematically fail (error theory).

Origins of moral principles. The spec opens 3.2.3 with this question: principles may originate in reason (self-evident truths grasped a priori — Moore, Ross), in emotion and attitudes (approval projected onto the world — Ayer, Mackie) or in society (invented conventions — Mackie's Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong). The answer fixes whether moral judgements can be true at all.

Cross-classification. The axes combine into a diagnostic grid:

Cognitivist (truth-apt)Non-cognitivist (not truth-apt)
Realist — mind-independent moral factsMoral naturalism (Bentham; naturalist virtue ethics); non-naturalism / intuitionism (Moore, Ross)—
Anti-realist — no mind-independent moral factsError theory (Mackie)Emotivism (Ayer, Stevenson); prescriptivism (Hare)

Examiner-flagged confusions (click to expand)
  • Mackie is not a non-cognitivist: error theory keeps the truth-aim and says it fails.
  • Non-cognitivism is not anti-realism: they answer different questions — semantics vs metaphysics.

Moral naturalism and Moore's open question argument (Spec 3.2.3)

Moral naturalism. Good, bad, right and wrong are natural properties: reducible to, or identical with, properties studied by the sciences. Two spec-favoured versions. (1) Naturalist utilitarianism (Bentham): 'good' means pleasure, 'right' means maximising it — moral facts are natural facts about pleasure and pain, discoverable and countable like any other facts (the felicific calculus). (2) Naturalist virtue ethics (Aristotle; Foot): 'good' means what enables a living thing to flourish as the kind of thing it is — good traits are those a well-developed member of the species displays, as good roots draw water. Attractions: it explains moral objectivity (moral claims true or false like scientific ones), moral knowledge (observation of pleasure and flourishing — no special faculty) and moral motivation (natural desires move us).

Moore's open question argument. Principia Ethica (1903): 'good' is simple and unanalysable — like 'yellow', it can only be pointed at, not defined. Any proposed natural definition N leaves 'but is N good?' open: significant, substantive, not self-answering. 'Is pleasure good?' is a genuine question (cruel pleasures seem bad); 'is a bachelor unmarried?' is closed. If good meant pleasure, 'pleasure is good' would be as trivial as 'pleasure is pleasure' — so good is not identical with any natural property. The argument targets all reductive metaethical theories — Bentham's, Foot's, any reduction. Defining good in natural terms is the naturalistic fallacy: deriving value from fact.

Critiques. (1) The argument begs the question: the question is open only on the assumption that good is unanalysable — what the naturalist denies. (2) Synthetic identities are open before science closes them: 'is water H2O?' was once a good question, yet water is H2O — natural properties can be discovered, so openness is compatible with identity. (3) Moore's own analysis ('that which we desire to desire') fares no better — the argument proves too much, undermining every definition including his. (4) At most it shows that 'good' and N differ in meaning, not in reference — and the fallacy charge needs the latter.

Moral non-naturalism and Hume's challenges (Spec 3.2.3)

Moore's non-naturalism (intuitionism). If good is simple and unanalysable yet we make true moral judgements, moral properties must be non-natural: simple properties of the world, reducible to no natural property and not studied by science, known by intuition — direct, self-evident, a priori grasping, like seeing that 2+2=4. Ross extends the view: we intuit plural prima facie duties (fidelity, reparation, gratitude, justice, beneficence, non-maleficence, self-improvement), each holding 'unless overridden'; no formula resolves conflicts — judgement decides which duty is most stringent here. Critiques: non-natural properties are metaphysically queer (Mackie, below); a shared faculty of intuition sits badly with persistent disagreement (abortion, capital punishment); intuitions track upbringing, not truth — slavery once seemed 'self-evidently' acceptable; and 'self-evident to whom?' risks subjectivism about the verdicts.

Hume's fork and the is-ought gap. Hume's fork (Spec 3.1.3, redeployed): substantive knowledge is either relations of ideas (a priori, analytic, necessary) or matters of fact (a posteriori, contingent). Moral judgements are neither: denying 'murder is wrong' involves no contradiction, yet no wrongness is observable in the act. In the Treatise (Book III, Part 1) Hume notes that writers move from 'is' to 'ought' without explanation: no ought from is — a moral conclusion requires a moral premise, so pure facts never entail obligations. Significance: the naturalist can add a value premise ('pleasure is good'), but then naturalism does not derive value from nature — it presupposes it.

Hume's motivation argument. Beliefs are representational and reason alone is inert: 'reason is, and ought only to be, the slave of the passions' — belief cannot by itself move us. But moral judgements are reliably motivating: judging 'I ought to repay the debt' characteristically issues in repayment. So moral judgements are not beliefs — beliefs alone could not motivate us; they involve non-cognitive desire: the classic argument for non-cognitivism. Cognitivist responses: (1) Externalism: motivation needs belief plus desire, and Hume shows only that belief alone is insufficient — the desire can be independent of the belief, present in the normally-constituted and absent in the amoralist who judges 'torturing the child is wrong' and feels nothing; (2) motivational power is no test of truth — we can be moved by false judgements; (3) desire-driven action can be irrational or immoral, so being motivating is no mark of genuine moral judgement.

Moral anti-realism: error theory, emotivism and prescriptivism (Spec 3.2.3)

Mackie's error theory. Ordinary moral thought presupposes objective values: categorical, universally binding 'to-be-pursuedness' woven into the fabric of the world. Mackie (Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong, 1977): that presupposition is an error — moral judgements aim at objective truth but systematically fail, so every positive moral judgement is false. His positive story is projectivism: we project our desires and attitudes onto the world, as colour-perception projects redness onto the tomato. The nihilism charge: if all positive judgements are false, nothing is morally right or wrong — moral practice looks pointless. Responses: error theory concerns truth, not meaning — moral language still makes sense and attitudes are untouched; and Mackie's title signals the constructive turn: morality should be invented and used as an agreed, useful convention. Counter: nobody can live the theory — deliberating, blaming and promising presuppose that 'wrong' sometimes applies; 'morality as invention' re-describes rather than saves morality.

Argument from relativity. Moral codes vary radically and persistently — monogamy, slavery, caste, abortion — and the variation tracks different ways of life, not perceptual conditions. The best explanation of scientific disagreement is misperception of objective facts (hence convergence); the best explanation of moral disagreement is that people approve differently because they live differently; so objective values are a poor hypothesis. Responses: much moral disagreement is about facts (does the foetus feel pain?); convergence on core prohibitions (murder, betrayal of trust) is real; and persistent disagreement in frontier science has never licensed anti-realism about science.

Argument from queerness. Objective values would be metaphysically queer: 'qualities or relations of a very strange sort, utterly different from anything else in the universe', carrying intrinsic to-be-pursuedness — a 'should' built into the world as no natural property is. And epistemologically queer: knowing them would require a special faculty of moral intuition unlike perception, memory or reasoning. Parsimony says they do not exist. Responses: we already accept strange features — consciousness, intentionality — so intrinsically normative properties are less isolated than Mackie suggests; and Moore and Ross accept the faculty, so for them nothing is queer to explain away.

Emotivism (Ayer, Stevenson). Ayer (Language, Truth and Logic, 1946) applies the verification principle: a statement is cognitively meaningful only if analytic or empirically verifiable. Moral judgements are neither — no observation verifies 'murder is wrong' — so they assert nothing and function to express emotions and arouse them: the boo/hurrah theory — 'Stealing is wrong' amounts to 'Stealing — boo!'. Stevenson (Ethics and Language, 1944) refines this: moral terms carry emotive meaning and a persuasive function — they work to redirect the hearer's attitudes; and moral reasoning is possible: we cite empirical beliefs as reasons to change attitudes — persuasion by reasons, not proof. Critiques: (1) the embedded-context problem (Frege–Geach): in 'If stealing is wrong, getting your little brother to steal is wrong', the unasserted antecedent expresses no emotion, yet the argument is plainly valid — unasserted moral claims must retain truth-apt meaning; (2) the disagreement critique: 'Capital punishment is wrong' vs 'Capital punishment is justified' presents itself as genuine contradiction backed by reasons — not two incompatible cheers; (3) the verification principle is self-refuting — itself neither analytic nor verifiable.

Prescriptivism (Hare). Hare (The Language of Morals, 1952): moral judgements are universalisable prescriptions: their meaning is practical — to command and guide action ('do this!') — and their logic is universalisability: whoever prescribes 'ought' for one situation is committed to the same prescription for any relevantly similar situation, including one in which they are the victim. Universalisability makes moral reasoning genuine (prescriptions must be backed by relevant similarities and evidence) and rules out special pleading. Critiques: (1) prescriptions are not truth-apt either, so the embedded-context and disagreement problems return; (2) the fanatic objection: a racist can consistently universalise 'no equal treatment for them' — Hare must insist the fanatic has failed to imagine himself in the victim's position, but universalisability alone does not force that; (3) moral judgements are statements that report, not imperatives that order — 'murder is wrong' can be embedded in arguments and said idly, as commands cannot; (4) moral progress: if judgements only express attitudes, the shift from approval of slavery to its condemnation is a change of fashion, not the discovery of a truth — yet 'progress' is how we describe it.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: 'Moral judgements do not state facts but express emotions.' Evaluate this claim. (25 marks)
Mark-scheme logic: AO1 required: define the axes — cognitivism (moral judgements state propositions and are truth-apt; naturalism: Bentham's good = pleasure; non-naturalism: Moore's simple non-natural property known by intuition) vs non-cognitivism (moral judgements express non-cognitive states, not truth-apt); explain emotivism — Ayer's verification principle (moral judgements neither analytic nor empirically verifiable, so they state nothing) and the boo/hurrah analysis, plus Stevenson's emotive meaning and persuasive function. AO2 required — argue with intent, e.g. FOR the claim: (1) Hume's motivation argument — beliefs alone are inert but moral judgements reliably motivate, so they are not pure beliefs but expressions of attitude; (2) Mackie's argument from relativity — persistent cross-cultural disagreement is best explained by different ways of life, not by misperception of mind-independent facts; (3) the verificationist point that no observation confirms 'stealing is wrong'. Consider the strongest objection AGAINST: the embedded-context (Frege–Geach) problem — 'if stealing is wrong, getting your little brother to steal is wrong' is valid, yet the unasserted antecedent expresses no emotion, so moral claims must retain truth-apt meaning; and moral disagreement is genuine contradiction supported by reasons on both sides, not a clash of cheers — Stevenson's 'persuasion' analysis re-describes rather than explains it. Respond: sophisticated non-cognitivism (Hare) treats moral judgements as universalisable prescriptions, allowing reasoning and disagreement within attitudes. Counter-response: prescriptions are not truth-apt either, and the fanatic who universalises consistently shows non-cognitivism cannot capture moral commitment as a stance towards facts. Weigh up and conclude consistently (e.g. the claim overstates: motivation and relativity support expressivism, but the logic of embedded contexts and the reason-giving character of moral disagreement show moral judgements are more than emotional expression — if cognitivism falls, error theory preserves the fact-stating purport better than emotivism).

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Explain Moore's open question argument and the naturalistic fallacy. (5 marks)
Answer: AO1 only — no evaluation. Moore (Principia Ethica, 1903): 'good' is a simple, unanalysable, non-natural property — definable only by pointing to instances, like 'yellow'. Any naturalist definition N (pleasure, what is desired, evolutionary fitness) leaves the question 'but is N good?' open: significant and not self-answering, unlike 'is a bachelor unmarried?'. If good really meant N, 'N is good' would be a trivial tautology; since 'pleasure is good' remains a substantive question (some pleasures seem bad), good cannot be identical with any natural property. Defining good in terms of natural properties therefore commits the naturalistic fallacy — deriving value from fact — and the argument defeats all reductive metaethical theories.
Explain Hume's motivation argument and one cognitivist response. (12 marks)
Answer: AO1 only. Set out the premises: (1) beliefs are representational — they picture how the world is — and reason operating on them alone is inert: it cannot by itself produce action ('reason is, and ought only to be, the slave of the passions'); (2) moral judgements are practical: judging 'I ought to repay this debt' characteristically motivates the repayment without further reasoning. Conclusion: moral judgements are not beliefs; beliefs alone could not motivate us — so moral judgement involves, or is an expression of, non-cognitive desire, and cognitivism is false. Explain one response — externalist cognitivism: motivation requires belief plus desire, but the argument shows only that belief alone is insufficient, not that the desire must be part of the moral judgement; the belief that the debt is due motivates the normally-constituted agent who additionally cares, and the amoralist (who judges 'torturing the child is wrong' and feels nothing) shows judgement and motivation can come apart. State the conclusion: the argument at most supports a weaker internalist claim, so cognitivism is not refuted.
Explain Mackie's argument from queerness. (5 marks)
Answer: AO1 only. Mackie (Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong, 1977): if objective values existed they would be 'queer' in two ways. Metaphysically: they would be 'qualities or relations of a very strange sort, utterly different from anything else in the universe' — intrinsically prescriptive, carrying to-be-pursuedness and not-to-be-brokenness built into the world, unlike any natural property. Epistemologically: knowing them would require a special faculty of moral intuition or perception unlike any other source of knowledge. Since such entities and faculties fit nowhere in our otherwise scientific picture of the world, the best explanation is that they do not exist — so the objective presupposition of ordinary moral thought is an error, and all positive moral judgements are false (error theory).
Explain Hare's claim that moral judgements are universalisable prescriptions. (5 marks)
Answer: AO1 only. Hare (The Language of Morals, 1952): moral judgements are prescriptions — their primary meaning is to command or guide action ('do this!'), not to state facts — and they are universalisable: whoever prescribes that anyone in circumstances C ought to be treated G is committed to prescribing the same for anyone relevantly similar to C, including themselves in reversed positions. 'You ought not to lie' thus entails an imperative applying to all relevantly similar cases. Illustrate: universalisability makes moral reasoning genuine — a prescription must be supported by citing relevant similarities and evidence, and it blocks special pleading: I cannot prescribe leniency for myself while prescribing punishment for others in the same situation.
'Emotivism cannot account for how we use moral language.' Evaluate this claim. (25 marks)
Answer: AO1 required: set out emotivism — Ayer's verification principle (statements meaningful only if analytic or empirically verifiable; moral judgements are neither, so they express and evoke emotion: 'stealing is wrong' amounts to 'stealing — boo!'), Stevenson's emotive meaning, persuasive definitions and reasoning as giving reasons to redirect attitudes; and the contrast with cognitivist theories (naturalism — Bentham; non-naturalism — Moore, Ross; error theory — Mackie). AO2 — argue with intent, e.g. FOR the claim: (1) the embedded-context (Frege–Geach) problem — in 'if stealing is wrong, getting your little brother to steal is wrong', the antecedent expresses no emotion yet the inference is valid, so unasserted moral claims must have truth-apt meaning, which emotivism cannot supply; (2) the disagreement critique — moral argument is genuine contradiction backed by reasons, not incompatible cheering, and Stevenson's 'persuasion' analysis re-describes rather than explains it. Consider the strongest objection AGAINST the claim: Stevenson's account does allow moral reasoning — we cite facts about consequences as reasons to change attitudes, and much ordinary moral exchange is exactly attitude-shaping, which the boo/hurrah label unfairly caricatures. Respond: reasoning within emotivism is only means-end reasoning from shared attitudes, so it cannot explain why the parties regard themselves as disagreeing about how things are. Counter-response: prescriptivism (Hare) repairs the logic via universalisable prescriptions but still refuses truth-aptness, and the fanatic who universalises consistently shows non-cognitivism cannot block immoral commitment; if anti-realism is kept at all, error theory — which preserves the fact-stating purport of moral language — accounts for its use better. Weigh up and conclude consistently (e.g. the claim succeeds in its strong form: emotivism explains the expressive and persuasive uses of moral language but cannot account for reasoning, disagreement and progress without abandoning the boo/hurrah analysis).
