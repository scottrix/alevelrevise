PH1: Epistemology, Moral Philosophy and Metaphysics of Mind

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level All Boards (AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC, CCEA) AQA

Tripartite definition of knowledge (JTB), Gettier problems, Utilitarianism, Kantian deontology, Substance dualism vs Physicalism.

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Justified True Belief (JTB): The classical tripartite definition of propositional knowledge: S knows that p iff p is true, S believes that p, and S is justified in believing p.
Substance Dualism: Descartes' metaphysical thesis that mind and body are two distinct fundamental substances: thinking non-extended res cogitans and extended physical res extensa.
Epistemic Luck: The feature of Gettier cases where a belief's truth is coincidentally connected to its justification; most solutions (e.g. Nozick's reliabilism, Zagzebski's virtue epistemology) try to exclude luck from knowledge.
Empiricism vs Rationalism: Locke's empiricism grounds knowledge in sensory experience (tabula rasa), while Descartes' rationalism grounds it in a priori reason and innate ideas such as the cogito.
Categorical Imperative: Kant's supreme moral principle: act only on universalisable maxims (universal law formulation) and never treat persons merely as means (humanity formulation).
Mind-Brain Identity Theory: Smart and Place's physicalist thesis that mental states are identical to brain states ('pain is C-fibre firing'), resolving dualism's interaction problem by reducing mind to matter.

🔍 Key Principles & Specification Requirements

  • Gettier counter-examples: show justified true beliefs can arise by epistemic luck (e.g. Smith and Jones coins in pocket), proving JTB is insufficient.
  • Moral theories: Bentham Act Utilitarianism (hedonic calculus), Mill Rule Utilitarianism (higher/lower pleasures), Kant's categorical imperative, Aristotle's virtue ethics.
  • Philosophy of Mind: Dualism (interaction problem), Behaviourism, Mind-Brain Identity Theory, Functionalism.

💡 Worked Example Question

Exam-Style Question

Question:

Explain how Edmund Gettier's counter-examples challenge the Tripartite Definition of Knowledge (JTB).

Model Solution & Mark Scheme:

Gettier constructed scenarios where an agent holds a belief that is both true and justified, yet the truth of the belief is connected to justification only by sheer coincidence/luck (e.g., Smith believes 'The man who will get the job has 10 coins in his pocket', justified regarding Jones, but Smith unexpectedly gets the job and coincidentally also has 10 coins). Because knowledge excludes epistemic luck, JTB is insufficient.

Q3: 'The categorical imperative makes morality too rigid.' Discuss.

Show Model Answer

Answer: Kant's universal law formulation yields inflexible duties: the famous murderer-at-the-door case requires truth-telling even to a would-be killer, and conflicting duties (never lie, never endanger life) cannot both be kept. However, a strong evaluation notes rigidity is arguably a strength — it protects persons from being used as means and avoids the calculating cruelty Bentham's hedonic calculus permits; a justified conclusion may hold that Kantian rigidity better respects moral integrity than consequentialist flexibility.

Q4: Evaluate whether the mind-brain identity theory successfully resolves the interaction problem facing substance dualism.

Show Model Answer

Answer: By identifying mental states with brain states, identity theory dissolves rather than answers Descartes' interaction problem: there is no immaterial substance needing a causal bridge into the extended world, and neuroscience correlations (e.g. pain and C-fibre firing) support the reduction. However, evaluation must note multiple realisability (Putnam): the same mental state can occur in differently structured brains, and phenomenal consciousness (Kripke's 'pain could have existed without C-fibres') resists strict identity; functionalism may therefore supersede identity theory, though it too faces absent qualia objections.
