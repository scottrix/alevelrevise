Moral Philosophy: Utilitarianism, Kant and Aristotelian Virtue Ethics

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA

AQA 7172 Paper 1 Section B: Bentham and Mill's utilitarianism, Kantian deontology and Aristotle's virtue ethics, applied ethics and 25-mark evaluation technique — with full mark-scheme answers.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain and evaluate Bentham's act utilitarianism (hedonism, felicific calculus) and Mill's qualitative, rule-based refinement (Spec 3.2.1).
  • Explain and evaluate Kantian deontology: the good will, duty, the categorical imperative's formulations and clashing-duty issues (Spec 3.2.1).
  • Explain and evaluate Aristotelian virtue ethics: eudaimonia, the function argument, the doctrine of the mean and phronesis (Spec 3.2.1).
  • Apply utilitarianism, Kantian deontology and virtue ethics to stealing, eating animals, simulated killing and telling lies (Spec 3.2.2).
  • Compare the three theories' decision procedures, strengths and weaknesses to form reasoned judgements (AO2).
  • Write 25-mark essays 'arguing with intent' with balanced argument sandwiches and consistent conclusions.

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Eudaimonia: Aristotle's highest human good: flourishing or living well — a complete, final end desired for its own sake, achieved over a whole life by exercising virtue.
Hedonism: The view (Bentham) that pleasure (and absence of pain) is the only thing good in itself; morality maximises pleasure summed over everyone affected.
Felicific (hedonic) calculus: Bentham's decision procedure: measure each option's pleasure by seven criteria — intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity, fecundity, purity and extent.
Higher and lower pleasures: Mill's qualitative distinction: pleasures of the intellect, imagination and moral sentiment are higher in kind than bodily pleasures; competent judges who have experienced both prefer the higher.
Rule utilitarianism: The view that the right act is what conforms to rules whose general acceptance maximises utility — avoiding the act-theory's case-by-case licence for injustice.
Good will (Kant): The only thing good without qualification: a will that acts from duty, out of respect for the moral law, rather than from inclination or for consequences.
Categorical imperative: Kant's supreme principle of morality, binding unconditionally (unlike hypothetical imperatives, which bind only given a desired end).
Universal law formulation: 'Act only according to that maxim through which you can at the same time will that it become a universal law' — test maxims for contradiction in conception (universalised) or in the will.
Humanity formulation: 'Act in such a way that you treat humanity, whether in your own person or in any other, always at the same time as an end, never merely as a means' — persons have absolute, non-negotiable worth.
Kingdom of ends: Kant's third formulation: act as a law-making member of an ideal community of rational agents, each legislating universal laws for all.
Doctrine of the mean: Aristotle's thesis that each virtue is a mean between two vices — excess and deficiency relative to us (courage between rashness and cowardice).
Phronesis: Practical wisdom: the intellectual virtue of perceiving what a situation requires and deliberating well about how to act — what makes the mean hit reliably.

📌 Key Points

Bentham: quantitative hedonism + felicific calculus; Mill: qualitative pleasures + rule utilitarianism + competent-judges 'proof'.
Kant: act from duty via the categorical imperative — universal law, humanity as an end, kingdom of ends; consequences are irrelevant to rightness.
Aristotle: eudaimonia via the function argument; virtues are means between vices, hit by practical wisdom and habituation.
Classic critiques: tyranny of the majority and integrity (utilitarianism); clashing duties and rigidity (Kant); circularity and guidance (Aristotle).
Applied ethics on spec: stealing, eating animals, simulated killing, telling lies — each theory delivers a different verdict.

Utilitarianism: Bentham, Mill and preference (Spec 3.2.1)

Bentham's act utilitarianism. Hedonism: nature has placed mankind under the governance of two sovereign masters, pain and pleasure; pleasure is the only intrinsic good. An act is right iff it produces the greatest happiness for the greatest number. The felicific calculus operationalises this: weigh each option's pleasure/pain by seven criteria — intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity (how soon), fecundity (will it produce more of the same), purity (will it be followed by pain), extent (how many affected). Act utilitarianism assesses each act individually by its consequences.

Mill's refinement. Mill accepts the consequentialist core but answers the doctrine of swine objection (that the theory makes humans no better than pigs) with a qualitative hedonism: pleasures of the intellect, imagination and moral sentiment are higher in kind; the test is the verdict of competent judges who have experienced both — they invariable prefer the higher. His 'proof' that happiness is desirable: the only proof something is desirable is that people actually desire it; everyone desires their own happiness; so general happiness is desirable — a fallacious slide (each person's happiness from my perspective vs everyone's). Mill also moves towards rule utilitarianism: acts are right if they conform to rules (e.g. keep promises, tell the truth) whose general acceptance maximises utility — the rules encode long-run, whole-society consequences.

Preference utilitarianism. The modern variant (Singer): maximise the satisfaction of preferences rather than pleasure — it avoids measuring pleasure and better handles diverse goods, at the cost of counting maladaptive or immoral preferences.

Issues. (1) Tyranny of the majority: aggregating happiness permits sacrificing individuals — punishing an innocent person to stop a riot maximises utility but is unjust. (2) Calculation: consequences are unknowable in advance; the calculus is impractical in real time (Mill's reply: rules summarise millennia of experience — the 'dead-weight' of calculation is already done). (3) Fairness/utility monster (Nozick): a being that gains enormous utility from any resource would justify total sacrifice of others. (4) Integrity (Williams): Jim and the Indians — shooting one hostage to save twenty maximises utility but destroys the agent's integrity; utilitarianism alienates agents from their own projects. (5) Motives/means: it assesses acts by outcomes, so a malicious act with good consequences comes out right. (6) Experience machine (Nozick): if pleasure were all that matters, we should plug in — most would not, suggesting we value contact with reality and authenticity.

Kantian deontology: duty and the categorical imperative (Spec 3.2.1)

The good will and duty. Kant: nothing is good without qualification except a good will — talents, wealth and even happiness can be used for evil. An act has moral worth only when done from duty (out of respect for the law), not merely in accordance with duty (the shopkeeper who is honest only because honesty profits). Hypothetical imperatives ('if you want X, do Y') bind conditionally and are hypothetical counsel of skill or prudence; morality needs an unconditional command: the categorical imperative.

Three formulations. (1) Universal law: act only on maxims you can will as universal laws. Kant's test case, the false promise: the maxim 'make a promise I do not intend to keep to escape difficulty' universalised destroys the institution of promising (if everyone lied, no one would believe promises — contradiction in conception); the maxim 'never help others' cannot be consistently willed (contradiction in the will — we all sometimes need help). (2) Humanity as an end: never treat persons merely as means — the false promiser uses the deceived person merely as a means; persons have dignity (priceless worth) rather than price. (3) Kingdom of ends: act as a legislating member of a community of rational agents — the formulations are claimed to be equivalent expressions of one supreme principle.

Issues. (1) Clashing duties (Constant's objection, the murderer at the door): 'never lie' and 'do not facilitate murder' cannot both be kept; Kant's own reply — lying to the murderer is wrong, and one may be truthful while the outcome harms — strikes most as unacceptable; a weaker reply: the duties do not genuinely clash if formulated precisely (the maxim against lying is about 'unavoidable falsehood', and lying to facilitate murder fails the humanity formulation). (2) Rigidity: universalisability yields inflexible rules insensitive to consequences. (3) Consequences ignored: an act's rightness is independent of outcomes — intuitively wrong when stakes are catastrophic (lying to save a life). (4) Universalisation is slippery: many wicked maxims universalise without contradiction if carefully worded ('always lie when it benefits me' may not contradict itself in conception). (5) Motives ignored: Kant counts only duty-motivated acts as morally worthy, ignoring love, friendship and kindness as motives with moral value. (6) Morality as hypothetical imperatives (Foot): Kant claims the moral law binds unconditionally, but obeying it itself requires a desired end (to be rational, to avoid blame, to live with oneself) — so morality is, after all, a system of hypothetical imperatives. (7) Animals: Kant holds rational nature is the source of worth, so animals have only indirect worth — mistreating them is wrong because it damages our humanity; contrast utilitarianism (sentience suffices) and virtue ethics (compassion).

Aristotelian virtue ethics: eudaimonia and the mean (Spec 3.2.1)

Eudaimonia and the function argument. The highest good is eudaimonia — flourishing, living well and doing well: complete (desired for its own sake, not as a means), final and self-sufficient. The function (ergon) argument: the good of anything with a function lies in performing that function well; the human function is activity of the soul in accordance with reason (plants have nutrition, animals perception — what is distinctive to humans is reason); so the human good is activity of the soul in accordance with virtue — and, for a complete life, in accordance with the best virtue (contemplation, Aristotle adds). Critique: the inference from 'distinctive capacity' to 'function' to 'good' is contested — a capacity for wickedness is equally distinctive of humans, and a solitary, rational but friendless life hardly looks like flourishing; Aristotle's own account of friendship answers the second point.

Virtues and the mean. A virtue is a stable character trait (a hexis) to feel, choose and act well. Doctrine of the mean: each virtue lies between two vices, excess and deficiency, relative to us — courage between rashness and cowardice, generosity between prodigality and meanness, proper pride between vanity and false modesty. Some actions (murder, adultery) admit no mean — they are wrong in any degree. Phronesis (practical wisdom) is the intellectual virtue that perceives the particulars of a situation and deliberates to the mean; it unifies the virtues (one cannot have one fully without the others). Virtues are acquired by habituation: we become just by doing just acts, first with instruction and correction, until feeling and action align. The skill analogy: virtues are like skills — acquired by practice, exercised with judgement, assessed by how well they are done — but unlike skills they involve feelings: the generous person enjoys giving. Voluntariness: we are responsible for voluntary actions and for our characters, since habits are built from choices; involuntary actions (from force or ignorance) are not blameworthy, and non-voluntary actions (done in ignorance the agent could not avoid) attract pity rather than blame.

Issues. (1) Circularity (Hursthouse): the right act is what the virtuous person would do; the virtuous person is who does the right acts — a vicious circle? Reply: it is an explanatory circle not a definitional one — virtue and right action are mutually illuminating, as health and healthy organs are. (2) Guidance: 'do what the practically wise person would do' seems to give no action-guidance. Reply: the mean, the virtuous examples and phronesis give substantial guidance — but less mechanical than a calculus or a test. (3) Relativism: 'relative to us' invites cultural relativism. Reply: relativism is about how the mean applies, not whether virtue is objective. (4) Applicability/conflict: what happens when two virtues pull apart (honesty and kindness)? (5) Must a trait contribute to eudaimonia to be a virtue? — if virtues are defined by what makes the individual flourish, the good for the individual and the moral good may come apart; Aristotle answers that the practically wise person sees that a flourishing life is also the morally good one. Note also the eudaimonia–pleasure relationship: pleasure completes virtuous activity 'as the bloom of youth' — eudaimonia is not pleasure alone, but the virtuous life is pleasant to the virtuous person.

Comparing the theories and applied ethics (Spec 3.2.2)

Three decision procedures. Utilitarianism: maximise aggregate welfare (consequentialist, agent-neutral). Kant: test maxims against the categorical imperative (deontological, intention-focused). Aristotle: ask what the person of practical wisdom would do (aretaic, character-focused). They disagree structurally: on what makes an act right (outcomes vs universalisable intention vs virtuous character), on whether consequences matter, and on partiality (utilitarianism counts everyone equally; Aristotle begins from relationships and communities; Kant from rational agents).

Why the comparison matters for AO2. 25-markers often ask you to argue that one theory is 'the best' or 'most convincing'. Strong answers do not merely list — they weigh structural features: utilitarianism's fairness problem vs its moral reach; Kant's rigidity vs its protection of persons from being used as means; Aristotle's richer moral psychology vs its weaker action-guidance. Then conclude consistently.

Exam technique: AQA 7172 moral philosophy (Paper 1, Section B)

Where it lands. Moral philosophy (Specs 3.2.1–3.2.5) is Paper 1 Section B: five questions worth 3, 5, 5, 12 and 25 marks. Short questions are pure AO1; the 25-marker (5 AO1 + 20 AO2) is a quoted claim + 'Evaluate this claim'. Metaethics (Spec 3.2.4) has its own revision page — cross-reference it.

25-mark essay structure. Introduction: define the theory/claim, state your thesis. Argument for your view; best objection; response. Argument against; weak response; strong response. Consistent conclusion, no new ideas. Mark scheme top band (21–25) rewards 'crucial arguments clearly identified against less crucial ones' — e.g. in a utilitarianism essay, the tyranny-of-the-majority/fairness issue is crucial; the calculation issue is less so and can be handled in a line with Mill's rule-utilitarian reply.

Common pitfalls and examiner tips (click to expand)
  • Act vs rule utilitarianism: act assesses each act by its consequences; rule assesses rules by the consequences of general acceptance. Mill is best read as moving from act to rule; do not attribute the felicific calculus to Mill.
  • From duty vs in accordance with duty: only the former has moral worth (Kant's shopkeeper). Candidates routinely miss this distinction.
  • Mill's 'proof' is fallacious: know the slide from 'each desires their own happiness' to 'the general happiness is desirable' — an A* answer flags it even while defending utilitarianism.
  • The mean is relative to us: not 'moderation in all things' — courage is not 'a bit of rashness plus a bit of cowardice', and some acts admit no mean.
  • Use applied ethics in evaluation: cases (the murderer at the door, Jim and the Indians, simulated killing) earn AO2 only when used to test a theory, not as decoration.
  • Timing: ~35–40 minutes per 25-marker, ~15 for the 12-marker; with 10 questions in 3 hours, move on when a short answer is complete — extra words on 3/5-markers lose marks.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: 'Utilitarianism is a better account of morality than Kantian ethics.' Evaluate this claim. (25 marks)
Mark-scheme logic: AO1 required: set out Bentham's act utilitarianism (hedonism, felicific calculus) and Mill's refinement (higher/lower pleasures, rule utilitarianism); set out Kantian ethics (good will, duty vs inclination, universal law and humanity formulations, with the false-promise example). AO2 — argue with intent, e.g. FOR utilitarianism: (1) moral reach: sentience, not rational nature, grounds moral status (Bentham), so utilitarianism captures duties to animals and future people that Kant cannot; (2) flexibility: consequences matter — refusing to lie to the murderer at the door (Kant/Constant) is intuitively unacceptable. Consider the strongest objection AGAINST: aggregation permits injustice — punishing the innocent to stop a riot (tyranny of the majority) and Jim and the Indians (Williams' integrity) show utilitarianism alienates agents from morality; respond: rule utilitarianism forbids such acts by general-utility rules. Counter-response: extreme scenarios (overruling a rule would save everyone) reveal rule utilitarianism collapsing back into act utilitarianism. Weigh up: utilitarianism's reach and flexibility vs Kant's absolute protection of persons; conclude consistently (either direction is defensible if argued with intent — e.g. Kantian ethics better respects dignity, but utilitarianism better handles real cases).

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Explain Bentham's felicific calculus. (5 marks)
Answer: AO1 only. Bentham's felicific calculus measures the pleasure or pain an act produces using seven criteria: intensity (how strong), duration (how long), certainty (how likely), propinquity (how soon), fecundity (whether it will produce further pleasures of the same kind), purity (whether it will be followed by pains) and extent (how many people are affected). The act producing the greatest net pleasure on these criteria — for everyone affected — is the right act. Illustrate with a pair of options (e.g. two uses of a charity budget) weighed across the criteria.
Explain Kant's universal law formulation of the categorical imperative and how it applies to false promising. (12 marks)
Answer: AO1 only. Define the categorical imperative (unconditional command, unlike hypothetical imperatives which bind only given a desired end). State the universal law formulation: act only according to that maxim through which you can at the same time will that it become a universal law. Explain the test: a maxim is impermissible if universalising it produces a contradiction in conception (the practice it relies on is destroyed) or in the will (what you must will conflicts with what you cannot rationally will). Apply: the maxim 'to escape difficulty, I will make a false promise'; if everyone made false promises when convenient, no one would believe promises — promising as an institution is destroyed, so the maxim cannot even be conceived as a universal law (contradiction in conception); the false promiser also treats the deceived person merely as a means (humanity formulation). Conclusion: false promising is impermissible for Kant, regardless of consequences.
Explain Aristotle's function argument. (5 marks)
Answer: AO1 only. Aristotle: the good of anything with a function (ergon) consists in performing that function well — a harpist's good is playing the harp well. The human function must be some distinctive capacity: mere life belongs to plants (nutrition) and perception to animals; what is distinctive of humans is activity of the soul in accordance with reason. Conclusion: the human good is activity of the soul in accordance with virtue — and if there is more than one virtue, in accordance with the best and most complete (for a complete life).
Explain Mill's distinction between higher and lower pleasures. (5 marks)
Answer: AO1 only. Mill accepts hedonism but holds pleasures differ in quality, not just quantity: pleasures of the intellect, imagination and moral sentiment are higher in kind than those of mere sensation (bodily pleasures). The test is the verdict of competent judges — those who have experienced both kinds: they give a marked preference to the higher, and would not resign it for any quantity of the lower ('it is better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied'). The distinction answers the doctrine of swine objection: human happiness involves the higher pleasures, so utilitarianism does not reduce human ends to swinish ones.
'Kantian ethics cannot be applied in practice.' Evaluate this claim. (25 marks)
Answer: AO1 required: set out Kantian ethics — the good will, duty vs inclination, the categorical imperative's universal law formulation (with the false-promise test), the humanity formulation and the kingdom of ends. AO2 — argue with intent, e.g. FOR the claim: (1) clashing duties: the murderer at the door (Constant) — 'never lie' and 'do not facilitate murder' cannot both be kept; Kant's reply (truthfulness even to the murderer) shows the theory cannot be lived with; (2) universalisation is slippery: wicked maxims can be worded so as to universalise without contradiction, so the test under-determines action. Consider the strongest objection AGAINST the claim: precision defeats the clash — the maxim against lying concerns unavoidable falsehood, and lying to facilitate murder fails the humanity formulation independently; and the theory gives clear, mechanical guidance (universalise the maxim; check for use as a mere means) where virtue ethics gives none. Counter-response: mechanical clarity is bought with rigidity — consequences are excluded, and catastrophic stakes (lying to save a life) make the theory unacceptable in practice. Weigh up and conclude consistently (e.g. the claim is too strong: Kantian ethics is applicable, but its rigidity in life-and-death cases remains its decisive cost).
