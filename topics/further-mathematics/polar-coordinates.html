Polar Coordinates

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC/Eduqas, CCEA

Polar coordinates for Edexcel 9FM0 Core Pure 2 and OCR MEI: converting between polar and cartesian form, sketching cardioids, limacons and rose curves, and finding enclosed areas with the half r-squared integral - revision with worked examples and mark-scheme logic.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Convert confidently between cartesian and polar coordinates, finding r and the angle theta with quadrant care, and express polar equations in cartesian form (AO1) - Edexcel 9FM0 Core Pure 2; OCR MEI H645 Further Pure
  • Sketch the standard polar curves - circles, cardioids, limacons (with and without inner loops) and rose curves - marking maxima, pole crossings and symmetry (AO2)
  • Determine the tangent at the pole as the half-line theta = theta0 where r = 0, and identify where a curve passes through the pole (AO2)
  • Calculate areas using A = (1/2) integral of r^2 d theta, including symmetric curves, single petals and regions between two curves, with justified limits (AO2/AO3)
  • Derive and use the polar gradient dy/dx = (r' sin theta + r cos theta)/(r' cos theta - r sin theta) to find tangents at a given point (AO1/AO2)

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Pole: The origin O of the polar coordinate system; distances are measured from it.
Initial line: The positive x-axis; the angle theta is measured anticlockwise from it, in radians.
Polar coordinates: The pair (r, theta): r is the distance from the pole, theta the anticlockwise angle from the initial line. Not unique: (r, theta) = (r, theta + 2pi) = (-r, theta + pi).
Negative r convention: The point (-r, theta) is plotted at (r, theta + pi), on the opposite ray. Curves with negative r values over some interval trace an inner loop.
Cardioid: r = a(1 + cos theta) - cusp at the pole where theta = pi, maximum r = 2a on the initial line; the sin versions point up or down, and r = a(1 - cos theta) points left.
Limacon: r = a + b cos theta. If a >= b there is no inner loop (dimple or oval); if a < b there is an inner loop, entered where r = 0 at cos theta = -a/b.
Rose curve: r = a cos n theta (or r = a sin n theta): n odd gives n petals, n even gives 2n petals; petal tips are at distance a from the pole.
Tangent at the pole: If r = 0 at theta = theta0, the tangent to the curve at the pole is the half-line theta = theta0.

📌 Key Points

x = r cos theta, y = r sin theta; r^2 = x^2 + y^2; tan theta = y/x - fix the quadrant from the signs of x and y before quoting theta.
Area: A = (1/2) integral of r^2 d theta between the correct limits - the formula is given in the Edexcel booklet, the limits are not.
Between two curves: A = (1/2) integral of (r_outer^2 - r_inner^2) d theta over the interval where the outer curve encloses the region.
Rose r = a cos n theta: n petals if n is odd, 2n petals if n is even.
The polar gradient formula is NOT printed in the booklet - derive it from x = r cos theta, y = r sin theta: dy/dx = (r' sin theta + r cos theta)/(r' cos theta - r sin theta).

Polar Coordinates and Conversion to Cartesian Form

The polar system. A point P is fixed by two numbers: r, its distance from the origin O (called the pole), and theta, the angle measured anticlockwise from the positive x-axis, the initial line. The link with cartesian coordinates is x = r cos theta, y = r sin theta; going the other way, r2 = x2 + y2 and tan theta = y/x. Because tan theta fixes theta only up to a multiple of pi, always settle the quadrant from the signs of x and y before quoting a final angle, and give theta in the range the question specifies (usually -pi < theta <= pi or 0 <= theta < 2pi), in radians.

Worked example (cartesian to polar). Express (-1, root3) in polar form. r = root(1 + 3) = 2. The point (-1, root3) is in the second quadrant, and tan theta = -root3 has reference angle pi/3, so theta = pi - pi/3 = 2pi/3. Hence (-1, root3) = (2, 2pi/3). Check: 2cos(2pi/3) = -1 and 2sin(2pi/3) = root3 ✓. Quoting theta = pi/3 (the raw arctan) loses the accuracy mark - this quadrant check is the single most examined detail in conversions.

Non-uniqueness and negative r. Polar coordinates are not unique: (2, 2pi/3) is the same point as (2, -4pi/3) and, using a negative radius, (-2, -pi/3). The convention for negative r is to plot the point on the opposite ray: (-r, theta) means (r, theta + pi). This matters when sketching, because limacons take negative r values over some theta-intervals and those points trace an inner loop.

Polar equations to cartesian. Multiply through by a suitable power of r so that r cos theta and r sin theta appear, then substitute. Worked example: r = 4cos theta becomes r2 = 4r cos theta, i.e. x2 + y2 = 4x, so (x - 2)2 + y2 = 4 - a circle of centre (2, 0) and radius 2. Two substitutions do all the work: r cos theta to x, r sin theta to y, r2 to x2 + y2. If an equation involves only r (for example r = 3), squaring gives x2 + y2 = 9 with no loss here, but in general squaring can introduce spurious points, so convert with the smallest multiplication that works.

Exam technique

Keep radians throughout and leave exact surds and pi in answers unless a decimal is demanded. Sketch the point roughly before working out theta: the sketch settles the quadrant instantly and protects you from the arctan trap. If a question asks for coordinates in the range 0 <= theta < 2pi, convert whatever you get into that range - marks are attached to the specified range, not to any valid description. Points on the vertical axis have theta = pi/2 or 3pi/2 directly, since tan theta = y/x cannot be formed when x = 0.

Sketching Polar Curves: Cardioids, Limacons and Roses

A reliable sketching routine. For any polar equation r = f(theta), build a table of r at standard angles (0, pi/6, pi/4, pi/3, pi/2, and onwards), then exploit symmetry: if only cos theta appears the curve is symmetric about the initial line (theta to -theta leaves r unchanged); if only sin theta appears it is symmetric about the line theta = pi/2. Mark three things on every sketch: the maximum value of r and where it occurs, every point where the curve passes through the pole (solve f(theta) = 0), and the tangent at the pole - when r = 0 at theta = theta0, the tangent there is the half-line theta = theta0.

The standard families. These shapes are examinable by name:

EquationCurve
r = 2a cos thetaCircle, centre (a, 0), radius a (r = 2a sin theta: centre (0, a))
r = a(1 + cos theta)Cardioid, cusp at the pole, max r = 2a on the initial line; -cos points left, +sin points up
r = a + b cos thetaLimacon; a >= b: no inner loop (dimple or oval); a < b: inner loop, pole where cos theta = -a/b
r = a cos n thetaRose; n odd gives n petals, n even gives 2n petals; petal tips at distance a

Worked example (rose). Sketch r = 2cos 3theta. The curve passes through the pole when 3theta = ±pi/2, i.e. theta = ±pi/6, and again every half-turn. One petal is traced for -pi/6 <= theta <= pi/6, centred on the initial line with tip at (2, 0). Since cos 3(theta + 2pi/3) = cos 3theta, the pattern repeats every 2pi/3, giving three petals with tips at (2, 0), (2, 2pi/3) and (2, 4pi/3). Odd n always yields n petals; r = 2cos 4theta would give eight.

Worked example (limacon). Sketch r = 1 + 2cos theta. Here a = 1 < b = 2, so there is an inner loop. The pole is reached when cos theta = -1/2, at theta = 2pi/3 and 4pi/3; between these angles r is negative, tracing the small inner loop on the left. Maximum r = 3 at theta = 0. This exact question type - identify the loop, find the pole angles, quote the maximum - appears regularly, and the pole angles also become the limits in loop-area questions (see Section 3).

Exam technique

Sketch marks are typically B1 for overall shape, B1 for symmetry, B1 for pole behaviour and B1 for labelled key points such as (2a, 0) on a cardioid. Always label the initial line and mark r values on the axes. A table of values with the r = 0 rows included is acceptable working and banks partial credit even if the final sketch wobbles.

Areas Enclosed by Polar Curves and Tangent Lines

The area formula. The area swept out by the radius vector from theta = alpha to theta = beta is A = (1/2) integral of r2 d theta - given in the Edexcel 9FM0 formula booklet, but you must choose the correct limits yourself. Limits come from the region in the question, the pole (r = 0), or intersections with another curve (equate the two r expressions). For curves symmetric about the initial line, compute half and double. For a region between two curves use A = (1/2) integral of (r_outer2 - r_inner2) d theta over the interval where the outer curve encloses the region.

Worked example (full cardioid). Find the area enclosed by r = a(1 + cos theta). By symmetry about the initial line: A = 2 × (1/2) integral from 0 to pi of a2(1 + cos theta)2 d theta = a2 integral of (1 + 2cos theta + cos2 theta) d theta. Use cos2 theta = (1 + cos 2theta)/2: the integral evaluates to a2[theta + 2sin theta + theta/2 + sin 2theta/4] from 0 to pi = a2(pi + pi/2) = 3pi a2/2. Exact answers in terms of pi are expected.

Worked example (between two curves). Region inside r = 2(1 + cos theta) but outside the circle r = 2: intersections where 2(1 + cos theta) = 2, i.e. cos theta = 0, so theta = ±pi/2. Then A = (1/2) integral from -pi/2 to pi/2 of [4(1 + cos theta)2 - 4] d theta = (1/2) integral of (8cos theta + 4cos2 theta) d theta = (1/2)[8sin theta + 2theta + sin 2theta] from -pi/2 to pi/2 = (1/2)(16 + 2pi) = 8 + pi. The cos theta and sin 2theta terms are antisymmetric and vanish across the symmetric interval - a built-in accuracy check.

Tangents to polar curves. The polar gradient formula is not printed in the booklet - derive it: x = r cos theta and y = r sin theta, so dy/dx = (dy/d theta)/(dx/d theta) = (r' sin theta + r cos theta)/(r' cos theta - r sin theta), where r' = dr/d theta. Worked example: for r = a(1 + cos theta) at theta = 0: r' = -a sin theta, so dx/d theta = -a sin theta cos theta - a(1 + cos theta) sin theta = 0, while dy/d theta = -a sin2 theta + a(1 + cos theta) cos theta = 2a at theta = 0. Zero denominator with non-zero numerator: the tangent is vertical, the line x = 2a, at the furthest point (2a, 0). Also remember the tangent at the pole: r = 0 at theta = theta0 gives the line theta = theta0.

Exam technique

State the limits and justify them (from r = 0 or from an intersection) - method marks hang on the limits, not just the integration. When subtracting r2 values, put the outer curve first, and never integrate r instead of r2. For single petals, find the two angles where r = 0 and integrate between them. If both dx/d theta and dy/d theta are zero at a point, that is the tangent-at-the-pole case - use the half-line rule.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: The curve C has polar equation r = 2(1 + cos theta), -pi < theta <= pi, and the circle D has polar equation r = 2. (a) Find the polar coordinates of the points where C meets D. (2 marks) (b) The region R lies inside C but outside D. Find the exact area of R. (6 marks)
Mark-scheme logic: (a) M1: solve 2(1 + cos theta) = 2, giving cos theta = 0 and theta = ±pi/2. A1: the points are (2, pi/2) and (2, -pi/2) - cartesian (0, 2) and (0, -2) if converted. (b) M1: area = (1/2) integral from -pi/2 to pi/2 of (r_C^2 - r_D^2) d theta. M1: r_C^2 - r_D^2 = 4(1 + cos theta)^2 - 4 = 8cos theta + 4cos^2 theta. M1: use cos^2 theta = (1 + cos 2theta)/2. M1: integrate to 8sin theta + 2theta + sin 2theta. A1: evaluate from -pi/2 to pi/2: (8 + pi) - (-8 - pi) = 16 + 2pi. A1: area = (1/2)(16 + 2pi) = 8 + pi.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

The point P has cartesian coordinates (-3, 3) and the point Q has polar coordinates (4, 2pi/3). Find polar coordinates of P (with the principal angle) and cartesian coordinates of Q. (4 marks)
Answer: P: r = root(9 + 9) = 3 root2. The point (-3, 3) is in the second quadrant, tan theta = -1 gives reference angle pi/4, so theta = pi - pi/4 = 3pi/4: P = (3 root2, 3pi/4). Q: x = 4cos(2pi/3) = -2 and y = 4sin(2pi/3) = 2 root3, so Q = (-2, 2 root3).
Convert the polar equation r = 4 sin theta into cartesian form and state the centre and radius of the curve. (3 marks)
Answer: Multiply by r: r^2 = 4r sin theta. Substitute r^2 = x^2 + y^2 and r sin theta = y: x^2 + y^2 = 4y, so x^2 + (y - 2)^2 = 4. This is a circle of centre (0, 2) and radius 2. Check with the area formula: (1/2) integral from 0 to pi of 16 sin^2 theta d theta = 4 pi, matching pi × 2^2 ✓.
Sketch the curve with polar equation r = 1 + 2 cos theta, finding the values of theta at which it passes through the pole and describing its key features. (4 marks)
Answer: The pole is reached where 1 + 2cos theta = 0, i.e. cos theta = -1/2, giving theta = 2pi/3 and theta = 4pi/3. Since a = 1 < b = 2, the curve is a limacon with an inner loop: r is negative for 2pi/3 < theta < 4pi/3, tracing a small loop on the left, with a larger loop passing through (3, 0). The curve is symmetric about the initial line, maximum r = 3 at theta = 0, and the tangents at the pole are the half-lines theta = 2pi/3 and theta = 4pi/3.
Find the exact area of one petal of the rose curve r = 2 cos 3 theta. (5 marks)
Answer: One petal is traced between the consecutive zeros r = 0: 3 theta = ±pi/2, so theta = ±pi/6. Area = (1/2) integral from -pi/6 to pi/6 of 4 cos^2 3theta d theta = 2 × integral of cos^2 3theta d theta. Substitute u = 3 theta, d theta = du/3, limits u = ±pi/2: area = (2/3) × integral from -pi/2 to pi/2 of cos^2 u du. Using cos^2 u = (1 + cos 2u)/2, this is (2/3)[u/2 + sin 2u/4] from -pi/2 to pi/2 = (2/3)(pi/2) = pi/3. Sanity check: three petals give a total area of pi ✓.
For the curve r = 2 cos theta, find dy/dx at the point where theta = pi/6. (4 marks)
Answer: At theta = pi/6: r = 2cos(pi/6) = root3 and dr/d theta = -2 sin(pi/6) = -1. Using dy/dx = (r' sin theta + r cos theta)/(r' cos theta - r sin theta): numerator = (-1)(1/2) + (root3)(root3/2) = -1/2 + 3/2 = 1; denominator = (-1)(root3/2) - (root3)(1/2) = -root3. So dy/dx = -1/root3. Check: the curve is the circle (x - 1)^2 + y^2 = 1; at (3/2, root3/2) the radius from the centre has gradient root3, so the tangent has gradient -1/root3 ✓.
