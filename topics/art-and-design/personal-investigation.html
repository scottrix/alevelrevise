The Personal Investigation (Component 1)

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC/Eduqas

AQA 7202/C Personal investigation revision: practical portfolio plus the 1,000-3,000 word critical essay, AO1-AO4 evidence requirements, structure and moderation. Coursework-based qualification - Component 1 is 60% of the A-level and there is no written exam paper.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • State the structure and mark demands of AQA Component 1 (7202/C): a practical investigation plus a 1,000-3,000 word written element, 96 marks, 60% of the A-level, no time limit, marked by the centre and moderated by AQA.
  • Identify a focused, independently chosen starting point (an idea, issue, concept or theme) and shape it into an investigation that sustains a line of reasoning from first starting point to final realisation.
  • Plan portfolio evidence against AO1-AO4, knowing each AO carries 24 of the 96 marks and that the component is judged as one integrated whole across practical and written material.
  • Structure the written element so it clarifies the focus, demonstrates critical understanding of contextual sources, substantiates decisions and connects visual and written elements.
  • Compile a bibliography identifying contextual references and acknowledge every source that is not your own.
  • Recognise the difference between AQA (7202/C, 96 marks), OCR (Component 01, 120 marks) and Pearson Edexcel (9AD0/01) Personal investigation requirements.

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Personal investigation: Component 1 of AQA A-level Art and Design (7202/C): a practical investigation into an idea, issue, concept or theme, supported by written material, leading to a finished outcome or a series of related finished outcomes; 96 marks, 60% of the A-level.
Non-exam assessment (NEA): Work set and marked in the centre and moderated by the board rather than sat in a written exam; both AQA Art and Design components are NEA, so the qualification is coursework-based.
Sustained line of reasoning: The coherent development of the investigation from an initial starting point through research, experimentation and refinement to a final realisation, where each piece of work visibly leads to the next.
Related finished outcomes: A set of final works produced as one resolved body - for example a triptych, a print series or an installation - rather than one isolated piece.
Written element: The 1,000-3,000 word extended response of continuous prose (a single passage, or linked shorter passages incorporated within the practical work) that documents the investigation and confirms understanding of creative decisions.
Critical/contextual sources: The artists, movements, texts, exhibitions and other research that inform the investigation; the spec requires the work to be informed by an aspect of contemporary or past practice of artists, photographers, designers or craftspeople.
Moderation: AQA's visit to the centre (normally in June) to check that centre marking of Component 1 is accurate and consistent; moderated marks can move up or down.
Candidate record form (CRF): The form completed for each student on which the teacher records a separate mark for each AO (out of 24) and a total out of 96.

Component 1 at a Glance: Structure, Weighting and the AO Mark Scheme

Say it first: A-level Art and Design is a coursework-based qualification - there is no written examination paper. Both AQA components are non-exam assessment (NEA). Component 1, the Personal investigation (7202/C), is the larger: 96 marks, 60% of the A-level, with no time limit. It is set and marked by your centre and moderated by AQA during a visit that normally takes place in June. Because the weighting dwarfs Component 2 (40%), every decision you make about the Personal investigation is an A* decision - a two-band swing on a single AO here is worth more than any amount of exam-hall revision. Revision in this subject therefore means something different from other A-levels: you are revising the specification's evidence requirements against a portfolio that already exists, auditing it for gaps, and preparing to close them - not memorising content for a hall.

The component has two inseparable parts. First, a practical investigation into an idea, issue, concept or theme whose focus must be identified independently by you and which must lead to a finished outcome or a series of related finished outcomes. Second, written material: a coherent, logically structured extended response of between 1,000 and 3,000 words of continuous prose, presented either as one passage or as a series of shorter, linked passages incorporated within the practical work. The spec is explicit that the component is assessed as a whole: evidence of all four assessment objectives must appear in both the practical and the written material. A brilliant sketchbook with a bolted-on essay, or an elegant essay with thin practical work, caps your mark in every band. The spec also frames the component as synoptic assessment: you must draw together the knowledge, understanding and skills learned across the whole course - contextual study, media technique, observational recording, presentation - and apply them to one sustained enquiry of your own, responding to a stimulus, issue or theme you chose yourself.

The four assessment objectives, set by Ofqual and identical across all boards, are:

  • AO1 - Develop ideas through sustained and focused investigations informed by contextual and other sources, demonstrating analytical and critical understanding.
  • AO2 - Explore and select appropriate resources, media, materials, techniques and processes, reviewing and refining ideas as work develops.
  • AO3 - Record ideas, observations and insights relevant to intentions, reflecting critically on work and progress.
  • AO4 - Present a personal and meaningful response that realises intentions and, where appropriate, makes connections between visual and other elements.

Each component is marked out of 96 with the AOs equally weighted: a maximum of 24 marks per AO. Six bands are provided, each worth four marks, and within a band the qualifiers just, adequately, clearly and convincingly fix the exact mark - the lowest mark in a band means you have just met its descriptor, the highest that you have convincingly met it and only just failed the band above. Know the band language - it tells you exactly what A* looks like:

Each AO has its own top-band phrase, and they are worth memorising because they double as a checklist for your portfolio. AO1 top band: an exceptional ability to develop ideas through sustained investigations informed by contextual and other sources, with exceptional analytical and critical understanding. AO2: an exceptional ability to explore and select appropriate resources, media, materials, techniques and processes, reviewing and refining ideas in a confident and purposeful manner as work develops. AO3: an exceptional ability to record ideas, observations and insights relevant to intentions and to reflect critically on work and progress. AO4: an exceptional ability to present a personal and meaningful response, successfully realising intentions and making connections between visual, written and other elements, with exceptionally clear, coherent and accurate use of language. In Component 1 terms this translates as: AO1 lives in your artist-analysis pages and the contextual thread running through your experiments; AO2 lives in media trials and the visible decisions between them; AO3 lives in observational drawing, photography and the critical notes after each phase; AO4 lives in the planning studies, the finished outcome(s) and the curation of the whole. Note that AO1 rewards sustained and focused investigation - both words matter: sustained means depth over time, focused means every page serves one line of enquiry. Note also that the AOs sit on top of a quality of making requirement: handling materials effectively, skilfully and safely underpins all four, because confident technique is what lets intentions reach the outcome.

How the AOs actually get judged: your teacher marks each AO separately on the Candidate record form after internal standardisation (the centre's process for checking all its marking is consistent), then AQA's moderator reviews a sample and can adjust marks in either direction. Because you will not be there to explain the portfolio, every piece of evidence must be legible, sequenced and self-explanatory. Before submission, audit the work AO by AO: for each objective, name the page where its evidence is strongest, the page where it is weakest, and what you will add or annotate to close the gap. This audit - thirty minutes with the assessment criteria grid and the spec's own band descriptors - is the single highest-value revision task in the subject, because it converts a vague sense of is my work good? into a specific list of fixable evidence gaps.

Raw marks are scaled: Component 1 (96) is multiplied by 3 to give 288 of the 480 total scaled mark; Component 2 (96) by 2 to give 192. Component 1 therefore carries 60% of the final outcome, which is why centres expect the investigation to be substantially complete before the Externally set assignment paper arrives on 1 February of Year 13. Assessment is to the standard of a full A-level course - 360 guided learning hours - so work is judged at A-level standard, not GCSE-plus. Your teacher marks each AO separately on the Candidate record form after internal standardisation; AQA's moderator then reviews a sample and can adjust marks in either direction, so evidence must be legible, sequenced and self-explanatory without you in the room.

Other boards are built on the same coursework logic. OCR (H601 Fine Art): Component 01 Personal investigation, 120 marks, 60%, comprising the practical portfolio plus a related study (an extended written element of roughly 1,000-3,000 words); Component 02 Externally set task, 80 marks, 40%, 15 hours supervised. Pearson Edexcel (9AD0): Component 1 Personal Investigation, 60%, self-initiated, internally marked and externally moderated; Component 2 Externally Set Assignment, 40%, with 15 hours supervised time. WJEC/Eduqas follows the same pattern: a coursework personal investigation at 60% and an externally set assignment at 40%. The percentages, the Ofqual AO wording and the coursework basis are common to all boards; only mark totals and administrative detail differ, so this revision transfers directly.

Choosing and Structuring the Investigation: From Theme to Sustained Enquiry

Because you must identify the focus independently, the first assessed act of Component 1 is choosing well. AQA asks for an investigation into an idea, issue, concept or theme - not a subject. Portraiture is a subject; how identity is fragmented and reassembled in contemporary portraiture is an investigation. The classic narrowing sequence works in three steps: broad theme, to a personal angle, to a question the work will answer. For example: Nature narrows to the relationship between humans and the coast, which narrows to erosion and memory on the Holderness coastline - a question that can drive a body of work combining photographic records, eroded monoprint surfaces and a final installation of suspended translucent panels. Each narrowing multiplies the connections you can make and sharpens the artists you can legitimately research. Equally, a personal angle - your commute, a family archive, cultural heritage, local politics, a medical experience - almost always produces better evidence than a generic theme, because AO4 rewards a response that is personal and meaningful, and authenticity is difficult to fake. Beware the opposite failure too: a focus so narrow that research dries up. The test is whether at least three or four substantial artists genuinely speak to your question.

Test a candidate title against four questions before committing:

  • Access - can you gather strong primary material (first-hand observation, photography, collected objects) for it?
  • Contextual depth - are there at least three or four substantial artists whose practice you can analyse in relation to it?
  • Media range - does it invite experimentation across several processes rather than one?
  • Outcome potential - can you visualise a finished outcome, or a series, that answers it?

A theme that fails two of these will strangle the investigation by February. Avoid titles that are really artist studies (The work of Lucian Freud) - the spec requires your idea investigated through critical/contextual material, not a biography; and avoid outcome-first choices (I want to paint a big canvas of the sea) because an outcome without a line of reasoning cannot show development from initial intentions. Also avoid themes chosen purely for what you think examiners like: moderators read thousands of identity and mental health investigations and reward the ones with genuine evidence, not the ones with fashionable words.

Structure is what turns material into a sustained line of reasoning. The spec demands a coherent, in-depth study that demonstrates the ability to construct and develop a sustained line of reasoning from an initial starting point to a final realisation, showing clear development from initial intentions to the final outcome. In practice the portfolio should read in a deliberate order:

  1. Starting point and intent - the chosen focus stated, mind-mapped, with initial visual responses and first primary observations.
  2. Contextual research - gallery, museum and site visits; artist analysis pages that go beyond copying: analysing methods, contexts and ideas, with your own transcriptions and studies made in response to the work.
  3. Experimentation - media, technique and process trials linked explicitly to the research: what you took from each artist's approach and why.
  4. Review and refinement - annotated evaluation of what is working, decisions to pursue, modify or discard, and second-generation experiments shaped by those decisions.
  5. Recording from observation throughout - drawing and photography that feed the developing idea, not a separate drawing unit bolted on for AO3.
  6. Realisation - planning studies, maquettes or compositional drafts leading to the finished outcome or related outcomes, followed by a critical evaluation against initial intentions.

A worked sketch of that architecture in action - focus how clutter and consumption bury the domestic interior: page one states the question and shows a mind-map plus photographs of a family kitchen; pages two to four analyse, say, the assemblages of Cornelia Parker and the interiors of Vilhelm Hammershoi, each analysed for method, context and idea with a transcription study in response; pages five to eight trial collage, cast objects and monoprint, each page ending with a decision; pages nine and ten review - one route abandoned with reasons, one escalated; pages eleven to twelve plan the final installation with compositional drafts; the last page evaluates the realised installation against the opening question. Every page answers what next, so the line of reasoning never breaks.

Gallery and museum visits are primary research, not decoration. The spec's written-element requirements name studies of others' work made during a residency, or on a site, museum or gallery visit as legitimate contextual references, and AO1 rewards first-hand engagement above screen-scrolling. Work a visit properly: study one work in person for twenty minutes and record what a reproduction cannot show (scale, surface, brushwork, installation logic); sketch in situ, however briefly; photograph labels and wall texts; note the curation - what hangs next to what, and why. Then convert the visit into portfolio pages within a week: a transcription study in response to a named work, an analysis of its method, context and ideas, and one experiment that applies what you learned. Postcards, catalogues and exhibition guides then feed the bibliography. A single well-worked visit generates more assessable AO1 evidence than fifty saved Instagram images.

Timing matters because the ESA paper arrives on 1 February of Year 13 and its preparatory period then takes over your working week. A realistic schedule: summer of Year 12 - first observations and reading, provisional theme; September-October - focus finalised, contextual research begun; November-January - experiments and refinement, written element drafted; January-February - final outcome completed, written element polished, work selected and presented for moderation. Students who leave the realisation until after February split their attention at exactly the wrong moment, and the Component 1 outcome suffers for it.

Development, not repetition, is the examiner's watchword. Ten competent pages that each restart the idea score lower than six pages that visibly escalate it. Every page should end by answering two questions: what did I learn, and what happens next? If you cannot answer, the page is decoration. Equally, do not delete the failures - rejected experiments with a reasoned annotation are precisely the reviewing and refining evidence AO2's top band demands. Run critique cycles with peers or your teacher at each phase transition and record the outcome: after the critique I abandoned X because... and tested Y instead is exactly the reflexive language the top bands reward.

The spec notes there is no restriction on the scale of practical work and that you should carefully select, organise and present your work so it is well structured - curation is assessed. A portfolio of fifty unsorted sheets buries the line of reasoning; a portfolio of thirty-five sequenced sheets, with larger resolved pieces supporting the flow, proves it. Common pitfalls, all visible in moderator reports every year: theme too broad or purely descriptive; contextual research that is copying without analysis; media trials disconnected from intentions; a final outcome that appears with no planning trail; a written element that narrates (then I did...) rather than argues; and sources not acknowledged. Each pitfall maps to a lost band. The remedy in every case is the same discipline: every piece of work must lead to the next and, as the spec puts it, relate in meaningful ways to relevant critical/contextual materials.

Choose formats deliberately. The spec allows sketchbooks, workbooks, journals and mounted sheets to underpin the work, and each has a different assessment personality. A sketchbook carries the investigative flow - dated, chronological, annotated on the page; a workbook suits layered experiments with tests taped in and evaluated; a journal handles the extended written passages and contextual analysis; mounted sheets present the polished and the large. Most successful investigations mix two or three: the sketchbook as engine, mounted sheets as showcases for the strongest developments, larger canvases or constructions as the realisation approaches. Whatever the mix, the sequencing must survive the moderator's cold read: if a stranger cannot reconstruct your line of reasoning from the order of the pages alone, the structure is not yet finished.

The Written Element (1,000-3,000 Words) and Evidence Requirements

The written material is not an add-on essay; it is one half of the evidence. AQA lists exactly what it must do - it must confirm understanding of creative decisions, providing evidence of all four assessment objectives by:

  • clarifying the focus of the investigation;
  • demonstrating critical understanding of contextual and other sources;
  • substantiating decisions leading to the development and refinement of ideas;
  • recording ideas, observations and insights relevant to intentions by reflecting critically on practical work;
  • making meaningful connections between visual, written and other elements.

It must also include specialist vocabulary appropriate to the subject matter, include a bibliography that identifies contextual references from books, journals, websites, studies made during a residency, or on a site, museum or gallery visit, and be legible with accurate spelling, punctuation and grammar. You may present it as a single continuous passage or as a series of shorter, linked passages incorporated within the practical work - the embedded-passage model often scores better because the writing sits beside the work it analyses and the examiner sees the connections live, which is precisely what AO4's connections between visual and other elements means.

Structure the essay as an argument, not a diary. A reliable shape for 1,000-3,000 words: an introduction (roughly 150-250 words) that states the focus, your line of argument and how the investigation unfolds; three to five thematic body sections (roughly 400-500 words each), each pairing contextual analysis with your own practical response - after studying Hockney's joiners I layered fractured photographic perspectives; this failed because..., which led me to...; and a conclusion (150-250 words) that returns to the initial intentions and judges how the final outcome realises them. A sweet spot of around 2,000-2,400 words usually leaves room for analysis without padding: examiners consistently reward density of insight over length, and anything drifting towards 3,000 risks thinning the argument and breaching the spec limit.

A paragraph skeleton that scores, with the moves labelled: (claim) Hockney's joiners offered a model of time compressed into a single plane; (evidence) his 1980s photographic collages stitch dozens of viewpoints so the viewer's eye wanders rather than fixes; (analysis) the method treats memory as spatial rather than sequential, which matched my intention to layer several moments of the same street; (application) I overlapped twelve photographs of the same junction, then intensified the overlaps in acrylic; (judgement) the trial succeeded in destabilising viewpoint but flattened colour, so the next experiment transferred the composition to translucent layers that could be hung apart. Every paragraph in your essay should contain that final judgement move - it is what separates analytical and critical understanding (top bands) from description (middle bands).

Write in first person where it carries judgement - I selected oil glazes because... - but keep the register academic: no conversational filler, precise terminology (impasto, intaglio, gesso, compositional space, negative space) and critical verbs (subverts, synthesises, echoes, rejects, distils). The top band of AO4 explicitly requires exceptionally clear, coherent and accurate use of language, and AO1 requires fluent use of appropriate specialist vocabulary - so SPaG is a marks issue, not just a presentation issue. Quote sparingly and always attribute; paraphrase other people's analysis in your own voice; never submit an online commentary or AI-generated text as your own critical understanding.

Evidence requirements across the whole component. Both practical and written work must show all four AOs, so plan the essay to cover what the sketchbook cannot easily prove, and vice versa:

AOBest evidenced by practical workWritten element's job
AO1 (24)Artist analysis pages, gallery studies, contextual links made visible inside experimentsCritical understanding in prose: analysis of artists' methods, contexts and ideas; synthesis of influences
AO2 (24)Media trials, sampling, rejected routes, refinement pagesSubstantiating why media and processes were selected, reviewed and refined
AO3 (24)Observational drawing, photography, annotated reflection on progressRecording insights; critical reflection on what the practical work shows and where it must go
AO4 (24)Planning studies, final outcome(s), presentation and curationConnecting visual and written elements; judging how intentions were realised

Sources and integrity. The spec states plainly: students must identify and acknowledge sources which are not their own. Keep a live bibliography from day one - books (author, title, publisher, year), journal and web sources (with access dates), and gallery visits recorded as primary research. A consistent minimum format: author surname, initial, year, title, publisher or site, and for websites the URL with the date you accessed it. Quote sparingly and always attribute; paraphrase analysis in your own voice; never submit a transcription of an online commentary as your own critical understanding. Moderators sample for exactly this, and unacknowledged sources put the award of the whole component at risk.

Common written-element failures, and the fixes. First, narration without argument - the essay that reads then I did X, then I did Y: fix by ending every paragraph with a judgement move (what worked, what failed, what happens next). Second, description of artists without critical analysis - a biography of the movement instead of an argument about what its methods contribute to your work: fix by pairing every artist claim with your own practical application. Third, disconnection between writing and work - the essay analyses artists that never appear in the portfolio, or the portfolio contains experiments the essay never mentions: fix by cross-referencing explicitly (as page seven shows...). Fourth, vocabulary collapse - the essay written in conversational register with no specialist terms: fix by drafting a terminology bank (fifteen to twenty terms from your media and movements) and checking each one appears where relevant. Fifth, a conclusion that introduces new material instead of judging the outcome: fix by returning to the introduction's question and answering it.

Finally, present like a curator. The spec asks you to carefully select, organise and present the Personal investigation so it is well structured and provides evidence meeting all four objectives. Mount sheets in sequence, label larger works, embed the linked written passages where they belong, and add a contents page or divider cards for the moderator. Photograph 3D and fragile work before transport, keep the Candidate record form with the submission, and rehearse a two-minute walk-through of the investigation's storyline before moderation day. The last impression the assessor gets should be of a study that was designed, not accumulated - and the first impression, on page one, should already announce the question the whole portfolio exists to answer.

A model opening paragraph, at the standard the top band expects: This investigation asks how erosion reshapes memory of place on the Holderness coastline. It grew from weekly photography of a cliff path I have walked since childhood, and from noticing that the maps no longer matched the ground. The investigation is informed by two strands of practice: land artists who treat landscape as material rather than view, and painters of memory whose surfaces encode loss. The first section analyses how these artists' methods and contexts shaped my initial experiments; the middle sections substantiate the media decisions - monoprint, photographic layering, cast plaster - through which the idea developed; the conclusion judges the final installation of suspended translucent panels against my initial intention to make absence visible. One paragraph, and the assessor already knows the focus, the influences, the media route and the outcome - which is exactly the clarity the written element exists to provide.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: Your Personal investigation must lead from an independently identified starting point to a finished outcome or a series of related finished outcomes. With reference to a theme of your choice, explain how you would structure the investigation so it demonstrates a sustained line of reasoning, and show how the written element evidences all four assessment objectives. (96-mark component, each AO out of 24 - AQA 7202/C, 60% of A-level)
Mark-scheme logic: Mark-scheme logic (AO1-AO4, integrated whole). AO1 hits: focus identified independently (theme narrowed to an assessable question, e.g. erosion and memory on the Holderness coast); sustained and focused investigation structured as starting point - contextual research - experimentation - refinement - realisation; work informed by an aspect of contemporary or past practice (artists analysed for methods, contexts and ideas, not copied); analytical and critical understanding shown by evaluating what each influence contributes; fluent specialist vocabulary. AO2 hits: exploration of a range of media (photographic records, monoprint, translucent panel installation); selection justified against intentions; reviewing and refining evidenced by rejected experiments retained with reasoned annotation and second-generation trials. AO3 hits: sustained observational recording (drawing, photography, collected objects) feeding the idea; critical reflection on progress after each phase. AO4 hits: planning studies and maquettes leading to a finished outcome or related outcomes (a series of suspended translucent panels); final evaluation against initial intentions; meaningful connections between visual, written and other elements made live by embedding linked written passages beside the practical work. Structural requirements stated: 1,000-3,000 words of continuous prose, bibliography identifying contextual references, sources acknowledged, assessed as one whole with 24 marks per AO. Top band requires exceptional, not merely consistent, performance in every strand.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

State the assessment requirements of AQA A-level Component 1 (7202/C) and the weighting of each assessment objective. (6 marks)
Answer: Component 1 is a practical investigation into an idea, issue, concept or theme, supported by written material; the focus is identified independently by the student and must lead to a finished outcome or a series of related finished outcomes (1). There is no time limit; it is worth 96 marks, 60% of the A-level, set and marked by the centre and moderated by AQA during a visit normally in June (1). The written material must be an extended response of 1,000-3,000 words of continuous prose, with specialist vocabulary, a bibliography identifying contextual references, and accurate SPaG (1). The AOs are equally weighted: 24 marks each out of 96 - AO1 develop ideas through sustained and focused investigations informed by contextual and other sources; AO2 explore and select appropriate resources, media, materials, techniques and processes, reviewing and refining; AO3 record ideas, observations and insights, reflecting critically; AO4 present a personal and meaningful response that realises intentions (2 for all four correctly stated). Practical and written work are assessed as an integrated whole and must each evidence all four AOs (1).
A student proposes 'Nature' as a Personal investigation title. Explain why this is too broad and show how it could be refined into a focused, assessable investigation. (10 marks)
Answer: Why too broad: 'Nature' is a subject, not an idea, issue, concept or theme (1); it permits no focused line of reasoning because almost any artwork could be dragged into it (1); contextual research would scatter across landscape traditions without a critical thread, weakening AO1's requirement for sustained AND focused investigation (1); primary material and media trials would have no common direction, weakening AO2 and AO3 (1); no natural finished outcome suggests itself, weakening AO4 (1). Refinement sequence: narrow to a personal angle - e.g. the human relationship with the eroding coastline (1); then to a question - e.g. how erosion reshapes memory of place on the Holderness coast (1). Test for access (first-hand visits and photography possible), contextual depth (three or four artists engaging landscape, memory or materiality), media range (photography, print, constructed panels) and outcome potential (a series of related outcomes) (1). The refined title drives a portfolio in which each experiment answers the question, contextual research is analysed for methods and ideas rather than copied, and the realisation judges itself against initial intentions - precisely the sustained line of reasoning the spec demands (1).
Explain how the written element of the Personal investigation demonstrates AO1 and AO4. (12 marks)
Answer: AO1: the written element clarifies the focus of the investigation, stating the independently identified question (2); it demonstrates critical understanding of contextual and other sources - analysing artists' methods, contexts and ideas in prose, synthesising influences rather than describing them, with fluent specialist vocabulary (2); it is informed by contemporary or past practice and shows analytical and critical understanding of it (2). AO4: it substantiates and judges the final response - explaining how decisions led to the outcome, then evaluating how the finished work realises the initial intentions (2); it makes meaningful connections between visual and other elements, linking the portfolio's experiments, the contextual research and the outcome into one argument (2); clarity, coherence and accuracy of language are themselves assessed within AO4's top band, which requires exceptionally clear, coherent and accurate use of language (2). The strongest answers pair every claim about an artist with evidence from the student's own practical response, so the writing and the work confirm each other as one integrated whole.
'In Component 1 the practical work and the essay are marked as one integrated whole.' Evaluate this statement and explain its implications for how you build the portfolio. (16 marks)
Answer: The statement is accurate to the spec: the Personal investigation is assessed as a whole, and evidence of all four AOs must be provided in BOTH the practical and the written material (2). Implication 1 - neither part can compensate for the other: a strong sketchbook with a bolted-on narrative essay caps AO1/AO4 because the critical understanding is not demonstrated across both (2). Implication 2 - the essay should not duplicate the sketchbook: its job is to clarify focus, substantiate decisions and judge outcomes; the sketchbook's job is visible exploration and recording (2). Implication 3 - embedded linked passages often serve integration better than a single appendix, because prose sits beside the work it analyses and the examiner sees the connections live (2). Implication 4 - plan the component as one narrative: each practical phase generates a written reflection, and each written conclusion launches the next practical phase, so the sustained line of reasoning is carried jointly (2). Evaluation of limits: practical excellence still dominates the impression at moderation, yet a thin essay can drag a whole AO band down; equally, an over-written essay nearing the 3,000-word ceiling can thin the practical evidence and dilute the argument (2). Conclusion: build both parts from the same plan, with shared vocabulary, acknowledged sources and a curation sequence in which writing and work interleave - the mark is awarded to the study, not to its halves (2).
Describe the evidence a moderator would expect for the top band of AO2 in a Personal investigation portfolio, and explain how you would generate it. (12 marks)
Answer: Top-band descriptor: an exceptional ability to explore and select appropriate resources, media, materials, techniques and processes, reviewing and refining ideas in a confident and purposeful manner as work develops (2). Evidence of exploration: trials across a genuine range - e.g. monoprint, photographic layering, gessoed panels - each sampled and compared rather than one medium repeated (2). Evidence of selection: explicit justification of what was chosen and why, tied to intentions (2). Evidence of reviewing and refining: rejected routes retained with reasoned annotation; second-generation experiments visibly shaped by the review; progressive mastery shown by later trials succeeding where early ones failed (2). Generation strategy: run a deliberate media-audit page after each contextual phase, asking what process each artist's approach implies and testing it (2); date and sequence all trials so development reads chronologically (2); write refinement notes as decisions, not descriptions - what is dropped, what is developed, what the next experiment must prove (2). The result is a portfolio in which refinement is visible on the surface of the work itself.
