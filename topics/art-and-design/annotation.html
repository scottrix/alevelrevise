Annotation and Visual Language

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC/Eduqas

A-Level Art annotation and visual language revision: annotating the portfolio with decision prose, the formal-elements vocabulary, recording ideas in sketchbooks, and how annotation maps to AO1-AO4 evidence in the coursework components.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain what annotation is for in a coursework portfolio: recording ideas, observations and insights (AO3) while evidencing the reasoning behind AO1, AO2 and AO4 decisions.
  • Write annotations that move beyond description - intention, action, finding, next step - using appropriate specialist vocabulary, legibly and accurately.
  • Deploy the formal-elements vocabulary (line, tone, colour, texture, shape, form, space, composition, rhythm, scale) in analysis sentences, per the AQA 7202 skills list.
  • Record ideas systematically in sketchbooks, workbooks and journals: observations, gallery notes, media trials, critiques and critical reflection on progress.
  • Map annotation types to the assessment objectives so the moderator can find AO evidence without explanation.
  • Avoid the standard annotation failures: decorative notes, description without judgement, no links to artists or intentions, and loose unlabelled material.

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Annotation: Written notes attached to practical work explaining intentions, decisions, findings and critical reflection - the primary vehicle for AO3 recording and the connective tissue between the AOs.
Formal elements: The core visual components of an artwork named in the AQA 7202 skills list: colour, line, tone, texture, shape, form, plus pictorial space, composition, rhythm, scale and structure.
Critical reflection: The second half of AO3: judging your own work and progress against intentions - what worked, what failed, why, and what happens next - rather than merely describing activity.
Primary observation: Recording drawn or photographed directly from the subject in front of you, rather than from reproductions; the first-hand evidence AO3 weights most heavily.
Negative space: The space around and between subjects, read as a shape in its own right; controlling negative space is a compositional decision, not an absence.
Pictorial space: The illusion of depth within a flat image, constructed by perspective, scale, overlap, atmospheric fade and tonal recession - named in the AQA 7202 skills list.
Decision prose: Annotation written as judgements - 'I am abandoning X because...; the next experiment must prove...' - rather than as a diary of activity.
Specialist vocabulary: The subject-specific terminology the spec requires in annotation and written material: process terms (impasto, glaze, registration), movement terms (tenebrism, readymade) and formal terms (pictorial space, negative space).

Annotating the Portfolio: Purpose, Method and the Decision Prose

Annotation earns marks in a coursework portfolio because it is the only place where reasoning is visible on the surface of the work. AO3 reads: record ideas, observations and insights relevant to intentions, reflecting critically on work and progress - annotation is the vehicle for both halves: the recording (what you noticed, tried, learned) and the critical reflection (what it means, what failed, what happens next). But annotation also feeds the other three AOs: a note explaining why an artist's method was borrowed is AO1 evidence; a note reviewing a media trial is AO2 evidence; a note judging how a planning study realises the outcome is AO4 evidence. The spec makes the language requirement explicit: annotation and notes should use appropriate specialist vocabulary and be legible with accurate use of language so that meaning is clear - sloppy annotation is therefore a marks issue in every AO, not a presentation issue.

What to annotate: every place where a decision or a judgement exists but the work alone cannot show it. The standard set:

  • Intentions - what this page or piece is for, stated at the start (this page tests whether layered translucency can carry the demolition theme).
  • Decisions - what was chosen and why (charcoal rejected: smears under the collage test; acrylic layers selected).
  • Findings - what the work revealed (the overlap flattens colour; the composition needs a vertical to hold the left edge).
  • Critical reflection - judgement against intentions (this fails my initial intention because the surface is too controlled - the concept needs chance).
  • Artist links - what was taken from analysed practice (glazing follows the method analysed on page four; the underpainting warms the darks as Caravaggio's ground does).
  • Next steps - what happens next (next trial: sand between layers; the final outcome must resolve the edges).

Where to annotate: on the page, in the margin, with arrows that visibly attach to the work. Never on loose notes, sticky labels or separate slips - they detach in transit and the moderator cannot attribute them; if a note cannot live on the page, photograph it, print it, and fix it down. Annotate as you go, dated: the dating is what makes the reflection credible (a note written next week reads as hindsight; a note written in the session reads as reflection). Under time pressure - Component 2's supervised hours especially - three disciplined notes per session are enough: what was attempted, what changed, what the next session must achieve. The spec requires the supervised work to be clearly identified as such, so label the session and hour in the annotation itself.

How much: quality over coverage. The moderator reads for decision prose, not word count - a page with two judicious notes outscores a page crowded with description. The formula that works: intention - action - finding - next step. Compare: weak - I used charcoal and it went quite well, I like the texture; strong - charcoal trialled for speed of tonal revision (intention); the deepest blacks read as eroded rock (finding); too fragile for the translucent layer test, so acrylic layering is selected for the outcome (decision and next step). The weak note describes activity; the strong note demonstrates the recording AND the reflection AO3 names - and it quietly evidences AO2's reviewing and refining at the same time.

Annotating contextual work needs the same discipline. A transcription study annotated with what the making taught you converts copying into evidence: copying the passage above the figure showed the glaze sits over a warm underpainting - my own ground needs warming before the darks go on. An artist analysis page should end in a judgement that feeds a decision: Hockney's joiners reassemble time; my equivalent overlap flattened colour, so the next trial separates the layers physically instead. The link is the point: annotation that connects the artist's method to your own decision is simultaneously AO1 and AO2 evidence, and it is the connective move moderators reward most consistently.

Common annotation failures, mapped to lost bands: decorative annotation (hand-lettered titles, washi tape, pages of mood board prose with no judgement); description without judgement (then I did); no artist links (analysis pages that never touch the practical work); no intentions (pages that cannot say what they are for); illegibility (illegible script violates the spec's language requirement); loose notes; and post-hoc annotation written the night before moderation, which dates itself. The fix in every case is the same: annotate in the session, write judgements, attach to the work, and keep the vocabulary precise. Before submission, audit the portfolio page by page: every page should answer what is this for, what did I learn, what next - and any page that cannot is the page to fix first.

Annotation per objective - the worked examples. For AO1 (contextual development): after analysing Gentileschi's tenebrism on page four, this trial uses single-source lighting on the figure; the immediacy works, but over-dramatises what the demolition theme needs - softened contrast next. One note, and it demonstrates the sustained, focused, contextual investigation the AO names. For AO2 (explore, select, refine): gessoed panel over paper: the impasto holds; paper buckled under wash - grounds now gessoed throughout; the third layer sands back to reveal the first, which the erosion concept needs. For AO3 (record, reflect): five-minute study of the collapsed path from the cliff top: the edge reads as a wound; the erosion is moving faster than the maps; the concept's absence becomes visible here - feed this line into the composition. For AO4 (personal, meaningful response): planning study resolves the suspended-panel format: five panels, translucent, hung with a gap; the gap is the absence the theme asks for - final evaluation against the opening intention to follow. Four notes, one per objective, all in decision prose - this is the per-page standard the top bands assume.

Annotating under the ESA's conditions needs compression, not omission. The supervised hours are unaided, and the spec requires the work produced in them to be clearly identified as such and annotated with appropriate specialist vocabulary. The compressed standard: every session, three notes - attempted, changed, next - plus the session number and hour (session 4, hours 10-12: glaze flattened the form; scraped and rebuilt with impasto neutral; next session: edges and unifying pass). Five minutes per session, and the moderator gains the independent decision-making evidence the component is distinctively assessed on. Prep annotation carries the fuller prose; supervised annotation carries the compressed version - both are required, and the boundary between them must stay visible because prep may be referred to but never amended once the 15 hours begin.

Visual Analysis Vocabulary: The Formal Elements and Beyond

The AQA 7202 skills list names the vocabulary: understanding of pictorial space, composition, rhythm, scale and structure and appreciation of colour, line, tone, texture, shape and form. These are the terms analysis sentences are built from, and precision with them is what fluent use of appropriate specialist vocabulary (AO1's top band) and exceptionally clear, coherent and accurate use of language (AO4's top band) mean in practice. Hold each element as a definition plus an effect:

  • Line - the path of a moving point; its weight, speed and direction carry character (nervous, assured, measured). Effect: guides the eye; contour describes; gesture performs.
  • Tone - lightness and darkness; modelling turns flat shapes into volume; tonal recession builds depth. Effect: drama (chiaroscuro), form, hierarchy of focus.
  • Colour - hue, saturation, temperature; complements vibrate at equal intensity, temperature models space (warm advance, cool recession), limited palettes unify. Effect: mood, focus, harmony or clash.
  • Texture - surface quality, actual (impasto, tooth) or implied (rendered). Effect: records the making; literalises the subject.
  • Shape - flat, bounded area (geometric, organic); Form - three-dimensional volume. Effect: composition's units; form models under light.
  • Space - pictorial space (the illusion of depth: perspective, overlap, atmospheric fade, scale) and negative space (the shapes between, read as shapes). Effect: depth, composition, tension.
  • Composition - the arrangement: balance, focal point, diagonals, rule of thirds, cropping. Effect: where the eye goes and in what order.
  • Rhythm and scale - repetition with variation; relative size. Effect: movement through the picture; hierarchy and monumentality.

Analysis sentences combine two or more elements and attach an effect. The pattern: element + how it is handled + what it does. The tenebrism pushes the figures out of darkness, so the eye lands on the lit gesture first. Warm underpainting shifts every glaze toward heat, so the shadow in the folds glows rather than greys. The negative space between the suspended panels is read as shape in its own right, so absence becomes the subject. Each sentence names the formal fact and claims its effect - and the effect claim is where analysis begins, because describing that a painting is dark says nothing; saying what the darkness does is analysis.

Media-specific vocabulary is the second layer. Painting: underpainting, glaze, scumble, impasto, alla prima, fat over lean, ground, gesso. Print: registration, burr, edition, proof, reduction, key block. 3D: armature, maquette, subtractive/additive, release agent, patina. Photography: aperture, shutter speed, ISO, depth of field, burning/dodging, contact sheet. Digital: layers, mask, blend mode, raster/vector. Movement vocabulary is the third layer: tenebrism, plein air, automatism, readymade, assemblage, installation, dematerialisation. The full stack - formal, media, movement - is what a terminology bank of fifteen to twenty terms should draw from; used accurately and annotated as decisions, it is the fluency the top bands name.

Weak vs strong phrasing - hear the upgrade:

Weak (description)Strong (analysis)
The painting is dark and dramaticTenebrism pushes the figures out of darkness, so the lit gesture lands first and doctrine reads as event
Van Gogh used thick paintImpasto records the pressure and speed of each stroke, so the surface performs the feeling the subject describes
The composition is unbalancedThe mass sits right of centre and the left is held only by the horizon line, so the negative space reads as tension - which is why the trial fails my intention
The colours are brightComplementary orange against blue vibrates at equal intensity, so the focal area is claimed by colour rather than by tone
I like the textureThe sanded between-layers texture mimics the eroded surface the concept needs - hence the acrylic selection

Sentence stems that generate analysis when the page is blank: The eye lands first on... because...; The composition holds... by means of...; The process of X allows Y, which serves the intention to...; Compared with [artist], my handling of X...; This fails/achieves my initial intention because...; The next trial must resolve.... Drill: take any annotation from your portfolio, strip its vocabulary, and rebuild it through the stems - if the rebuilt sentence claims something the original did not, the original was description.

Accuracy is the final discipline. A term used loosely costs credibility: chiaroscuro is light-dark modelling, tenebrism is its extreme (figures in near-darkness); a glaze is transparent, a scumble semi-opaque and broken; an edition is the numbered print run, a proof the trial. The Tate's art-terms glossary is the authority of record for movement and technique definitions - use it to check each term before it enters the portfolio, and cite it in the bibliography. Precision with twenty terms beats fluency with none: the examiner marks what is accurate, and the top band's word is fluent - which presupposes correct.

The elements read differently across movements - and naming the difference is analysis. The same element carries different meanings in different hands, and the comparative move is where vocabulary earns its depth. Line: Ingres's measured contour (neoclassical discipline) against Van Gogh's nervous directional stroke (expression performed in the mark) against Pollock's allover line (no contour at all - the line is the subject). Tone: Caravaggio's tenebrism (drama and doctrine) against Whistler's tonal whisper (atmosphere over narrative). Colour: the Fauves' arbitrary colour (Matisse - colour liberated from description, feeling made visible) against the Impressionists' optical colour (broken, observed, transient) against Rothko's saturated fields (contemplation). Texture: Vermeer's smooth enamel surface (invisibility of the making) against Van Gogh's impasto (visibility of the making as meaning). Space: Renaissance perspective (measured, single viewpoint) against Cezanne's tilted planes (space as constructed, not given) against the flattening of cubism and the cropped space of Degas. Drilling the element-across-movements pattern prepares two question types at once: the comparison question, and the 'how does X's use of [element] reflect [context]' question - both answered by the same sentence: element, handling, effect, context.

Rhythm, scale and structure deserve more attention than students give them. They are named in the spec's skills list and they are the vocabulary of composition-level analysis. Rhythm: repetition with variation - the repeated arches of a Renaissance fresco, the repeated panels of a Warhol grid, the repeated gestures of a Pollock allover - and what the variation does (breaks monotony, creates focus, performs accumulation). Scale: relative size within the work (hierarchical scale - the patron painted larger than the saints) and the scale of the work to the viewer (Rothko's canvases at body height engulf; miniatures require intimacy - and scale decisions are reasons, not accidents). Structure: the underlying armature - pyramidal composition in Raphael, the golden section, the grid's modernist order, the allover field's refusal of hierarchy. An analysis sentence for each: the pyramidal structure steadies the theological hierarchy, so stability itself carries the doctrine; the allover rhythm denies a focal point, so no single gesture outranks another - equality is the meaning. Structure, rhythm and scale are where your own compositional planning studies should be annotated too - the planning page that names its structure is doing in practice what the analysis page does in prose.

Recording Ideas: Sketchbooks and the AO Map

The AQA 7202 spec expects you to use sketchbooks/workbooks/journals to underpin their work where appropriate - three formats, three jobs. The sketchbook carries the investigative flow: dated, chronological, annotated on the page - the engine of the portfolio. The workbook suits layered experimentation: tests taped in, evaluated, cross-referenced. The journal handles the extended written passages and contextual analysis. Mounted sheets present the polished. Most successful investigations mix two or three: the sketchbook as engine, mounted sheets as showcases, larger resolved works as the realisation approaches. Whatever the mix, one rule decides the mark: the sequence must survive the moderator's cold read - if a stranger cannot reconstruct the line of reasoning from the order of the pages alone, the recording is not yet finished.

What to record: the full spread of AO3's demand - ideas, observations and insights relevant to intentions. The standard inventory:

  • Primary observation - drawing and photography from the subject itself; the portfolio's foundational evidence, and the behaviour the spec names explicitly (recording from observation, on a variety of scales).
  • Ideas - mind-maps that generate artists, processes and imagery rather than synonyms; visual notes for compositions and outcomes.
  • Gallery and museum notes - first-hand study: one work for twenty minutes, labels photographed, what a reproduction cannot show (scale, surface, installation logic).
  • Media trials and their decisions - the AO2 record: question, samples, comparison, decision.
  • Critique outcomes - what teachers and peers said, what you changed because of it.
  • Critical reflection - the judgement notes after each phase: what worked, what failed, what next.

Recording is not the same as annotating, and the distinction maps onto AO3's two halves. Recording captures (observation, notes, trials); annotation judges and connects (decisions, reflection, links). A page of observation with no annotation is half the evidence; a page of annotation with no observation is unsupported. The strongest pages pair them: a sustained observational drawing beside a note that says what the drawing revealed and what it feeds - the eroded edge reads as a wound; the concept's absence becomes visible; next trial tests the edge in monoprint.

Critical reflection is the top-band differentiator. AO3's second clause - reflecting critically on work and progress - is what separates 21-24 from 17-20: exceptional reflection judges the work against intentions at every phase transition, not just at the end. Build it structurally: after each phase (research, trials, planning, realisation) write a full review page answering three questions - what did this phase show, how does it compare with my initial intentions, what must the next phase prove. Cross-reference those pages in the written element, because the spec asks the essay to record ideas, observations and insights... by reflecting critically on practical work. Reflection written in the session and dated reads as reflection; reflection reconstructed later reads as hindsight, and moderators can tell.

Sketchbook hygiene makes the reflection credible. Date every page (the dating is what distinguishes reflection from reconstruction); number pages so the contents and the written element can cross-reference them (as page nine shows...); never remove pages - rejected work stays in with a reasoned note, because visible rejection is the reviewing and refining AO2 names; keep working pages unglamorous and let the mounted sheets carry the polish; and reserve the first page for the investigation's question, restated. The moderator's cold read starts at page one: if the question is not visible there, the storyline begins buried. In Component 2 the same hygiene applies across the preparatory period - and stops at the first supervised session, with the supervised work labelled and the prep sealed.

The reflection examples that earn. At phase transitions, the review page answers three questions with specific evidence. After research: the two analysed methods point in opposite directions - Hockney's reassembly and Monet's dissolution; the investigation follows reassembly because the concept needs memory made spatial; next phase must prove the overlap can carry the theme without flattening colour. After trials: three grounds tested; gessoed panel survives - acrylic layers visibly, dries inside supervised time, sands back to reveal earlier layers; the monoprint's texture is recovered by sanding; next phase resolves scale. After planning: the five-panel format resolves; the gap between panels is the absence the theme asks for; final evaluation must judge whether the installation realises the opening intention to make absence visible. Each example names the finding, the judgement and the next phase's test - the three-beat structure the top AO3 band assumes, and the structure the written element's prose should echo paragraph by paragraph.

The AO map - which annotation type evidences which objective:

AO (24 marks per AO)Annotation that evidences it
AO1 - develop ideas through sustained, focused, contextual investigationArtist-analysis notes with judgements; links from analysed methods to your own trials; gallery notes read critically
AO2 - explore and select, reviewing and refiningTrials-page decisions; review notes written as decisions; refinement notes naming what changed and why
AO3 - record ideas, observations, insights; reflect criticallyPrimary observation with findings; dated reflection at phase transitions; critique outcomes
AO4 - present a personal and meaningful response realising intentionsPlanning-study annotations; final-outcome evaluation against initial intentions; connections made between visual and written elements

Use the map as a pre-submission audit: walk the portfolio page by page, and for each AO name where its annotation evidence is strongest, weakest, and what to fix. Any AO whose evidence lives only in the written element, or only in the practical work, is an AO to fix - the components are assessed as integrated wholes.

Before moderation: the presentation checklist. Sequence the pages in the order they were made; label larger works and 3D pieces (photographed before transport); attach every note to the page it belongs to; include the contents or divider cards; keep the Candidate record form with the submission; and rehearse the two-minute walk-through of the storyline - starting point, research, trials, refinement, outcome. In a coursework-based subject there is no exam hall in which to perform: the portfolio and its annotation ARE the performance, and the last page the moderator sees should read as the conclusion of an argument - what was asked, what was found, what was made, and what the making proved.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: Explain how annotation in a coursework portfolio records ideas, observations and insights relevant to intentions, and show how the same annotations reflect critically on work and progress and evidence the other assessment objectives. (AO3 - 24 marks at A* level; assessed within AQA 7202/C and 7202/X)
Mark-scheme logic: Mark-scheme logic (AO3: record + reflect critically; feeds AO1/AO2/AO4). Recording hits: primary observation (drawing and photography from the subject, on a variety of scales) annotated with findings; mind-maps generating artists, processes and imagery; gallery and museum notes recording what reproduction cannot show; media trials recorded as question - samples - comparison - decision; critique outcomes recorded. Reflection hits (the top-band differentiator): judgement notes in every session, dated so they read as reflection not hindsight; phase-transition review pages judging work against initial intentions and setting what the next phase must prove; reflection written as decisions - what failed, why, what changes. Feeds the other AOs: artist links in annotation evidence AO1; trials decisions and refinement notes evidence AO2; planning-study annotations and final-outcome evaluations evidence AO4. Language and format requirements stated: appropriate specialist vocabulary, legible, accurate language, notes attached to the work, supervised work clearly identified in Component 2. Top band: exceptional ability to record AND reflect critically - reflection at every phase, judgement in every note, and an audit trail the moderator can read cold.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

State AO3 and explain the difference between its two halves. (6 marks)
Answer: AO3: record ideas, observations and insights relevant to intentions, reflecting critically on work and progress (2). First half - recording: capturing observation, notes, trials and insights (2). Second half - critical reflection: judging the work and progress against intentions - what worked, what failed, why, what happens next (2). The distinction matters for marks: a page that records without reflecting earns the middle bands; reflection at every phase transition is the top-band behaviour.
Rewrite this annotation at the standard the top band expects: 'I used charcoal and it went quite well, I like the texture.' Explain each improvement. (8 marks)
Answer: Rewrite: 'Charcoal trialled for speed of tonal revision; the deepest blacks read as the eroded rock the concept needs; too fragile for the translucent layer test, so acrylic layering is selected for the outcome and the texture will be recovered by sanding between layers' (2). Improvements: intention stated - why charcoal was trialled (1); specialist vocabulary - tonal revision (1); finding analysed - what the blacks do for the concept, not just 'I like' (1); critical judgement - the fragility that fails the next test (1); decision and next step - the selection and how the quality is recovered (1); the note now evidences AO3's recording and reflection plus AO2's selection simultaneously (1).
Explain how the formal-elements vocabulary should be used in analysis, illustrating with two elements. (8 marks)
Answer: The pattern: element + how it is handled + what it does - each sentence names the formal fact and claims its effect (2). Example 1, tone: tenebrism pushes the figures out of darkness, so the eye lands on the lit gesture first - tone claimed as focus and drama, not described as 'dark' (2). Example 2, colour: complementary orange against blue at equal intensity vibrates, so the focal area is claimed by colour rather than tone - colour claimed as focus mechanism (2). The effect claim is where analysis begins: describing that a painting is dark says nothing; saying what the darkness does is analysis - which is what the spec's appreciation of colour, line, tone, texture, shape and form, and pictorial space, composition, rhythm, scale and structure expects (2).
A student's portfolio shows strong final outcomes but almost no annotation on the pages between research and outcome. Explain the risk and the remedy. (10 marks)
Answer: Risk: without annotation the reasoning between research and outcome is invisible, so AO3's recording and reflection cannot be assessed - the pages read as activity without judgement (2); AO2's reviewing and refining is also unevidenced, because trials without decisions read as repetition or luck (2); and AO1's contextual thread breaks - artist links that never touch the practical work cannot demonstrate informed development (1). Remedy: annotate in the session, dated, attached to the page (1); write the formula - intention, action, finding, next step - on every working page (1); add phase-transition review pages judging work against initial intentions and setting what the next phase must prove (1); cross-reference the review pages in the written element so the essay substantiates the decisions (1); audit AO by AO before submission - name the strongest and weakest evidence for each and fix the weakest (1). Annotation is the only place reasoning is visible on the surface of the work: without it the moderator marks the work alone, and the integrated whole the spec assesses disappears.
Explain what should be recorded in a sketchbook during a gallery visit, and why first-hand study outweighs screen-based research. (8 marks)
Answer: Record: one work studied in person for twenty minutes - what a reproduction cannot show: scale, surface, brushwork, installation and hanging logic (2); a sketch in situ, however brief (1); labels and wall texts photographed or noted - dates, materials, context statements (1); the curation - what hangs adjacent and why (1). Why first-hand outweighs screens: the spec's written-element requirements name studies made on a site, museum or gallery visit as legitimate contextual references, and primary engagement generates observations no saved image reproduces (2); the visit converts into portfolio pages within a week - a transcription study in response, an analysis of method, context and idea, one experiment applying it (1). Fifty Instagram thumbnails generate less assessable AO1/AO3 evidence than one well-worked visit.
