The Externally Set Assignment (Component 2)

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC/Eduqas

AQA 7202/X Externally set assignment revision: the eight-question paper, preparatory period from 1 February, 15 hours of supervised time, AO1-AO4 strategy and exam-day rules. Coursework-based qualification - Component 2 is 40% of the A-level, assessed as NEA.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • State the structure and rules of AQA Component 2 (7202/X): eight starting points, one selected, preparatory period from 1 February plus 15 hours of unaided supervised time, 96 marks, 40% of the A-level.
  • Select a starting point strategically and convert it into preparatory work that evidences all four assessment objectives before supervised time begins.
  • Plan the 15 supervised hours against a time budget, knowing the first three hours must be consecutive and preparatory work may be referred to but not amended.
  • Work independently within the specified time constraints, developing a personal and meaningful response that leads to a finished outcome or series of related outcomes.
  • Apply the conduct rules correctly: stopping prep before supervised time, keeping work secure, acknowledging sources, and the no-internet condition.

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Externally set assignment (ESA): Component 2 of AQA A-level Art and Design (7202/X): a question paper set by AQA offering eight starting points, from which you select one; assessed through a preparatory period plus 15 hours of supervised time, 96 marks, 40% of the A-level.
Starting point: One of the eight questions or themes printed on the ESA paper, used as the beginning of your response; the paper is a prompt for an independent personal response, not a brief to illustrate.
Preparatory period: The period from receipt of the paper on 1 February (or as soon as possible after) until supervised time begins, in which research, planning and preparatory studies are produced in any suitable format.
Supervised time: The 15 hours of unaided, supervised work in which the finished outcome or related outcomes are produced; the first three hours must be consecutive.
Unaided work: Work produced without assistance from teachers or peers during supervised time; teachers may give general supervisory guidance but not practical or creative help.
Maquette: A small-scale preparatory model of a three-dimensional or installation outcome, used to test form, scale and construction before the supervised hours.
Time plan: A written schedule for the 15 supervised hours allocating hours to composition, main making and resolution, usually annotated onto the final preparatory sheet.
JCQ instructions: The Joint Council for Qualifications document 'Instructions for the conduct of examinations', whose guidelines must be followed during the supervised time.

Component 2: Structure, Rules and What the Board Actually Assesses

Remember the frame: A-level Art and Design is a coursework-based qualification - there is no written exam paper, and Component 2 is still non-exam assessment. The Externally set assignment (7202/X) is worth 96 marks, 40% of the A-level, and it is set by AQA, marked by your centre and moderated by AQA during a visit normally in June. What is external is the question paper: AQA prints a separate paper for each endorsed title (Fine Art is 7202), each offering a choice of eight questions to be used as starting points, and you select one. Papers are provided on 1 February, or as soon as possible after. Everything after that - the research, the trials, the outcome - is coursework logic, not invigilated silence.

The component runs in two phases:

  1. Preparatory period - from 1 February. You consider the eight starting points, select one, and build preparatory work in any suitable format: mounted sheets, design sheets, sketchbooks, workbooks, journals, models and maquettes.
  2. Supervised time - 15 hours. Unaided, supervised work in which you produce a finished outcome or a series of related finished outcomes, informed by your preparatory work. The first three hours must be consecutive.

Preparatory work and the supervised-time work are assessed together, as a whole, against all four assessment objectives - the same 24-marks-per-AO structure as Component 1, with six bands of four marks and the qualifiers just, adequately, clearly, convincingly. The spec adds a distinctive emphasis for this component: you are assessed on the ability to work independently, working within the specified time constraints, and developing a personal and meaningful response. Independence and time discipline are therefore examined behaviours, not just admin. The AOs translate into ESA practice as follows: AO1 - contextual research into artists relevant to the chosen starting point, analysed critically and used to inform the response; AO2 - media trials during prep and confident, purposeful handling in the supervised hours; AO3 - observational recording and reflective annotation throughout; AO4 - a resolved, personal final outcome that answers the starting point and connects with the preparatory evidence.

The conduct rules are mark-critical and easy to lose marks against:

  • Stop prep when supervised time starts. You must stop work on preparatory work as soon as the first period of supervised time begins. You may refer to preparatory work in the supervised hours, but it must not be added to or amended.
  • Security. Preparatory work and supervised work must be kept secure between sessions of supervised time, and all submitted work kept secure at the end of the 15 hours.
  • Identification. The work produced during supervised time must be clearly identified as such - label it, because the moderator must be able to see what was made when.
  • Sources. You must identify and acknowledge sources which are not your own, in prep as in Component 1.
  • No internet in the 15 hours. You must not have access to the internet during supervised time. Access to web-based applications (for example Adobe Creative Cloud) is permitted, but all other internet access must be disabled - so download references, fonts and textures before the first session.
  • Unaided. The 15 hours are unaided and follow the JCQ 'Instructions for the conduct of examinations'.
  • Annotation. Annotation and notes during supervised time should use appropriate specialist vocabulary and be legible with accurate use of language.

Raw marks scale by 2: Component 2's 96 becomes 192 of the 480 total scaled mark, against Component 1's 288. Other boards mirror the structure: OCR (H601) Component 02 Externally set task - 80 marks, 40%, 15 hours supervised; Pearson Edexcel (9AD0/02) - externally set assignment, 40%, with 15 hours supervised time; WJEC/Eduqas - externally set assignment, 40%, 15 hours. The eight-question paper format and 1 February release are AQA's specifics; the 15-hour supervised structure is common to the boards. Note the contrast with GCSE, where supervised time is typically 10 hours - the A-level gives you half as much again, and expects a more developed outcome within it.

The strategic consequence of 60/40 is easy to state and easy to forget in spring: Component 1 is worth one and a half times Component 2. A common Year 13 error is to over-invest in ESA prep from February and let the Personal investigation's final outcome drift. The ESA is time-boxed and rules-bound; the Personal investigation is open-ended. Finish the open-ended one first.

What the ESA is really testing is transfer. The spec frames both components as synoptic: Component 2 asks you to draw together everything learned across the course - contextual analysis, media control, observational recording, curation - and apply it to a stimulus you did not choose, under constraints you cannot negotiate. That is why the strongest ESA submissions come from students who reuse their Component 1 skillset deliberately: the same analysis framework applied to new artists, the same media-audit discipline applied to the starting point, the same annotation method carried into supervised hours. Treat the ESA as Component 1 compressed - the personal voice must survive the compression, which is why the board emphasises a personal and meaningful response.

How marking works within the component. Your centre marks preparatory work and supervised work together against the four AOs, each out of 24, using the same six-band grid as Component 1; AQA's moderator samples and can adjust. Two marking consequences follow. First, prep is not 'practice' - it is half the evidence, so a thin preparatory period caps every AO before you sit down. Second, because the supervised work must be clearly identified as such, the moderator must be able to reconstruct what was made when; label sessions, keep the work secure between them, and make the storyline - research to trials to plan to outcome - readable from the sequence of pages alone.

Use past ESA papers as your main practice material. AQA publishes previous years' eight-question papers, and they are the closest thing this coursework-based subject has to a past paper. Work them as a timed exercise: given one hour, read all eight starting points of a previous year, run the four-part test, choose one, and sketch a one-page plan - artists, media, compositional direction, outcome, 15-hour time budget. Repeat across three or four years and the selection skill becomes reflexive, which is precisely what the first week of February demands. The papers also reveal the house style of starting points: single words, short quotations and open questions that invite personal interpretation rather than illustration - a pattern worth knowing before the paper arrives.

The Preparatory Period: From 1 February to the First Supervised Hour

The preparatory period is where ESA marks are won or lost, because the 15 supervised hours can only execute what prep has already planned. The spec says students should consider the starting points and select one - so begin with a deliberate choice, not a reflex. Read all eight questions on day one. For each, run the four-part test you used for the Personal investigation: access (can you gather strong primary material in the six to eight weeks available?), contextual depth (are there artists you already understand who genuinely speak to it?), media range (does it invite your strongest processes?), outcome potential (can you visualise a resolved outcome a moderator would recognise as finished?). Choose the question you can evidence most completely, not the one that sounds most interesting - and choose fast, because every week of prep is a week of evidence.

Convert the chosen starting point into a personal interpretation. An ESA question such as fragments or layers is a trigger, not a title; the moderator is assessing whether you developed a personal and meaningful response. Write your interpretation in one sentence at the top of your first prep sheet - I will investigate how layered translucency records the experience of revisiting a demolished place - and let every subsequent prep page serve it. Mind-map visually rather than verbally: the map should generate imagery, artists and processes, not synonyms.

Prep must do four jobs, one per AO, before the first supervised hour:

  • AO1 - research. Two to four artists relevant to the starting point, analysed for methods, contexts and ideas, with transcription studies and experiments in response. Gallery visits if possible; otherwise high-quality reproductions with acknowledged sources.
  • AO2 - trials. Media and process experiments that test the approaches you intend to use in the outcome, with review notes deciding what the outcome will be made in and how.
  • AO3 - recording. First-hand observational drawing and photography of primary material relevant to the starting point, plus critical reflection on what the recordings suggest.
  • AO4 - planning. Compositional studies, colour studies, maquettes or design sheets that resolve the outcome's format, scale and structure - and a written time plan for the 15 hours.

The time plan is the single highest-value prep sheet. It allocates the 15 hours in advance: for example, hours 1-2 composition and ground; hours 3-7 main development; hours 8-11 detail and secondary passages; hours 12-13 layering and adjustment; hours 14-15 resolution, edges, signature area and final annotation. Annotate it onto the last prep sheet where the moderator can see it, because it demonstrates the working within the specified time constraints the spec explicitly assesses. Rehearse against it: know what you will be doing in hour eight before you sit down.

Prep formats are flexible - mounted sheets, design sheets, sketchbooks, workbooks, journals, models and maquettes - and most successful submissions combine them: a sketchbook for flow and reflection, mounted sheets for the strongest studies, a maquette if the outcome is 3D or installed. What matters is that the prep tells a continuous story ending in a plan the supervised hours can execute. Sequence it, date it, and end it with a page that says: this is what I will make, in this media, at this scale, in this order.

The stopping rule is absolute: preparatory work stops the moment the first supervised session starts. Anything added afterwards is a conduct breach and risks the component's award. In practice this means the last week of prep is closure, not expansion: finish the time plan, finish the compositional studies, photograph and print any references you will need (the no-internet rule means nothing can be downloaded in the 15 hours), prepare grounds and stretch paper where the centre permits, and pack a kit list. Common prep failures, all mapped to lost bands: choosing a starting point late and thinning the research; copying artists rather than analysing them; trials that explore media with no decision about the outcome; no compositional resolution, so the first supervised hours are spent hesitating; and no time plan, so the 15 hours are spent evenly rather than strategically. Each is fixable in the six to eight weeks the preparatory period gives you - if you treat it as the exam, because in this subject, it is.

Analysing artists under time pressure. The prep window is short, so compress the analysis framework rather than skipping it. For each artist, force four sentences: method (what materials and processes, handled how); context (when, where, for whom - what the work responded to); idea (what the work is about, and how the method carries the idea); application (what you take from it, and the trial that follows). Two artists analysed this way, with transcription studies and experiments in response, generate more AO1 evidence than six artists pasted with commentary. Select artists fast using the Tate's collection and art-terms pages, gallery exhibition listings, or monographs - and pick artists whose methods you can actually trial in the centre, because the strongest AO1-AO2 link is an experiment that visibly applies an analysed method.

The mind-map should generate work, not words. Verbal mind-maps produce synonyms; visual ones produce artists, processes and imagery. From the chosen starting point, branch into: primary material you can gather; artists per branch; processes per branch; and compositional possibilities. Keep the map in the portfolio - it is the visible record of the independent selection the spec rewards - and date the branches so the moderator can see decisions accumulating.

Run prep to a week-by-week schedule. A realistic model across eight weeks: week one - read the paper, select the starting point, write the sentence of intent, mind-map; week two - primary recording (photography, observational drawing, collected objects) and first artist analysis; week three - second and third artist analysis with transcription studies and experiments in response; week four - media trials round one, with review notes; week five - refinement of the strongest trial routes, second-generation experiments; week six - compositional studies, colour studies or maquettes resolving the outcome; week seven - the time plan, kit list, grounds prepared, references printed; week eight - closure: final prep pages, labelling, secure storage, and a rehearsal walk-through of the planned 15 hours. Students who freestyle the preparatory period consistently arrive at supervised time with research but no plan - and the 15 hours then convert a plan-shaped deficit into a mark.

Primary sources beat secondary ones in prep, as in Component 1. The starting point usually connects to something you can access first-hand - objects, places, people, archives - and first-hand material feeds stronger AO3 evidence than screen-scraped images. Photograph your primary material well (light, scale reference, multiple viewpoints), draw from it directly, and note what the photographs cannot show. Where a gallery visit is possible, work it as primary research: one work studied in person for twenty minutes beats fifty thumbnails. Secondary sources - monographs, gallery websites, journals - then supply context the primary material cannot, with sources acknowledged from the first week so the bibliography assembles itself.

The 15 Supervised Hours: Strategy, Rules and Technique

The 15 hours are unaided, supervised, and the first three must be consecutive - plan that opening session like a launch. Before it: grounds prepared, references printed, materials counted, kit list checked, time plan memorised. In it: settle the composition. The classic failure of supervised time is spending the first session redrawing the plan; the moderator expects to see the outcome's structure established in the first three hours because prep already resolved it. Hours 1-3 are for transferring the chosen composition, setting scale, blocking the ground and fixing the format - so that every later hour adds, rather than searches.

Run the remaining 12 hours against the time plan, but know where the flex is. A workable pattern for a painted outcome: hours 4-8 main development - the largest, most demanding passages while concentration is fresh; hours 9-12 secondary passages, detail, texture and adjustment; hours 13-14 layering, glazing or reworking weak areas; hour 15 resolution - edges, unifying passages, the final annotation. For a constructed or installed outcome, front-load construction (materials and joins fail slowly) and reserve the final hours for surface and presentation. For a print or photographic outcome, front-load the technical work - exposure, processing, proofs - because failures there consume recovery time you will not have. The principle in every medium: risk early, refine late. An hour spent troubleshooting in hour three is recoverable; the same hour in hour fourteen is not.

Annotation during supervised time is worth reclaiming minutes for. The spec requires that annotation and notes use appropriate specialist vocabulary and be legible with accurate language, and AO3 explicitly rewards recording insights and reflecting critically on work and progress. Three disciplined notes per session - what was attempted, what changed, what the next session must achieve - cost five minutes and give the moderator the independent decision-making evidence this component is distinctively assessed on. Write them as judgements, not logs: the glaze flattened the form, so I scraped back and rebuilt with an impasto neutral shows reflection; applied glaze shows only activity. Keep a small annotation sheet in the kit list so the habit survives fatigue in hour eleven.

You may refer to preparatory work, but not add to or amend it - so the boundary is visible: prep is evidence of planning, supervised work is evidence of independent realisation. Label all supervised work clearly identified as such; annotating it with the session number and hour helps the moderator, and that annotation itself should use appropriate specialist vocabulary and be legible. Brief reflective notes during supervised time - session 3, hours 7-9: glaze too saturated; scraped back and rebalanced with complementary neutral - are worth marks under AO3 and cost minutes.

The no-internet rule needs practical planning: you must not have access to the internet during the 15 hours, though web-based applications such as Adobe Creative Cloud are permitted with all other internet access disabled. If you work digitally, export everything - reference images, brushes, fonts, previous files - before the first session and work offline throughout. If you work traditionally, print your references in colour at the scale you will need. Nothing in the rules prevents referring to printed materials; what is prohibited is live access.

What the moderator is looking for in the supervised work mirrors the AO bands: confident, purposeful execution (AO2); a resolved outcome that visibly realises the prep's plan and answers the starting point (AO4); recording and reflection continuing inside the hours (AO3); and independent decision-making under constraint (the component's distinctive emphasis). The outcome must be informed by preparatory work - a final piece that ignores the prep breaks the storyline the two are assessed as; equally, a final piece that merely photocopies the prep's strongest study wastes the 15 hours. The strongest submissions develop beyond the plan: the composition holds, but scale, surface or colour resolve further than the studies managed, and the final annotation explains those decisions.

Do the arithmetic on what 40% means. Two bands on one AO in Component 2 is 8 raw marks, which scale to 16 of 480 - roughly 3.3% of the qualification, close to a typical grade boundary. Conversely, because the AOs are equally weighted, a candidate strong in three AOs but capped by a weak final outcome loses exactly a quarter of the component. This is why the final hour matters as much as the first: AO4's 24 marks are awarded to the response that realises intentions, and an unresolved edge or an unfinished passage reads as unrealised intentions. Budget resolution time with the same seriousness as making time.

Common supervised-time failures: treating the hours as ten silent hours plus five for panic; re-composing in session one; running out of a key material in hour nine; leaving resolution to minutes that do not exist; forgetting to label supervised work; and amending prep during a break between sessions - a conduct breach that risks the whole component. The countermeasures are all in the prep: the time plan, the kit list, the compositional resolution, and the discipline of finishing. At the end of the 15 hours all submitted work is kept secure and then presented - prep and outcome together, in sequence, with the supervised work identified - for centre marking and AQA moderation. Your last hour is not for the artwork; it is for the evidence.

Digital candidates need an offline workflow. Because internet access must be disabled during the 15 hours (web-based applications such as Adobe Creative Cloud are permitted), everything must be exported in advance: reference images at working resolution, fonts, brushes, textures, and every file version the outcome depends on. Practice the export before the first session, keep files named by session and hour - esa_outcome_s4.jpg - and annotate digitally in the same specialist vocabulary required on paper. Save incrementally; a corrupted file in hour ten is not an excuse the board recognises, and version history is your only undo.

Presentation closes the component. At the end of the 15 hours all submitted work is kept secure, then presented for centre marking and AQA moderation: preparatory work in sequence, the supervised outcome(s) clearly identified, annotations legible, sources acknowledged, and the Candidate record form completed. The final presentation should let the moderator read the entire storyline - starting point, interpretation, research, trials, plan, outcome - from the order of the work alone. Mount the prep sheets in the order they were made, label the supervised work, and place the time plan where it can be seen. In a coursework-based subject the presentation is not packaging; it is the last assessed page.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: Select one of the eight starting points on a past AQA 7202/X paper and explain how you would use the preparatory period to develop a personal and meaningful response, then how you would structure the 15 hours of supervised time to produce a finished outcome. (96-mark component, each AO out of 24 - AQA 7202/X, 40% of A-level)
Mark-scheme logic: Mark-scheme logic (AO1-AO4, prep and supervised work assessed as a whole). Selection and personalisation: all eight starting points considered against access, contextual depth, media range and outcome potential; one selected and reinterpreted in a personal sentence of intent - the paper is a trigger for an independent response, not a brief to illustrate. AO1 hits: two to four relevant artists analysed for methods, contexts and ideas, with transcription studies and experiments in response; sources acknowledged. AO2 hits: media and process trials testing the intended approaches; review notes deciding media, scale and format of the outcome. AO3 hits: first-hand observational recording of primary material; critical reflection on what the recordings suggest. AO4 hits: compositional studies, colour studies or maquettes resolving the outcome; written time plan allocating the 15 hours. Supervised-time strategy: first three hours (consecutive) establish composition and ground; main development hours 4-8; detail and adjustment 9-12; resolution 14-15; risk early, refine late; work unaided within the time constraints; prep referred to but never amended; supervised work clearly identified; no internet (references pre-printed); annotation in specialist vocabulary; outcome informed by prep and developed beyond it; work presented with prep in sequence for moderation.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

State the structure and assessment requirements of AQA Component 2 (7202/X). (6 marks)
Answer: The question paper offers eight questions as starting points and you select one (1); papers are provided on 1 February, or as soon as possible after (1). The preparatory period runs from receipt of the paper until supervised time begins; preparatory work may be presented in any suitable format such as mounted sheets, design sheets, sketchbooks, workbooks, journals, models and maquettes (1). The 15 hours of unaided supervised time follow, with the first three hours consecutive, and you must produce a finished outcome or a series of related finished outcomes informed by the preparatory work (1). Preparatory work and supervised work are assessed together as a whole against all four AOs (24 marks each, 96 total, 40% of the A-level), marked by the centre and moderated by AQA (1). Rules: prep stops when supervised time starts and may be referred to but not amended; work is kept secure between sessions; supervised work is clearly identified; sources acknowledged; no internet during the 15 hours (1).
Explain how you would select one starting point from an ESA paper and justify why your chosen question offers the strongest mark potential. (10 marks)
Answer: Selection process: read all eight questions on the day the paper is received (1); run the four-part test on each - access to primary material within the preparatory weeks, contextual depth of artists you can analyse, fit with your strongest media, and a visualisable finished outcome (2); shortlist two and choose the one you can evidence most completely (1). Justification example: a starting point such as 'layers' is chosen not for its sound but because it connects to primary material you can gather (family photographs, urban peel-back posters), to artists you already critically understand (for example collage and translucent-construction practice), and to a media route you have already trialled, so AO2 experiments begin from evidence rather than guesswork (2). The chosen question is converted into a personal interpretation in one written sentence of intent, because the board assesses a personal and meaningful response, not an illustrated brief (2). Fast selection protects prep time: every week spent hesitating is a week of research, trials and compositional resolution lost (1).
Explain what preparatory work must contain to evidence all four assessment objectives before the first supervised hour. (12 marks)
Answer: AO1: contextual research into two to four relevant artists, analysed for methods, contexts and ideas rather than copied, with transcription studies and experiments in response, and sources acknowledged (3). AO2: media and process trials testing the approaches intended for the outcome, with review notes that decide the outcome's media, scale and format - exploration plus explicit selection (3). AO3: first-hand observational drawing and photography of primary material relevant to the starting point, plus critical reflection on what the recordings suggest for the response (3). AO4: compositional studies, colour studies, maquettes or design sheets resolving the outcome's structure, culminating in a written time plan that allocates the 15 supervised hours (3). The prep must end as a continuous story - research, trials, recordings, resolution - because preparatory work and the supervised outcome are assessed together as one whole, and the outcome must be informed by the prep.
'The first three hours of supervised time must be consecutive.' Explain the purpose of this rule and how you would use those hours. (8 marks)
Answer: Purpose: the consecutive opening block forces the outcome's structure to be established early, under sustained concentration, so the remaining twelve hours add development rather than search for it (2); it also secures standardised conditions - every candidate begins the realisation in a comparable way, which supports fair moderation of work produced under time constraints (1). Use of the hours: transfer the composition resolved in prep onto the final support at final scale; block in the ground and the largest tonal or spatial relationships; fix format and proportions before detail (2). In practice: grounds and references are prepared in advance so none of the three hours is spent on setup (1); the time plan has already allocated these hours to structure, so no re-composing occurs (1). The pattern generalises: risk early, refine late - an error corrected in hour two is recoverable, an error discovered in hour fourteen is not (1).
A student amends a preparatory collage during a break between supervised sessions 'to keep it consistent with the outcome'. Explain why this breaches the rules and what should be done instead. (8 marks)
Answer: The spec requires students to stop work on preparatory work as soon as the first period of supervised time starts: preparatory work may be referred to during supervised time but must not be added to or amended (2). Amending it between sessions is a conduct breach because prep must remain evidence of planning made before supervised time - altering it afterwards destroys the distinction the assessment relies on, and the JCQ conduct guidelines apply (2). It also distorts the assessed whole: prep and supervised work are assessed together, so artificially improved prep misrepresents the development story (1). Instead: freeze prep the moment supervised time begins (1); if a genuine mismatch emerges between prep and outcome, record it as reflective annotation on the supervised work - explaining what changed and why is itself AO3 evidence and demonstrates independent decision-making (1); keep both prep and supervised work secure between sessions, and label the supervised work clearly as such (1). The storyline should show development, not retro-fitting.
