Materials, Techniques and Processes

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC/Eduqas

A-Level Art materials, techniques and processes revision: drawing media, oil/acrylic/watercolour painting methods, printmaking, 3D construction, digital and photography - with the AO2 explore-select-refine evidence that earns top marks in the coursework portfolio.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Name the drawing and painting media, their handling properties and the techniques each supports, and match each to a purpose - observation, analysis, expression or imagination - as the AQA 7202 skills list requires.
  • Distinguish the four printmaking families (relief, intaglio, screen, lithography) by where the ink sits, plus mono printing, and describe the method of each.
  • Distinguish the 3D methods - modelling, carving, casting, constructing, assembling, welding - as additive or subtractive, and outline digital and photographic processes.
  • Explain how AO2 is awarded: exploring and selecting appropriate resources, media, materials, techniques and processes, then reviewing and refining ideas as work develops.
  • Generate visible AO2 evidence: trials pages, sampling, review notes written as decisions, and refinement that escalates the investigation rather than repeating it.
  • Work safely and purposefully with the studio hazards of each medium - solvents, dust, blades, heat and presses.

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Fat over lean: The oil-painting rule that each successive layer contains more oil (fatter medium) than the one below; breaking it makes underlayers dry first and crack the paint film.
Gesso: A primer (traditionally chalk and animal glue, today acrylic polymer) applied to a support to seal and abrade the surface so paint grips; the standard ground for panels and canvas.
Impasto: Paint applied thickly so it stands off the surface in relief, retaining the mark of the tool; Van Gogh's loaded directional strokes are the canonical case.
Glazing: A thin, transparent layer of paint laid over a dried layer so underlying colour glows through; the Renaissance method of building depth and luminosity from a dark ground.
Scumbling: A broken, semi-opaque layer dragged lightly over a dried layer so patches of the underlayer show through; softens and unifies passages.
Relief print: Printmaking from a raised surface - the block (lino, wood) is cut away where no ink is wanted, ink sits on what remains, and the image prints in reverse.
Intaglio: Printmaking from an incised surface - lines are cut (engraving, drypoint) or bitten with acid (etching) into a metal plate; ink sits in the grooves and is forced out under a press.
Mono print: A one-off print made by transferring paint or ink from a surface to paper - by rolling, drawing on the back, or stencilling; combines printmaking's surface with drawing's gesture.

Drawing and Painting: Media, Methods and Their Purposes

The AQA 7202 Fine Art skills list frames drawing as a set of different approaches to recording images, such as observation, analysis, expression and imagination - four purposes, four drawing behaviours. Observational drawing records what is there (measured, sustained, from primary material). Analytical drawing takes things apart (structural studies, anatomical analysis, plans and diagrams). Expressive drawing performs feeling (pressure, speed, distorted scale). Imaginative drawing invents (compositions that combine recorded elements into things never seen). Your portfolio should show all four, and the medium should follow the purpose: hard graphite (2H-4H) and fine liner for observational precision; soft graphite (2B-8B), charcoal and conte for tonal and expressive range; ink, biro and scratchy tools for texture and line quality; and mixed media where imagination needs it. Scales matter too - the spec expects drawing on a variety of scales, so pair sketchbook studies with at least one large-scale sustained drawing per investigation.

Drawing media and their behaviour. Graphite grades run 9H (hardest, lightest) to 9B (softest, darkest): hard grades hold an edge and detail; soft grades build rich tone but smear - fixative or fixation discipline required. Charcoal (willow and compressed) gives the deepest blacks and the fastest tonal coverage, works lifted and reworked with a kneaded eraser, but is fragile - fix, or work on toned paper with white chalk highlights. Conté (chalk and pigment) offers controlled tone in a limited palette - sanguine, black, white - ideal for figure studies on toned grounds. Ink (dip pen, brush, technical pen) gives permanence and line character: hatching and cross-hatching build tone from line density. Biro rewards accumulated pressure-borne tone but fades and shiny-slicks; ballpoint layering suits dense texture studies. Each medium choice is an AO2 decision, so annotate it: charcoal chosen for speed of tonal revision; graphite below for the measured structure.

Painting media: know the properties, not just the names. Oil dries slowly (days), blends and reworks wet-in-wet (alla prima), supports glazing and impasto extremes, and obeys fat over lean: successive layers carry progressively more medium, or underlayers dry first and crack. The Renaissance method - dark ground, warm underpainting, glazes of transparent pigment - builds luminosity no other medium matches. Acrylic dries fast (minutes), which forbids wet blending but rewards layering: thin underlayers, rapid overpainting, and mediums (gels, pastes, retarders, flow improvers) that extend the working window or build texture. Acrylic is the medium of collage-like construction and of fast compositional trial. Watercolour is transparent and unforgiving: white comes from the paper (reserve), tone comes from dilution. Its techniques are wash (flat, graded), wet-on-wet (bleeds and blooms), wet-on-dry (controlled edges), granulation (pigment pooling in paper tooth) and masking fluid. Gouache - opaque watercolour - corrects and flattens. Know what each medium forbids: oil forbids lean-over-fat; watercolour forbids retrieval (light cannot be recovered); acrylic forbids slow blending. Naming the constraint is analysis; choosing around it is AO2.

Supports and grounds are part of the technique, not the backdrop. Paper weights and tooth (smooth hot-press for detail, rough cold-press for granulation); canvas (linen, cotton) stretched or on board; panels (MDF, plywood) primed with gesso and sanded between coats. A ground can be a decision: toned mid-grey grounds enable both deep shadow and white highlight in one drawing; coloured grounds (Van Gogh's oranges, Holbein's pinks) shift every colour laid over them. Annotate grounds as choices - gessoed panel for impasto; toned grey paper so white chalk reads as light - because the moderator reads grounds as evidence of exploring and selecting resources.

Colour decisions are technical decisions. Complementary contrast (orange/blue, red/green) produces simultaneous vibration at equal intensity - useful for focal points, dangerous for harmony. Temperature (warm advance, cool recession) models space without perspective. Limited palettes (a triad, or primaries plus white) force mixing discipline and unify a surface - a defensible AO2 selection when harmony matters. Underpainting in a complementary shifts the overpainted colour's temperature. Each of these is a named technique with a named effect - the vocabulary the top AO2 band expects - and each becomes evidence the moment it is annotated as a decision with a reason.

Match medium to meaning, and say so. The examiner's question of every media choice is not was it difficult? but why this medium for this idea? Impasto where the surface should record pressure and feeling; glazing where depth and luminosity are the subject; watercolour where transience and chance feed the concept; acrylic layering where time-stratification matches the investigation. A media decision without a stated reason is a trial; a media decision with a reason is evidence. Every annotated decision in your portfolio should read: medium, method, effect, reason - four words that turn materials into marks.

Drawing technique worth naming. The processes the examiner recognises: measuring and sighting (pencil at arm's length, comparative angles), the envelope or block-in (largest shapes first, detail last), hatching and cross-hatching (tone built from line density - the Durer and Ingres tradition), tonal massing (charcoal blocks erased into light), gestural starts (30-second responses for movement), and sustained observational studies (an hour or more from the same position, which is the discipline AO3 rewards). Perspective vocabulary: one- and two-point perspective, aerial (atmospheric) perspective - warm advance, cool fade, detail loss with distance - and foreshortening. Each is a named technique with a named effect; each becomes evidence once annotated. Note what the spec's list implies for assessment: observing from the real world and recording from observation are named expectations, so primary observation - drawn from the thing itself, not from a screen - is a marked behaviour, and a portfolio that cannot show sustained first-hand drawing caps AO3 whatever its concept.

Painting history gives the techniques their authority. The Renaissance glaze method - dark ground, green-earth underpainting, layered transparent pigment - is how Van Eyck and Leonardo achieved depth no opaque layering matches; cite it when you glaze. Van Gogh's impasto - directional loaded strokes that record pressure and speed - makes the surface perform feeling; cite it when you load. Rothko's thin, layered fields - veil over veil until colour breathes - are the modern glaze; Turner's washes and scumbles are the tonal version. Referencing a named precedent for each technique you use converts media exploration into AO1 and AO2 simultaneously: glazing in this trial follows the Renaissance method over a warm ground - and failed because my ground was too absorbent, so the next trial seals with gesso first. Technique, precedent, result, decision: the four-beat sentence that makes a media page evidence.

Printmaking, 3D, Digital and Photography

The 7202 spec names the families explicitly: relief, intaglio, screen processes and lithography, plus etching, engraving, drypoint, mono printing, lino printing, screen printing, photo silkscreen - and the way to hold them is by where the ink sits. Relief: the surface is cut away (lino, wood), ink sits on what remains, and the image prints in reverse - plan reversed lettering and remember that cutting is subtractive and permanent; registration (aligning paper to block) matters for multi-block colour. Intaglio: lines are incised into metal - cut with a burin (engraving), scratched with a point (drypoint, which leaves a burr that gives a soft fuzzy line), or bitten with acid through a ground (etching; aquatint bites tone rather than line) - ink sits in the grooves, the surface is wiped clean, and a press forces damp paper into the cuts. Screen (serigraphy): ink is pushed through a mesh; areas are blocked by stencil or photo-emulsion (photo silkscreen, the Warhol method - photographs burned onto the screen, flat colour printed through). Lithography: grease and water repel; drawing in greasy crayon on stone or plate holds ink while damp areas refuse it. Mono print: a one-off transfer - ink on a slab printed by press or hand-rubbing, or paper laid over inked glass and drawn on the back - combining print surface with drawing gesture; the fastest way to a portfolio-quality print evidence page.

Three-dimensional methods divide into additive and subtractive. Modelling is additive - clay, wax or plasticine built up by hand; armatures (wire skeletons) support larger forms. Carving is subtractive - stone, wood or plaster reduced to the form; irreversible, so plan from maquettes and measure twice. Casting converts a negative mould into a positive - plaster into a clay-pressed or alginate mould; the classic clay-to-plaster school route. Construction assembles rigid materials (card, wood, metal) by joining; assemblage gathers found objects into a new whole (the Cornelia Parker and Picasso-guitar lineage); welding fuses metal. Every 3D route should pass through a maquette - a small test model that resolves form, proportion and construction before material is committed; maquettes are AO2 and AO4 evidence simultaneously. Annotate the transition: maquette showed the cantilever failed in card, so the final form adds a strut - resolved before committing to timber.

Digital processes are legitimate 7202 media when they evidence exploration and refinement: photo editing (exposure, contrast, colour, compositing), digital drawing and painting (pressure-sensitive tablets, layered files), vector work for clean geometry, and moving image for time-based investigations. The technical vocabulary to command: layers (independent stacked elements, non-destructive editing), masks, blend modes, resolution and file formats, and the difference between raster (pixels) and vector (mathematical paths). Digital work earns marks the same way manual work does: annotate the process - layers composited at low opacity to test the translucent installation's effect before committing to material - so the moderator sees decisions, not software. Remember the Component 2 condition: web-based applications are permitted in supervised time, but internet access must be disabled - export everything first.

Photography is both a recording tool (AO3 evidence) and a medium in its own right (an area of study: moving image and photography). As a medium, command the exposure triangle: aperture (f-number - width of the lens opening; wide aperture f/1.8 gives shallow depth of field and isolation), shutter speed (duration of exposure; slow shutter records movement as blur, fast freezes it), ISO (sensor sensitivity; high ISO brightens but adds noise). Composition borrows the formal elements: rule of thirds, leading lines, negative space, framing, and the cropped asymmetry borrowed from ukiyo-e and Degas. Light is the medium's true subject: direction (side light models form), quality (hard sun, soft overcast), and colour temperature (golden hour, cool shade). Processing - darkroom or digital - is a technique with named effects: burning and dodging, contrast grading, high-key and low-key printing. Every photographic choice annotated as aperture, shutter, reason converts a snapshot into evidence.

Named precedents give each process its authority, as in painting. Printmaking: Durer's engravings (Melencolia I, 1514) show intaglio line at its analytical peak; Hockney's etchings show drawing translated to the plate; Warhol's photo silkscreens (Marilyn Diptych, 1962) show the screen process carrying Pop's mechanical-repetition idea - flat colour through a burned photograph, so the process itself performs the idea of mass media. Picasso's transfer drawings and single-sheet monoprints show print gesture fused with drawing. Three dimensions: Michelangelo's David (1501-04) is subtractive carving planned from maquettes; Rodin's modelling builds form additively in clay before casting; Cornelia Parker's Cold Dark Matter (1991) is assemblage - a garden shed exploded and suspended - where found objects carry the concept. Photography: Cartier-Bresson's decisive moment (one exposure timed to the event), Ansel Adams's zone-system exposure and burning/dodging, Sugimoto's long exposures (theatre screens burned to white by cumulative light). Each is a named work where a named process carries a named idea - the standard your own process evidence should meet, because process carries the idea is the sentence that links AO2 to AO1 in every medium.

Sequencing process evidence in the portfolio. Whatever media the investigation uses, the pages must show the process being learned, then applied, then refined - three stages the moderator reads as competence. For printmaking: a technical page (tool tests, pressure tests, proofing), an applied page (the technique inside an investigation composition), a refined page (registration improved, edition quality raised, colour reduced or layered differently). For 3D: material tests (joins, weights, armatures), the maquette, then the construction stages photographed and annotated as they happen. For photography: contact sheets showing selection, annotated exposure decisions, then the refined prints - because contact sheets are the photographer's trials pages, and unedited rolls read as snapshots. For digital: layered file screenshots, iteration versions, then the final - with the non-destructive workflow visible. In every case the three-stage pattern (learned, applied, refined) is what converts a process from an activity into AO2 evidence, and the annotations that name each stage are what the moderator can actually mark.

Processes across media are the spec's connective tissue. The knowledge requirement is a working vocabulary and specialist terminology relevant to the chosen area - so build a terminology bank from whatever media your investigation touches: for painting, underpainting, glaze, scumble, impasto, fat over lean, ground; for print, registration, burr, edition, proof, reduction; for 3D, armature, maquette, subtractive, release agent; for photography, aperture, ISO, burning, dodge. Fifteen to twenty terms used accurately and annotated as decisions is the fluency the top AO2 band names. And the connective move that scores: transfer a technique between media deliberately - a lino-cut's flat planar colour reinterpreted in acrylic layering; a photographic crop driving a drawing's composition - because cross-media translation is visible refinement, and visible refinement is what AO2's top band is written to reward.

Media Exploration, Selection and Refinement: Building AO2 Evidence

AO2 reads: explore and select appropriate resources, media, materials, techniques and processes, reviewing and refining ideas as work develops. Three verbs, three behaviours: explore (trial a genuine range), select (justify what you choose against your intentions), review and refine (evaluate as you go, and let the evaluation change the work). The band descriptors reward exactly this progression: top band (21-24) - exceptional ability to explore and select appropriate resources, media, materials, techniques and processes; reviews and refines ideas in a confident and purposeful manner as work develops; the lower bands step down through confident (17-20), consistent (13-16), reasonably consistent (9-12), some (5-8) to minimal (1-4) - minimal ability to explore and select; minimal evidence of reviewing and refining. Note what the top band does NOT say: it does not say many media - it says appropriate. Six shallow trials with no selection score below three deep trials with justified selection and visible refinement.

The trials page is the unit of AO2 evidence. A strong trials page contains: the question it is testing (from the investigation's needs or an analysed artist's method); two to four samples in different media or variants of one process; a comparison against intention; and a decision. Layout: samples side by side so the comparison is visual, not remembered; annotation as decision prose - monoprint gives the eroded surface the concept needs; charcoal smears under the translucency test - rejected; next trial: acrylic layers on gessoed panel. Date every page. A portfolio of eight dated, decision-bearing trials pages, escalating toward the final outcome, is the visible shape of the top AO2 band; a portfolio of one sketchbook page per medium with no decisions is the visible shape of the middle bands.

Refinement is not repetition. The moderator distinguishes refinement (each trial changed by the previous one's findings) from repetition (ten versions of the same idea). The test: does trial three exist because of trial two's failure? If you cannot point to the finding that caused the change, the work is repeating. Refinement also operates inside a single medium: first glaze too saturated - scraped back; second glaze in a leaner mix; third glaze over a warmer underpainting - a sequence of three versions of one technique, each annotated with what changed and why, is dense AO2 evidence. Sequence and date make the causality legible; annotation makes the reasoning legible; both together make the mark.

Review notes must be decisions, not diaries. The wording that earns: I am abandoning X because...; I am escalating Y by...; the next experiment must prove.... The wording that wastes: today I tried X and it went well. A review note answers three questions: what did the trial show, what does that mean for the investigation, what happens next. Run a structured review at each phase transition - a full page evaluating the trials so far against the initial intentions, naming what survives into the next phase - and cross-reference it in the written element. The spec's written-element requirements ask the prose to substantiate decisions leading to the development and refinement of ideas: the review notes are where those decisions are recorded, the essay is where they are substantiated, and the trials are where they are proved.

Selection is a judgement about appropriateness. Selection answers: which media and processes best carry this investigation's intentions, at this scale, in this time? The justification should weigh: expressive fit (does the medium's behaviour match the idea?), technical control (can you execute it to the standard the outcome needs?), time cost (can the ESA outcome be made in 15 hours?), and availability. Discard the trials that fail the test - visibly, with reasons - and say what the survivors inherit: acrylic layering survives: it stratifies time like the concept requires, it is controllable at scale, and it dries fast enough for the 15 hours. A selection justified on those four criteria reads as confident and purposeful - the top band's exact words - because it shows the choice was a judgement, not a habit.

Link AO2 outward. Two cross-links multiply its value. AO2 to AO1: derive a trial from an analysed artist's method - Hockney's joiners imply layered photographic collage; trial follows - and the same page evidences both objectives. AO2 to AO4: make the last trials page a resolution page - the final media, scale and format decided - so selection visibly delivers the final outcome. Between those two links, refinement becomes the spine of the whole portfolio: research proposes trials, trials produce decisions, decisions shape the outcome, the outcome justifies the selection. That closed loop - explore, select, refine, realise, and round again - is what reviewing and refining ideas as work develops means in practice, and it is the single most efficient structure to build into the work before moderation.

A worked trials page, at the standard the top band expects. Question stated at the top: which process gives the eroded, layered surface the coastline concept needs? Samples side by side, dated: (1) charcoal on cartridge - fast tonal mass, but smears under the collage test; (2) monoprint on newsprint - the roller's texture mimics erosion, but too thin to layer; (3) acrylic on gessoed panel, three layers with sanding between - controllable, durable, layers visibly; (4) gesso on board with oil glaze - luminous depth, but too slow for the 15 supervised hours. Comparison against intention, then the decision: acrylic layering on gessoed panel selected - it stratifies time as the concept requires, is controllable at scale, and dries within supervised time; the monoprint's eroded texture will be recovered in the final piece by sanding between layers. One page, and it evidences exploration (four processes), selection (justified on four criteria), refinement (findings carried forward into the decision), vocabulary (gesso, glaze, tonal mass) and the AO4 link (media fixed for the outcome). This page is the atom of AO2; multiply it by eight and sequence it toward the final outcome.

Common AO2 failures, and the fixes. First, range without selection - six trials, no decision: fix by ending every trials page with a justified choice. Second, selection without exploration - the same medium throughout with no alternative tested: fix by running at least one honest comparison against intention. Third, diaries instead of decisions - today I tried prose: fix by writing I am abandoning X because, the next experiment must prove. Fourth, repetition instead of refinement - ten versions with no visible cause: fix by dating everything and naming the finding that caused each change. Fifth, disconnection - media trials unrelated to the concept or to the analysed artists: fix by deriving trials from the investigation's needs and the researched methods. Sixth, unsafe or unlogged practice: fix by following studio protocol and noting it. Every failure maps to a band, and every fix is a habit you can build into the next page you make.

Work safely and log it. Handling materials effectively, skilfully and safely underpins all four AOs, and safety failures in a studio component are real hazards, not just lost marks: solvent-based media (oil paint, turpentine, white spirit) need ventilation and safe disposal - never down the sink; printmaking presses, blades and lino cutters need cutting-away discipline; plaster and clay dust need masks and wet-cleaning; heat guns, soldering and welding need supervision and PPE. Log safe practice where relevant - solvent cleanup per studio protocol - because the spec names safety in the quality-of-making requirement, and a moderator reading a welding-assemblage investigation with no safety evidence will discount the practical competence claim.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: Explain how you explored, selected and refined media, materials, techniques and processes in response to your investigation's focus, and justify how your final selections realise your intentions. (AO2 - 24 marks at A* level; assessed within AQA 7202/C and 7202/X)
Mark-scheme logic: Mark-scheme logic (AO2: explore and select, reviewing and refining). Exploration hits: a genuine range trialled across the investigation - drawing media (graphite grades, charcoal, ink), painting media (oil glazing over warm underpainting, acrylic layering with gels, watercolour washes), print (mono print, lino), and photographic layering - each sampled and dated. Selection hits: choices justified against intentions on four criteria - expressive fit, technical control, time cost, availability - e.g. acrylic layering selected because it stratifies time as the concept requires, is controllable at scale and dries within supervised time; rejected routes visible with reasons. Reviewing/refining hits: trials pages structured as question - samples - comparison - decision; refinement evidenced by each trial changed by the previous one's findings (glaze too saturated, scraped back, leaner mix, warmer underpainting); review notes written as decisions (abandoning X because, escalating Y by, next experiment must prove); refinement distinguished from repetition. Resolution hits: final trials page resolves media, scale and format for the finished outcome; cross-media translation shown (lino-cut flat colour reinterpreted in acrylic layers). Vocabulary: fat over lean, gesso, impasto, glaze, scumble, registration, maquette, ground - used accurately and annotated as decisions. Top band language: exceptional ability to explore AND select; reviewing and refining in a confident and purposeful manner as work develops; safety evidence where relevant (solvent disposal, press discipline).

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

State the AO2 assessment objective and describe what its top band (21-24 marks) looks like in a portfolio. (6 marks)
Answer: AO2: explore and select appropriate resources, media, materials, techniques and processes, reviewing and refining ideas as work develops (2). Top band: an exceptional ability to explore AND select appropriate resources, media, materials, techniques and processes (2); ideas reviewed and refined in a confident and purposeful manner as work develops (2). In portfolio terms: a genuine range of dated trials, each annotated with a comparison against intention and a decision; justified selections; refinement where each trial is changed by the previous one's findings; fluent specialist vocabulary.
Explain the rule of 'fat over lean' in oil painting and the consequence of breaking it. (6 marks)
Answer: The rule: each successive layer of an oil painting must contain more oil - a fatter medium - than the layer below (2). It is managed by adding medium (linseed, stand oil) progressively through the build, or by keeping early layers lean (thinned with solvent, on an absorbent ground) (2). Consequence of breaking it: underlayers dry faster than the fat layers above, so the film dries unevenly and cracks - craquelure - sometimes months later, destroying the surface (2). The rule exemplifies why media knowledge is assessed: a technique decision (how to layer) has a material consequence the artist must control and annotate.
Distinguish relief and intaglio printmaking by where the ink sits, and name one method of each. (8 marks)
Answer: Relief: the block (lino, wood) is cut away where no ink is wanted; ink sits on the raised remaining surface and the image prints in reverse (2); method: lino printing (or woodcut) (1). Intaglio: lines are incised or bitten into a metal plate; ink sits in the grooves, the surface is wiped clean, and a press forces damp paper into the cuts (2); methods: etching (acid bites lines through a ground), engraving (burin-cut), drypoint (scratched, leaving a burr that prints a soft line), aquatint (acid bites tonal areas) (2 for one correctly described). Contrast stated: relief prints from what remains, intaglio from what is removed - the two families are opposite logics of the same process (1).
A student's sketchbook shows ten near-identical studies of the same subject in the same medium. Explain why this earns little AO2 credit and how the evidence could be rebuilt. (10 marks)
Answer: Why little credit: AO2 rewards exploring and SELECTING appropriate media and processes and reviewing/refining ideas as work develops (2); ten identical studies show no exploration of range (1), no selection judgement (1), and repetition rather than refinement - each trial should be changed by the previous one's findings (1); without annotated decisions the moderator cannot read any review at all (1). Rebuilding: convert two of the ten into a trials page testing alternative media against intention - e.g. the same subject in charcoal, mono print and acrylic layering, side by side, dated (1); annotate a comparison and a decision - what the concept needs, what is rejected, why (1); escalate the strongest route in a second-generation trial visibly changed by the findings (1); write review notes as decisions - abandoning X because, next experiment must prove (1). The result is exploration, selection and refinement visible on the surface of the work - the top band's exact behaviour (1).
Explain how you would build AO2 evidence for the 15 supervised hours of Component 2 during the preparatory period. (12 marks)
Answer: AO1-AO2 link first: derive trials from analysed artists' methods - each researched artist's approach implies a process to test (2). Trials: media and process experiments in prep testing the approaches intended for the outcome - supports, grounds, dilution, layering times - each dated and annotated with a comparison against intention (2). Selection: decide the outcome's media, scale and format on four criteria - expressive fit, technical control, time cost within 15 hours, availability - with rejected routes visible and reasons stated (2). Refinement: second-generation trials changed by the first round's findings; problems (drying times, opacity, adhesion) solved in prep where recovery time exists (2). Resolution: the final trials page becomes the resolution page - final media, ground, scale fixed - feeding the time plan (2). Safety and logistics: materials counted, solvents and disposal protocols checked, references pre-printed for the no-internet condition (2). The supervised hours then execute a selection already made and refined - which is why prep is half the AO2 evidence.
