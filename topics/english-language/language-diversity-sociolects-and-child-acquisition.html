LA1: Language Diversity, Sociolects and Child Acquisition

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level All Boards (AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC, CCEA) AQA

Dialect and sociolect, gender and language theories, Child Language Acquisition (CLA), historical language change.

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Sociolect: A variety of language associated with a particular social class, group, or subculture.
Language Acquisition Device (LAD): Chomsky's nativist theory proposing an innate biological mechanism enabling children to learn grammatical rules.
Holophrastic Stage: First acquisition stage (roughly 12-18 months) where single words such as 'milk' function as entire utterances, often conveying a whole proposition.
Telegraphic Stage: Stage from around 24 months where content words appear in grammatical order ('daddy go work') while function words and inflections are omitted.
Virtuous Errors: Grammatically logical but non-standard errors such as 'I runned' or 'foots', showing children apply rules rather than imitating adult speech — evidence for nativism.
Accommodation Theory: Giles' theory that speakers converge towards or diverge from each other's sociolect to signal solidarity, authority or social distance.

🔍 Key Principles & Specification Requirements

  • Gender theories: Deficit model (Lakoff), Dominance model (Zimmerman & West), Difference model (Tannen), Diversity model (Cameron).
  • Child language theories: Nativist (Chomsky), Behaviourist (Skinner: imitation/reinforcement), Interactionist (Bruner: LASS scaffolding).
  • Language change: prescriptivism (judging language against arbitrary standards) vs descriptivism (observing language use neutrally).

💡 Worked Example Question

Exam-Style Question

Question:

Compare Chomsky's nativist view of language acquisition with Skinner's behaviourist model.

Model Solution & Mark Scheme:

Skinner argues children acquire language purely via operant conditioning (imitation, reinforcement, association). Chomsky refutes this using the 'poverty of the stimulus' argument: children produce novel grammatical utterances ('virtuous errors' like 'I runned') they never heard, demonstrating an innate Language Acquisition Device (LAD) parsing universal grammar.

Q3: Analyse how children's telegraphic speech demonstrates rule-governed grammar rather than simple imitation.

Show Model Answer

Answer: Telegraphic utterances such as 'daddy go work' preserve subject-verb-object order and omit unstressed function words, revealing children abstract underlying word order rather than copying adult strings. Berko's 'wug test' (1958) showed children pluralise nonsense words correctly ('wugs'), and overgeneralisations like 'goed' prove children construct and apply rules they have never heard adults use — supporting Chomsky's poverty of the stimulus argument against Skinner's imitation model.

Q4: Evaluate the claim that gendered differences in conversational style are best explained by Tannen's Difference model.

Show Model Answer

Answer: Tannen's report versus rapport talk and her cross-cultural analogy plausibly explain why same-sex conversations often feel cooperative, and her 'do you love me?' example illustrates status versus connection goals. However, evaluation must note Zimmerman and West's interruption data supports a Dominance reading, Hyde's gender similarities hypothesis shows variation within genders exceeds variation between them, and Cameron's Diversity model reframes 'female' styles as contextual performance rather than essential difference — so the Difference model is useful but not definitive.
