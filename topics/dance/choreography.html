Choreography

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA

AQA 7237 A-level Dance choreography revision: the group choreography task, choreographic devices (motif, development, contrast, climax), structures, choreographic intent and safe practice - exam-focused, A* sufficient.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Apply the requirements of AQA 7237 Assessment Task 3: create an original group choreography of 3-4 minutes for 3, 4 or 5 dancers responding to one of three externally set tasks (AO2).
  • Select and manipulate the movement components (action, spatial, dynamic) through choreographic devices - motif and motif development, variation, repetition, contrast, highlights, climax, transitions - in relation to the dance idea(s).
  • Structure movement material coherently using sectional use of binary, ternary, rondo, theme and variation, narrative, episodic or chance forms.
  • Use the aural setting (and physical setting where appropriate) and the skills of your dancers to communicate artistic intention, documented in a programme note of no more than 300 words.
  • Direct the choreographic process: research, develop and experiment with dance ideas through studio and non-studio investigation, then refine through the rehearsal process.
  • Apply safe practice - correct alignment, technical accuracy, appropriate dancewear, healthy approach to training - when creating and rehearsing group choreography.
  • Draw on the choreographic approaches of named practitioners in the areas of study to inform your own practical work (synoptic link to Component 2).

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Motif: A short movement phrase with significance to the dance idea; the building block that is developed through choreographic devices.
Motif development: Altering a motif through changes of action, space or dynamics (e.g. retrograde, inversion, augmentation, transference to another body part) to create unity with interest.
Repetition: Exact restatement of movement material to reinforce the dance idea and give the audience a recognisable anchor.
Contrast: Juxtaposition of opposing movement material (fast/slow, high/low, solo/ensemble) to create interest and mark structural change.
Highlight: A moment choreographed to catch the eye and direct audience attention, e.g. unison, stillness or a lift.
Climax: The point of greatest intensity in the dance; its placement is a deliberate structural decision.
Transition: The link between phrases, sections or formations; may be fluid (continuity) or abrupt (contrast).
Binary: Two-part structure (AB) built on contrast between its sections.
Ternary: Three-part structure (ABA) in which a return to altered opening material provides resolution.
Episodic: Structure of linked, often non-chronological episodes connected by thematic threads.

The Group Choreography Task: Assessment Task 3 (AO2, 40 marks)

Assessment Task 3 requires you to create a complete, original group dance lasting a minimum of three minutes and a maximum of four minutes for three, four or five dancers, in response to an externally set task (spec 3.2). AQA publishes three set tasks on Centre Services by 15 September in the academic year of assessment; you respond to one task in its entirety - tasks must not be edited, changed or abridged. The choreography is assessed by an external AQA visiting examiner during a visit to your centre (normally between March and May) and is worth 40 marks, contributing 25% of the A-level through AO2: create dance applying choreographic skills to communicate artistic intention.

  • Own unaided work: you must be wholly responsible for the creation of a complete dance; you select and develop the movement material rather than the teacher.
  • You may perform in your own dance if you wish, or act as, for example, accompanist or sound operator - the mark rewards choreography, not your performance.
  • Programme note (maximum 300 words): must clearly inform the examiner of the choreographic intention of the work and how it relates to the selected task; handed to the examiner at the start of the live assessment.
  • Timing penalty: choreography under three minutes or over four minutes incurs a penalty scaled to the severity of the infringement.
  • Evidence: a single-camera, unedited audio-visual recording from an audience perspective, identifying you by name and candidate number, submitted with a signed candidate record form.

The group choreography assessment grid divides the 40 marks into five criteria worth 8 marks each, and every criterion is judged in relation to the dance idea(s):

Mark bands and penalties

Bands run exceptional (7-8), highly refined (5-6), proficient (3-4), adequate (1-2) per criterion. A mark of 0 is awarded where no work is presented or nothing is worthy of credit. Duration penalties apply to the total mark. Examiners only conduct assessments where health and safety requirements are securely in place, so a formally risk-assessed space and warm-up time are non-negotiable.

Choreographic Processes and Devices

The specification names two strands of choreographic process: researching, developing and experimenting with dance ideas through studio and non-studio investigation, and the rehearsal process. Non-studio investigation includes stimulus research (image, text, music, observation of people), task-setting and recording of ideas; the rehearsal process converts material into performance - refining dynamics, fixing spatial patterns, cleaning timing and gathering dancer feedback.

Manipulation of movement happens through choreographic devices. Learn each device with a definition, an example and a reason for its use in relation to the dance idea(s) - examiners credit manipulation that develops the idea, not decoration:

  • Motif: a short movement phrase or sequence with significance - the dance idea in miniature. A strong motif is simple enough to be recognised after development.
  • Motif development: altering the motif through changes of action, space or dynamics - e.g. retrograde, inversion, augmentation, diminution, transference to another body part, embellishment, or changes of level, direction and dynamics. Development creates unity while maintaining interest.
  • Variation: a restatement of material with notable changes, usually longer than a single developed motif.
  • Repetition: restating movement exactly; reinforces the idea and gives the audience a hook.
  • Contrast: placing opposing material (fast/slow, high/low, solo/group) side by side to create interest and mark structural change.
  • Highlights: moments that catch the eye - a sudden stillness, unison, lift or change of direction - used to direct the audience's attention.
  • Climax: the point of greatest intensity; its placement is a structural decision (commonly, but not always, late in the work).
  • Transitions: the links between phrases and sections; fluid transitions create continuity, abrupt ones create contrast.

An A* response uses a small number of devices thoroughly: one motif developed in many ways across the work is more creditable than many motifs developed superficially. Expect to justify why each device serves the intention stated in your programme note - the grid criterion is manipulation in relation to the dance idea(s), so a device that does not develop the idea earns nothing.

Anchor devices in the practitioners you study rather than inventing in a vacuum: Bruce's Rooster takes one movement idea - the strutting, chest-forward walk - and develops it through dynamics (swagger to sudden bursts), number (unison verses broken into duet variations) and spatial patterning (circular parades around a central pair). That development logic - one idea, many manipulations, each signposted to the audience - is exactly the pattern the grid rewards, and it can be transplanted into your own group choreography without borrowing the material itself.

Structure and Form

Structuring of movement material is a separate assessment criterion: the examiner rewards material structured in a coherent way through sectional use of choreographic structures:

  • Binary (AB): two contrasting sections; effective for opposing moods or question-and-answer material.
  • Ternary (ABA): a return to opening material gives resolution; the reprise should be recognisably altered to avoid pointless repetition.
  • Rondo (ABACA): a recurring principal section alternating with contrasting episodes; strong for group works built on a signature motif.
  • Theme and variation: a theme stated then varied successively; mirrors motif development at structural scale.
  • Narrative: order dictated by a story; the dance idea and the set task's stimulus control the sequence.
  • Episodic: linked episodes, often non-chronological, connected by thematic threads.
  • Chance: ordering determined by chance procedures (associated with Merce Cunningham); if you use chance deliberately, coherence must still be communicated to the audience.

Choose the structure that serves the set task and state the intention clearly in the programme note. A common weakness is a structure that exists only on paper: the audience must be able to see section boundaries - through changes of spatial organisation, dynamics, aural setting or use of dancers. Study how practitioners in your area of study structure works (for example, the sectional construction of Bruce's Rooster, one section per Rolling Stones song) and borrow the logic, not the material.

Handle transitions as material in their own right: travelling pathways that re-form the cast during a section change keep the work continuous, while a deliberate freeze or blackout break announces a new section. Decide which reading you want and be consistent - an A* choreography shows intentional structure throughout, and the examiner can see every section boundary from the auditorium. If you use an episodic or chance structure, thread a recurring motif or aural cue through the sections so the fragments read as one work.

Choosing structure for the task

Match structure to stimulus: contrast-rich stimuli suit binary or ternary; a strong recurring idea suits rondo or theme and variation; a story suits narrative; linked episodes suit episodic. Whatever you choose, the set task's wording must still be answered - re-read the task and check your structure delivers every demand it makes.

Intent, Constituent Features, Dancers and Safe Practice

Choreographic intent is the through-line of every criterion: what you want the dance to communicate and how each decision serves it. The programme note (maximum 300 words) must tell the examiner the intention and its relation to the selected task; the choreography must then deliver exactly what the note promises.

The aural setting is the dominant constituent feature in group choreography. Know these music-movement relationships and use at least two deliberately:

  • Direct correlation: movement matches the music's beat, phrasing and accents exactly.
  • Music visualisation: movement makes visible the music's colours, textures and instrumentation.
  • Mutual coexistence: music and dance develop independently yet complement one another.
  • Disassociation: movement deliberately ignores or opposes the music - effective for irony or tension.
  • Narrative aural setting: the soundtrack carries or advances the story.
  • Call and response: one voice or part initiates, another answers - mirrored in movement exchange.

Also consider tempo, rhythm, mood and atmosphere of the accompaniment, and use physical setting (costume, properties, set, lighting) where appropriate to the task. Dancers are a constituent feature too: communicate with and organise your dancers, utilising their individual skills and attributes (flexibility, partnering strength, performance presence, musicality) to develop the artistic intention - the grid rewards this specifically.

Safe practice (spec 3.1.1) applies throughout creating and rehearsing: awareness of correct alignment; technical accuracy; appropriate dancewear and presentation of self; and a healthy approach to training, including the lifestyle of a dancer. Give dancers warm-up time, develop lifts and contact work progressively on a risk-assessed floor, and never choreograph beyond the safe physical capability of your cast.

Finally, the specification expects the study of the choreographic approaches (historical or current) of named practitioners in the areas of study to inform your practical work - a synoptic link that also feeds the written paper. Anchoring your process in a practitioner's approach (for example, Bruce's synthesis of Graham and Cunningham techniques, or Alston's Cunningham-based clarity) demonstrates theory informing practice, which is precisely what synoptic assessment demands.

Work the rehearsal process in phases: early studio investigation (improvisation tasks from the stimulus, recording promising fragments), then selection and refinement (fixing motif variants, structuring the sections), then production rehearsal (spatial patterns, timing, lifts developed progressively, full runs with the aural setting). Keep the programme note in draft from the start - an intention stated early keeps every later decision answerable to it, and the final version (maximum 300 words) should read as the plan the examiner has just watched, not a retro-fit.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: Discuss the choreographic approach of ONE named practitioner from your selected area of study, showing how this approach is evident in at least TWO of their works, and explain how it can inform your own group choreography for Assessment Task 3. (25 marks)
Mark-scheme logic: Mark-scheme logic (AO3 + AO4, with synoptic link to AO2). A top-band answer must hit: (1) the practitioner's choreographic approach defined precisely - technique (e.g. Graham-based contraction/release), movement style and choreographic style; (2) the influences affecting the development of that approach (training, teachers, companies, cultural context); (3) for each of two works: subject matter and its treatment, form (phrases/sections), and constituent features (movement components, dancers, aural setting, physical setting) with their relevance to subject matter; (4) an evaluative judgement of the importance of the works in the development of the genre in relation to the area of study; (5) an explicit synoptic link - how specific features of the approach (e.g. Bruce's sectional structure, Alston's spatial clarity) can be applied in your own group choreography through devices, structure, aural setting and use of dancers; (6) genre-specific terminology used accurately throughout, with judgements rather than description alone. Mid-band answers describe the approach and works but lack dated evidence, the two-work requirement, or the link to own practice.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Describe what is meant by a motif and explain how a single motif can be developed to create a coherent group choreography. (6 marks)
Answer: A motif is a short movement phrase carrying significance for the dance idea - the idea in miniature (2). Development alters the motif's action, spatial or dynamic content: retrograde, inversion, augmentation, diminution, transference to a different body part, or changes of level, direction and dynamics (2). Restating the developed motif in different sections and assigning variants to different dancers creates unity - the audience recognises the idea - while maintaining interest, so the choreography reads as one coherent work rather than separate phrases (2).
Explain the difference between binary and rondo structure and when each is an effective choice. (4 marks)
Answer: Binary (AB) presents two contrasting sections - effective where the task demands a single, clear contrast of mood or material, e.g. restriction then release (2). Rondo (ABACA) alternates a recurring principal section with contrasting episodes - effective where the dance idea needs a signature motif to return, giving the audience recurring anchors while allowing contrasting stimuli between returns (2).
Discuss how the aural setting can be used to communicate the dance idea(s) in group choreography. Refer to at least three music-movement relationships. (8 marks)
Answer: Direct correlation: movement matches beat, phrasing and accents - communicates order, drive and precision; effective for rhythmic ideas (2-3). Disassociation: movement opposes the music - communicates tension, irony or instability (2-3). Music visualisation: movement reveals the score's textures and instrumentation - communicates mood and atmosphere drawn from the accompaniment (2-3). Call and response or narrative aural settings can structure movement exchanges or advance a story (2-3). Top-band answers name the relationship, describe its use in a specific moment, and interpret its effect in relation to the dance idea (AO4).
Explain how you would organise and communicate with your dancers to utilise their skills in developing the artistic intention of your group choreography. (6 marks)
Answer: Identify each dancer's attributes - flexibility, strength for partnering, dynamic attack, musicality, performance presence - and assign material accordingly: the strongest jumper carries elevation highlights, the most flexible dancer anchors fluid contractions (2). Communicate the intention clearly so dancers interpret dynamics and emphasis consistently rather than merely executing steps (2). Organise rehearsals progressively: task-based exploration, then fixing spatial patterns, cleaning timing and unison, gathering dancer feedback to refine transitions - the grid rewards utilisation of dancers' skills in relation to the dance idea (2).
With reference to TWO works, discuss the choreographic approach of Christopher Bruce and the importance of his work in the development of Rambert Dance Company. (25 marks)
Answer: Bruce's approach: technique rooted in Graham-based training with Cunningham influence - the two techniques Rambert institutionalised after 1966; movement style synthesising modern dance, ballet and social dance colour; choreographic style theatrical, musical and humanist, frequently addressing political and social themes (7-8). Work 1, Ghost Dances (1981, Ballet Rambert): subject matter - oppression in Latin America; treatment through gaunt, skeleton-clad ghost figures and folk-influenced movement; aural setting of Andean folk music; episodic form contrasting the living victims with the timeless ghosts (7-8). Work 2, Rooster (1991): 1960s social dance idiom to Rolling Stones recordings; gender play and sexual politics; celebratory register showing the range within Bruce's output (7-8). Importance: Bruce became associate director in 1975 and associate choreographer in 1979; the 1980s repertoire centred on North, Bruce and Alston; he returned as artistic director 1994-2002, continuing development with a range of techniques, new works and a repertoire of neo-classical and modern works (AO4: judge his centrality to the company's modern-dance identity) (2-3). Terminology and dated evidence throughout (1-2).
