Dance Appreciation

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA

AQA 7237 A-level Dance appreciation revision: Component 2 Critical engagement structure, constituent features, choreographic approach, context and essay technique for Rooster and the areas of study.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Recall the structure, mark allocation and section demands of Component 2: Critical engagement (2h30, 100 marks, Section A compulsory, Section B optional).
  • Analyse any set work through the four constituent features - movement components, dancers, aural setting, physical setting - interpreting each in relation to the subject matter.
  • Define and apply the choreographic approach (technique, movement style, choreographic style) of set-work choreographers and named practitioners.
  • Explain the influences, origins and significance of set works in the development of their choreographers and genres.
  • Situate each area of study in its historical, cultural and social context, and discuss the genre's capacity to reflect and challenge society.
  • Write timed 25-mark essays with a thesis, dated evidence, interpretation, AO4 judgement and genre-specific terminology.
  • Demonstrate synoptic understanding by connecting set works to choreographic and performance practice.

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Constituent features: The four analytically separable features of a dance: movement components; dancers; aural setting; physical setting.
Movement components: The action, spatial and dynamic elements of the movement itself.
Dynamic elements: Qualities of movement - attack, weight, flow, tempo - as distinct from what is done (action) and where (space).
Aural setting: Everything heard: music, sound, the spoken word, the audible aspect of dance and silence.
Physical setting: Everything seen that surrounds the movement: costume, properties, set, lighting design, performance environment, film.
Choreographic approach: A choreographer's particular technique, movement style and choreographic style.
Genre: A category of dance defined by style, technique and history - modern dance, ballet and jazz dance in AQA 7237.
Subject matter: The theme or topic of the dance and its treatment.
Form: The structure of the dance - phrases and sections - and its effectiveness in communicating the subject matter.
Leitmotif: A recurring musical theme associated with a particular character, incorporated into ballet scores during the romantic period.

Component 2: Critical Engagement - Exam Structure

Component 2: Critical engagement is a written exam of 2 hours 30 minutes, 100 marks, 50% of the A-level, assessing AO3 (knowledge and understanding of performance and choreography from different periods and genres) and AO4 (critically appreciating and assessing performance and choreography through analytical, interpretative and evaluative judgements). You study two set works: one compulsory, one chosen from four options.

  • Section A (50 marks) - compulsory: short answer questions (25 marks) and one essay (25 marks) on the compulsory set work and area of study: Rooster (Christopher Bruce, 1991) and Rambert Dance Company (formerly Ballet Rambert) 1966-2002.
  • Section B (50 marks) - two essays, 25 marks each: one on your optional set work and one on its corresponding area of study. Options: Giselle / the romantic ballet period; Appalachian Spring / the origins of American modern dance 1900-1945; Singin' in the Rain / American jazz dance 1940-1975; Sutra / the independent contemporary dance scene in Britain 2000-current.

The assessment is synoptic: you must draw together knowledge from across the course, demonstrating the relationships between theory and practice - examiners reward answers that connect set works to choreographic and performance understanding, not isolated recall.

Command words determine depth demanded: identify/state (recall), describe (account of features), explain (give reasons - how and why), discuss/analyse (interrogate features and their effect), evaluate (AO4 judgement with justification). Short answers reward precise, dated facts; essays reward a thesis supported by constituent-feature evidence and secured by judgement.

Timing plan for 2h30

Section A: short answers (25 marks) in about 30 minutes; essay (25 marks) in about 30-35 minutes. Section B: two essays (25 marks each) in about 30-35 minutes each; keep 10 minutes to check dates and terminology. At roughly 1.2 marks per minute, every minute must produce dated, interpretable content - plan each essay in its first two minutes (thesis plus three evidence points) rather than after writing.

Also know how the two AOs divide the paper: AO3 (knowledge and understanding of performance and choreography from different periods and genres) dominates the short answers; AO4 (critically appreciating and assessing performance and choreography through analytical, interpretative and evaluative judgements) dominates the essays. An essay with no judgement is capped at mid-band however dense its recall.

Command words ladder

Identify/state = recall a fact (1-2 marks). Describe = give a precise account of features using action, spatial and dynamic vocabulary. Explain = give reasons - how and why features work. Discuss/analyse = interrogate features, weigh their effect, make links across the work. Evaluate = reach a justified judgement (AO4). Match paragraph length to the command word: short answers earn marks per accurate point; essays earn them per developed, evidenced, judged point.

Constituent Features: The Analysis Framework

Spec 3.3.1 defines four constituent features - learn this framework as the skeleton of every analysis answer. Each feature must be interpreted in relation to the subject matter, not merely described:

Constituent featureElements
Movement componentsaction, spatial and dynamic elements
Dancersnumber, gender, role, physique
Aural settingmusic, sound, the spoken word, the audible aspect of dance, silence
Physical settingcostume, properties, set, lighting design, performance environment, film

Analyse in three moves: describe the feature precisely (what is seen/heard, using action, space and dynamic vocabulary); interpret (what the feature communicates about the subject matter or the character of the dance); evaluate (how effectively it embodies the subject matter - AO4). For example, in Rooster: the men's strutting, cock-of-the-rooster carriage (action) performed with swaggering, syncopated attack (dynamics) to Rolling Stones tracks (aural setting) in 1960s-inspired costume (physical setting) embodies the sexual politics and swagger of the decade - description becomes interpretation once linked to the work's subject matter.

Also know the language of form (phrases, sections) and the significance of the character of the dance - both named in the spec as things you must understand about each set work: whether the work is abstract or has a character, how form communicates subject matter, and how the sections build.

Build movement vocabulary you can deploy in any work: action - travel, turn, gesture, elevation, stillness, fall, jump, contract, extend; spatial - pathway, direction, level, formation, symmetry/asymmetry, personal versus stage space; dynamic - attack, weight, flow, tempo, sustained/percussive/suspended/swinging. Every set-work answer should name the feature, describe it precisely and interpret its effect on the subject matter in one move.

Test the framework on a second work: in Sutra (2008), the dancers move among Antony Gormley's wooden boxes - the physical setting is simultaneously apparatus (boxes are carried, stacked, tipped) and metaphor (a hut, a wall, a void), and the audible aspect of the dance (knocking, sliding) merges with Szymon Brzoska's composed score; in Appalachian Spring (1944), Isamu Noguchi's sculptural set reduces a farmhouse to suggestion, and Graham's dancers work in contraction and release to Aaron Copland's Americana. Both show constituent features embodying subject matter - the pattern every 25-mark answer must expose.

Give the dancers feature full treatment too: number (why this many - ensemble weight, symmetry, opposition), gender (casting as meaning - the flirtatious gender play of Rooster), role (principal, corps, ghost, outsider) and physique (the physique a style demands and displays). Casting choices are interpretation: connect them to the subject matter rather than listing them.

Quick check before any set-work answer

Can you name: the action, spatial and dynamic features of a key moment; the dancers' number, gender, role and physique; the aural setting elements heard; the physical setting elements seen? If not, that feature is your next revision target - every 25-mark answer draws on all four.

Choreographic Approach and the Areas of Study

Choreographic approach = the choreographer's particular technique, movement style and choreographic style. For each set work you must know the approach, the influences affecting its development, the origins of the dance, its importance in the development of the choreographer and the genre, and similarities and differences with other works by the choreographer. This requires understanding of other related works - selected by your teacher from visual and/or written sources - to place the set work in context within the area of study and the genre.

For each area of study you must know the stylistic features of the genre and the choreographic approach of a minimum of two named practitioners, showing the range within the genre, with at least two works from each (subject matter, treatment, form, constituent features of each). The three genres are defined by AQA:

  • Modern dance: theatrical dancing that began in the early 20th century, originally in opposition to the formality of ballet; the term is now sometimes replaced by other labels, e.g. contemporary.
  • Ballet: dancing focused on the technique and style of the danse d'ecole - port de bras, the five positions of the feet, turn-out of the legs.
  • Jazz dance: dancing of African origins focused on complex rhythms and techniques including isolation of body parts; a wide range of styles has evolved, e.g. lyrical jazz and urban dance.

Named practitioner lists: Rambert 1966-2002 - Glen Tetley, Robert North, Richard Alston, Siobhan Davies, Ashley Page; romantic ballet - Filippo Taglioni, August Bournonville, Arthur Saint-Leon; American modern 1900-1945 - Loie Fuller, Isadora Duncan, Ruth St Denis, Doris Humphrey; American jazz 1940-1975 - Agnes de Mille, Jack Cole, Jerome Robbins, Bob Fosse; independent contemporary Britain 2000-current - Shobana Jeyasingh, Matthew Bourne, Jasmin Vardimon, Akram Khan, Hofesh Shechter.

Structure practitioner comparison by influence, then approach, then work: state the practitioner's influences (training, teachers, companies), define their approach (technique, movement style, choreographic style), then evidence it in two works with subject matter, treatment, form and constituent features. To show the range within the genre, pair practitioners whose approaches genuinely oppose - within the Rambert list, for example, pair a Cunningham-derived, abstract approach with a more theatrical, dramatic one - and make the contrast itself your argument.

The spec also asks for similarities and differences between the set work and other works by the choreographer - a cheap source of AO3 content once prepared: Rooster's celebratory social-dance register against the political gravity of Bruce's other repertoire; Appalachian Spring's celebratory Americana against Graham's darker psychological works; Sutra's ritual austerity against Cherkaoui's more overtly theatrical pieces. Prepare one similarity and one difference per set work, each grounded in subject matter, form or constituent features.

Context, Genre and Critical Judgement (AO4)

You must understand the relationship between the dance and its context - the position of the genre within history, culture and society - and the genre's capacity to reflect and challenge society. This is where top-band essays separate from mid-band: connect the work's features to its world. Rooster only makes full sense against 1960s social dance and gender politics; Giselle against Romanticism's supernatural and the ballerina cult; Appalachian Spring against American modern dance's individualism and Copland's American sound; Sutra against Britain's independent, collaborative contemporary scene.

Essay craft for the 25-mark questions:

  1. Open with a thesis that answers the question directly (one sentence).
  2. Build 3-4 paragraphs, each anchored in precise, dated evidence - choreographer, work, year, company, constituent feature.
  3. Interpret: link every feature to subject matter, form or character.
  4. Judge (AO4): how effectively, how significantly - use evaluative language (the most striking embodiment; this changed the company's direction because...).
  5. Use terminology specific to the genre (contraction and release; ballon; isolation; Bharatanatyam) accurately.
  6. Cross-reference across the course where possible - synopticity is credited.

Practise by converting every fact into a claim: not 'Morrice changed the company in 1966' but 'Morrice's 1966 changes - reducing company size, introducing Graham technique into training, engaging guest choreographers - reoriented Ballet Rambert from ballet towards modern dance, which is why a Bruce work using Rolling Stones and social dance idiom could enter its repertoire by 1994.' Facts + consequence + judgement = AO3 + AO4.

Prepare AO4 language deliberately: evaluative sentence openers (the most significant; the decisive change; this crystallised the company's identity), consequence links (which meant that; as a result), and balanced judgement (however; the exception being). Address the genre's capacity to reflect and challenge society with concrete cases: the romantic ballet reflected its era's cult of the unattainable yet, by elevating the ballerina above the male dancer, eventually narrowed ballet's expressive range; jazz on screen democratised dance for mass audiences while drawing on racialised popular forms; Bruce's Rooster both celebrates and gently satirises the sexual politics it reconstructs.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: Discuss the choreographic approach of Christopher Bruce in Rooster (1991) and analyse how the constituent features of the work embody its subject matter. (25 marks)
Mark-scheme logic: Mark-scheme logic (AO3 + AO4). AO3 hits: Bruce's approach defined - technique (Graham-based contraction/release plus Cunningham-influenced clarity), movement style (synthesis of modern dance, ballet and 1960s social dance), choreographic style (theatrical, musically driven, humanist themes); influences (Ballet Rambert's post-1966 modern-dance orientation, guest choreographers such as Glen Tetley, the company's neo-classical and modern repertoire); origins of the work (created for Ballet du Grand Theatre de Geneve, premiere 10 October 1991; Rambert performance 8 December 1994); importance in the development of Bruce and the genre. Constituent features, each interpreted in relation to subject matter: movement components (strutting, twist- and jitterbug-derived actions with syncopated, flirtatious dynamics); dancers (men and women in role play of sexual pursuit and control); aural setting (Rolling Stones recordings - direct correlation and music visualisation; one section per song); physical setting (Marian Bruce's 1960s-inspired costume, Tina MacHugh's lighting, minimal set). AO4 hits: evaluative judgement on effectiveness and significance - e.g. the section-per-song form communicates the decade's shifting moods; the gender play both reflects and gently satirises 1960s sexual politics. Terminology: contraction/release, syncopation, unison, direct correlation, motif. Top band: precise dates, consistent thesis, judgement in every paragraph.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Identify the elements of the aural setting and physical setting in Rooster and explain their relevance to the subject matter. (6 marks)
Answer: Aural setting: songs recorded by the Rolling Stones; within that, driving syncopated rhythm and swaggering vocals - the audible aspect of the dance; pauses and silences in phrasing (2). Physical setting: 1960s-inspired costume designed by Marian Bruce; lighting by Tina MacHugh isolating soloists against a sparse stage (2). Relevance: the Stones' swagger and syncopated drive directly embody the sexual politics and confidence of 1960s social dance; the costume dates the movement's era of quotation; lighting focus converts ensemble numbers into flirtatious duels (2).
Explain the changes made to Ballet Rambert in 1966 and why they were significant. (6 marks)
Answer: Norman Morrice, associate director, encouraged Marie Rambert to make changes: company size, the preservation and creation of works, the inclusion of Graham technique in the dancers' training, the involvement of guest choreographers and teachers, and the development of the touring schedule (3). Significance: 1966 heralded the company's decisive turn from ballet towards modern dance, creating the repertoire into which works by Tetley, North, Alston and Bruce would later fit - the reason a Bruce work with modern and social dance vocabulary sits within the Rambert area of study (3).
Discuss how the choreographic approaches of TWO named practitioners from the Rambert area of study show the range within the genre. (25 marks)
Answer: Richard Alston: technique grounded in Cunningham - articulate spine, rapid changes of direction, clarity of line; choreographic style abstract and musical; consolidated the Cunningham influence as resident choreographer (1980) then artistic director (1986), when the company was renamed Rambert Dance Company (1987) (7-8). Robert North: approach emphasising physicality, musicality and dramatic quality; he directed the company 1981-1986; works lean theatrical and playful (7-8). Range: Alston's cool, formal modern dance versus North's muscular theatricality shows the genre's breadth within one company - from Cunningham-derived abstraction to dramatic ensemble work (5-6). AO4: judge significance - the 1980s repertoire anchored Ballet Rambert's identity in three British choreographers (North, Bruce, Alston), giving the company its distinct modern-dance voice (3-4). Terminology and dated evidence throughout (1-2).
Describe the characteristics of the romantic ballet style and explain how Giselle reflects them. (8 marks)
Answer: Characteristics: focus on the ballerina; contrast between reality and fantasy; a sense of the supernatural and mystery conveyed through the illusion of weightlessness, gas lighting and the characteristic romantic tutu; folk or national dances for local colour; leitmotifs relating to principal characters in the score (4). Giselle reflects them: two-act structure contrasts the real world (Act I village) with the supernatural (Act II wilis); Giselle's ghostly transformation is conveyed through weightless, floating port de bras and pointe work under gas-lit haze; Adolphe Adam's score includes leitmotifs associated with Giselle; peasant dances provide local colour in Act I (4).
Explain how American jazz dance developed between 1940 and 1975, with reference to TWO named practitioners. (8 marks)
Answer: Early 1900s jazz performed by African-American artists showed links to minstrelsy and ragtime with new syncopated rhythms; from the 1930s to the 1960s the style became a more theatre-based form requiring trained dancers, with choreography from practitioners of the ballet and modern worlds (2-3). Jack Cole: an important figure in the development of jazz dance technique and its theatrical form; inspired Matt Mattox, Jerome Robbins and Bob Fosse (2). Bob Fosse: developed his own distinctive stylisation - angled lines, inverted limbs, hunched-over posture - created by his early influences (2). Other figures (Agnes de Mille, Jerome Robbins, Donen and Kelly) made dance of equal importance to drama and music in storytelling on stage and screen (1-2).
