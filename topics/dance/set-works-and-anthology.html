Set Works and Anthology

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA

AQA 7237 A-level Dance set works revision: Rooster (Bruce) and the Rambert 1966-2002 area of study plus the four optional works - Giselle, Appalachian Spring, Singin' in the Rain, Sutra - with constituent features and comparison.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Recall the credits, premieres and music of the compulsory set work Rooster and of the four optional set works.
  • Reconstruct the Rambert 1966-2002 chronology as a chain of consequences, naming the roles and dates of Morrice, Chesworth, Bruce, North and Alston.
  • Define the stylistic features of each area of study and the choreographic approaches of its named practitioners.
  • Analyse the constituent features of each set work and interpret them in relation to subject matter and context.
  • Compare set works and practitioners across eras and genres for the Section B essays.
  • Connect area-of-study understanding to your own choreographic and performance practice (synopticity).

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Set work: A professional dance work prescribed by AQA for in-depth study in Component 2: Critical engagement.
Area of study: A prescribed genre-focused period (e.g. Rambert Dance Company 1966-2002) providing historical, cultural and social context for the set work.
Compulsory set work: Rooster (Christopher Bruce, 1991) with the Rambert Dance Company (formerly Ballet Rambert) 1966-2002 area of study - required of all candidates.
Romantic ballet: 19th-century ballet style peaking in the 1830s-40s: focus on the ballerina, reality/fantasy contrast, supernatural mystery, illusion of weightlessness, gas lighting, folk colour; formulaic by 1870.
Modern dance: Theatrical dancing begun in the early 20th century in opposition to ballet's formality; the label is now sometimes replaced by contemporary.
Jazz dance: Dance of African origins focused on complex rhythms and isolation of body parts; evolved into lyrical jazz, urban and theatre styles.
Denishawn: The school and company founded by Ruth St Denis and Ted Shaw; students included Martha Graham, Doris Humphrey and Charles Weidman.
Contraction and release: Martha Graham's technique, based on the act of breathing - percussive contraction of the torso and its release.
Fall and recovery: Doris Humphrey's technique, built on the arc between surrendering to and resisting gravity.
Leitmotif: A recurring musical theme associated with a principal character, incorporated into ballet scores during the romantic period.

The Prescribed Set Works: One Compulsory, One Optional

Component 2 requires in-depth knowledge of two set works and their corresponding areas of study. One is compulsory: Rooster (Christopher Bruce, 1991) with Rambert Dance Company (formerly Ballet Rambert) 1966-2002. One is selected from four options. Learn the credits precisely - short answers reward dated facts:

Choose by genuine comparative strength: your Section B essays need a minimum of two named practitioners plus two works each from the area of study, so pick the area whose practitioner list and repertoire you can evidence best. Professional works studied must be in the public domain and easily accessible to examiners.

For your selected set work you must know: the significance of the character of the dance; the subject matter and its treatment; the form (phrases, sections) and its effectiveness; the constituent features and their relevance to subject matter; the choreographic approach; the influences affecting the development of the choreographer; the origins of the dance; the relationship between the dance and its context; the importance of the dance in the development of the choreographer and the genre; and the similarities and differences between the dance and other works by the choreographer.

For short answers, credits win marks: know each work's choreographer, year, company and premiere, music and designers cold. Section A short answers ask for exactly this recall plus constituent-feature identification; the essays ask you to convert it into argument. Also note the spec's list of what you must know about each area of study: the stylistic features of the genre and how they relate to it; the choreographic approach of a minimum of two named practitioners (influences, technique, movement style, choreographic style) with at least two works each; the relationship between the genre's development and its context; the genre's capacity to reflect and challenge society; and terminology specific to the genre.

Rooster and the Rambert Area of Study in Detail

Rooster credits: choreography Christopher Bruce; music - songs recorded by the Rolling Stones; costume designs Marian Bruce; lighting designs Tina MacHugh. First performed 10 October 1991 by the Ballet du Grand Theatre de Geneve; British premiere 28 October 1992 by London Contemporary Dance Theatre; first performance by Rambert Dance Company 8 December 1994.

The work: a suite of sections, each built on one Rolling Stones song, reviving the social dance idiom of the 1960s - strutting, twist- and jitterbug-derived actions, flirtatious gender play. The subject matter is the swagger and sexual politics of the decade: the men preen, the women counter with knowing control, and the mood shifts from celebratory to darker across the sections. The form - one section per song - communicates the decade's shifting moods; the aural setting works through direct correlation and music visualisation with the Stones' syncopated drive.

Bruce's approach: technique rooted in Graham-based training with Cunningham influence - the two techniques Rambert institutionalised after 1966; movement style synthesising modern dance, ballet and social dance colour; choreographic style theatrical, musical and humanist - his works frequently address political and social themes (cf. Ghost Dances, 1981, Ballet Rambert, responding to oppression in Latin America). Rooster's celebratory register shows the range within Bruce's output - a contrast examiners like to see argued.

Rambert 1966-2002 chronology - learn as a chain of consequences:

  • 1966: Norman Morrice (associate director) encourages Marie Rambert to change the company - company size, preservation and creation of works, Graham technique in training, involvement of guest choreographers and teachers, development of the touring schedule. The modern-dance reorientation begins.
  • 1974: Morrice leaves; John Chesworth continues his policies - promotes new work from company members, expands the repertoire through guest choreographers, develops Rambert's educational activities.
  • 1975: Christopher Bruce becomes associate director; 1979 associate choreographer.
  • 1975-1985: links between Ballet Rambert and London Contemporary Dance Theatre through choreographers such as Robert North and Richard Alston.
  • 1980s: repertoire focuses on three British choreographers - North, Bruce and Alston. North directs 1981-1986, keen to develop the physicality, musicality and dramatic quality of the dancers. Alston: resident choreographer 1980, artistic director 1986 - consolidating the Cunningham influence.
  • 1987: company renamed Rambert Dance Company.
  • 1994-2002: Bruce returns as artistic director - inclusion of a range of techniques, new works, guest choreographers, and a repertoire of neo-classical and modern works. Rooster enters the repertoire (December 1994).

Named practitioners (know two in depth, two works each): Glen Tetley (1926-2007), Robert North (born 1945), Richard Alston (born 1948), Siobhan Davies (born 1950), Ashley Page (born 1956).

The Four Optional Areas of Study and Their Practitioners

Option 1 - The romantic ballet period (with Giselle): Romanticism rebelled against Classicism's focus on form, balance and order, emphasising creativity, emotion and subjectivity; ballet was the last art affected. The romantic ballet peaked in the 1830s-40s. Characteristics: focus on the ballerina; contrast between reality and fantasy; the supernatural and mystery via the illusion of weightlessness, gas lighting and the characteristic romantic tutu; folk or national dances for local colour; leitmotifs for principal characters. Taglioni, Perrot and Bournonville led development - Bournonville alone maintained balance between male and female dancing. The style became formulaic by 1870; female dancers performing male roles signalled decline. Practitioners: Filippo Taglioni (1777-1871), August Bournonville (1805-1879), Arthur Saint-Leon (1821-1870).

Option 2 - The origins of American modern dance 1900-1945 (with Appalachian Spring): America had no tradition of professional ballet; innovators performed barefoot to concert music in freer costume. Loie Fuller led the way in modern stage lighting; Isadora Duncan developed natural movement. Ruth St Denis (eclectic styles, the Delsarte system) with Ted Shawn created a company and the Denishawn school - students included Graham, Humphrey and Weidman. Modern dance stressed individualism: angular asymmetries, aggressive and earthy qualities; early works minimised the importance of music and art; from the 1930s choreography addressed psychological and social issues. German modern dance (Mary Wigman) reached America via tours and via Hanya Holm's branch of the Wigman school. Graham, encouraged by Louis Horst, built technique on breathing - contraction and release; Humphrey on fall and recovery. University support (e.g. Bennington) accelerated acceptance of modern dance as a distinct art form. Practitioners: Fuller (1862-1928), Duncan (1877-1927), St Denis (1879-1968), Humphrey (1895-1958).

Option 3 - American jazz dance 1940-1975 (with Singin' in the Rain): early 1900s jazz performed by African-American artists showed links to minstrelsy and ragtime via new syncopated rhythms; from the 1930s-60s it became a theatre-based form requiring trained dancers, with choreography from the ballet and modern worlds. Jack Cole developed jazz dance technique and its theatrical form (inspiring Matt Mattox, Jerome Robbins and Bob Fosse); Katherine Dunham founded the anthropological dance movement, introducing African and Caribbean ritual into American choreography; Matt Mattox's teaching shaped future generations' training. Gene Kelly - physique and personality, acrobatic and athletic training, camera-conscious choreography in collaboration with Stanley Donen; Jerome Robbins combined social dance with classical ballet for characterisation, rhythmic complexity and dynamic variation, following Agnes de Mille in making dance of equal importance to drama and music in the storyline; Bob Fosse's style - angled lines, inverted limbs, hunched-over posture. Practitioners: de Mille (1905-1993), Cole (1911-1974), Robbins (1918-1998), Fosse (1927-1987).

Option 4 - The independent contemporary dance scene in Britain 2000-current (with Sutra): several generations of practitioners have achieved international recognition through their own work and commissions for different companies; the work embraces cultural similarities and differences, enhanced by collaborations with designers (Antony Gormley, Anish Kapoor) and composers (Nitin Sawhney, Szymon Brzoska), and responds to social, political and historical issues. Shobana Jeyasingh: Bharatanatyam foundation, multicultural themes of personal identity, relationships and heritage; Matthew Bourne: choreographed body language and a variety of dance and movement styles for storytelling, supported by design and music; Jasmin Vardimon: observations of human behaviour through physical theatre, inventive characterisation, technology, text and dance; Akram Khan: Kathak and contemporary training, constantly exploring other styles through collaborators from a range of backgrounds; Hofesh Shechter: composer involvement - soundscapes providing powerful backgrounds for his contemporary movement and interest in aspects of contemporary life; Cherkaoui: yoga and jazz backgrounds explored from a theatrical starting point. Practitioners: Jeyasingh (born 1957), Bourne (born 1960), Vardimon (born 1971), Khan (born 1974), Shechter (born 1975).

Comparison and Section B Essay Application

Section B demands two essays: one on your optional set work, one on its corresponding area of study (25 marks each). Strong essays do three things the mark scheme rewards:

  1. Argue through features: every paragraph anchored in a constituent feature or formal detail of a named work, interpreted in relation to subject matter.
  2. Show the range within the genre: contrast two practitioners' approaches (technique, movement style, choreographic style) with at least two works each - e.g. Bournonville's gender-balanced virtuosity against the ballerina-centred romantic norm; Khan's Kathak-contemporary hybridity against Shechter's rock-inflected ensemble drive.
  3. Situate and judge: place the genre in history, culture and society, and discuss its capacity to reflect and challenge society - jazz on screen democratised dance but traded on racialised popular forms; the romantic ballet worshipped the unattainable yet narrowed opportunities for male dancers.

Use comparison sentences that carry facts: 'Where Graham's Appalachian Spring (1944) stripped the setting to Noguchi's sculptural shorthand, Cherkaoui's Sutra (2008) made Gormley's wooden boxes both set and apparatus - both physical settings concentrate meaning, but Sutra's is also choreographic infrastructure.' Compare across the two set works you study for synopticity: Bruce's social-dance revival and Cherkaoui's intercultural ritual both reconstruct borrowed movement idioms, but to opposite effect - nostalgia versus inquiry.

Finally, connect to practice: the specification states that study of the choreographic approaches of named practitioners in the areas of study informs practical work. Knowing how Graham built material through contraction and release, or how Kelly choreographed for the camera, feeds directly into AO2 decisions in your group choreography - theory informing practice is the stated aim of the area-of-study design.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: Discuss the stylistic features of the romantic ballet period, showing how Giselle reflects the characteristics of the period and referring to the work of ONE other named romantic ballet practitioner. (25 marks)
Mark-scheme logic: Mark-scheme logic (AO3 + AO4). AO3 hits: characteristics defined with dates - focus on the ballerina; contrast between reality and fantasy; the supernatural and mystery (illusion of weightlessness, gas lighting, romantic tutu); folk or national dances for local colour; leitmotifs; the period's rise in the 1830s-40s and formulaic decline by 1870. Giselle evidence: choreographed by Jean Coralli and Jules Perrot, first performed 28 June 1841 at the Paris Opera; Adolphe Adam's score with leitmotifs; two-act reality/fantasy structure; weightless Act II wilis; Act I peasant dances and national colouring. Second practitioner: e.g. Bournonville - maintained the balance between male and female dancer with challenging choreography for both genders (contrast to the ballerina-centred norm); or Taglioni - the weightless ballerina archetype of the period. AO4 hits: evaluative judgement - e.g. the style reflected Romanticism's desire to attain the unattainable but its imbalance of the sexes narrowed ballet's status; Giselle endures because its supernatural treatment is matched by structural clarity. Terminology: port de bras, tutu, leitmotif, danse d'ecole, pointe work (as appropriate). Top band: dated facts, two-practitioner range, judgement in every paragraph.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

With reference to the premieres of Rooster, explain the origins of the work and its relationship to its context. (6 marks)
Answer: Origins: created for Ballet du Grand Theatre de Geneve, premiere 10 October 1991; British premiere 28 October 1992 by London Contemporary Dance Theatre; first performed by Rambert Dance Company 8 December 1994 (2). The work revives the 1960s social dance idiom to songs recorded by the Rolling Stones (2). Context: 1960s popular culture and gender politics are the work's subject matter; within Rambert's post-1966 modern-dance repertoire a social-dance-based suite was possible, and Bruce's return as artistic director in 1994 places the work at the end of the area-of-study period (2).
Describe the choreographic style of Bob Fosse and how his early influences contributed to it. (6 marks)
Answer: Style: a distinctive jazz stylisation featuring angled lines, inverted limbs and a hunched-over posture, built on the complex rhythms and isolation of body parts characteristic of jazz dance (3). Influences: the specification credits his early influences with creating this style - argue through that framing: an American theatre-dance background, itself descended from the jazz technique tradition established by Jack Cole, who inspired Fosse directly (3).
Discuss how the independent contemporary dance scene in Britain since 2000 reflects a range of styles and collaborations, with reference to TWO named practitioners. (25 marks)
Answer: Range of styles: the scene embraces cultural similarities and differences - Jeyasingh's Bharatanatyam-founded multicultural choreography (personal identity, relationships, heritage) versus Khan's Kathak-contemporary hybridity, constantly exploring other styles through collaborators from a range of backgrounds (8-9). Collaborations: designers (Gormley, Kapoor) and composers (Sawhney, Brzoska) enhance the work; Sutra itself - Cherkaoui with Gormley's design and Brzoska's score - is the set-work exemplar; Shechter composes his own soundscapes; Vardimon integrates technology and text (8-9). Context: the choreography responds to social, political and historical issues using an eclectic range of styles and aural setting; international recognition through independent work and commissions (3-4). AO4: judge the collaborative model as the defining feature distinguishing this scene from company-based eras such as Rambert (3-4). Terminology (Bharatanatyam, Kathak, soundscape) (1-2).
Explain the importance of Doris Humphrey's fall and recovery technique in the origins of American modern dance. (6 marks)
Answer: Humphrey, a Denishawn student, established her own group with Charles Weidman after leaving Denishawn (2). Her technique was based on the principles of fall and recovery - the arc between surrendering to gravity and resisting it - providing an American alternative to Graham's breathing-based contraction and release (2). This widened the technical vocabulary of the emerging art form: two indigenous American techniques, developed independently of ballet, consolidated modern dance's identity as individualistic, angular and earthy (2).
Compare the treatment of the aural setting in Sutra and Appalachian Spring. (8 marks)
Answer: Appalachian Spring: Aaron Copland's commissioned score - concert music integral to the work's Americana; although early modern dance minimised the importance of music, Graham's collaboration with Copland shows music and dance in mutual coexistence; the Library of Congress premiere (30 October 1944) framed it as art-music dance (3-4). Sutra: Szymon Brzoska's composed score, with live percussion and vocal elements interwoven with the physical sound of Gormley's wooden boxes - the audible aspect of the dance (knocking, sliding) becomes part of the aural setting itself (3-4). Comparison: both use newly composed scores rather than borrowed popular music (contrast with Rooster's Rolling Stones recordings), but Sutra integrates the dancers' own sound production, blurring the line between aural setting and movement (1).
