Performance

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA

AQA 7237 A-level Dance performance revision: solo and quartet tasks, physical, technical, expressive and safe practice skills - alignment, projection, musicality and the assessment grids, A* sufficient.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Apply the requirements of Assessment Task 1: perform a solo of 2-3 minutes in the style of a specified practitioner from a prescribed set work or area of study (AO1).
  • Apply the requirements of Assessment Task 2: perform within a quartet for 3-4 minutes in a style relevant to the defined genres (modern, ballet, jazz).
  • Demonstrate precision and control of physical/technical skills - alignment, flexion, extension, rotation, whole body participation/isolation, transference of weight, locomotion, elevation, balance.
  • Demonstrate spatial control (personal and stage space), spatial awareness with sensitivity to other dancers, and dynamic variation.
  • Apply interpretative/performance skills - projection, focus, expression, emphasis, timing, musicality - to communicate the dance idea(s) in a clear and considered manner.
  • Apply safe practice: correct alignment, technical accuracy, appropriate dancewear and presentation of self, and a healthy approach to training including the lifestyle of a dancer.
  • Prepare through the rehearsal process and perform confidently in the live, risk-assessed assessment environment.

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Alignment: The correct stacking of head, ribcage, spine, pelvis, knees and ankles; the foundation of safe, efficient technique.
Transference of weight: Controlled shifting of body weight between points of support; underpins locomotion, falls and partnering.
Locomotion: Travelling movement across space (walk, run, leap, slide) executed with clear direction and momentum.
Elevation: Height achieved in jumps and leaps, with controlled, quiet landings.
Kinaesthetic awareness: The dancer's internal sense of body position, weight and movement in space, enabling continuous self-correction.
Neuromuscular coordination: The nervous system and muscles working together to produce smooth, accurate movement.
Projection: Directing movement energy outward to reach the audience; sustaining performance presence to the back of the space.
Musicality: Sensitivity to the music beyond the beat - phrasing, texture, accent and silence - embodied in movement.
Dynamic variation: Command of contrast in attack, weight, flow and tempo within performance.
Isolation: Precise independent movement of one body part while the rest of the body remains controlled or still.

Two Practical Tasks, One AO: Solo and Quartet

Component 1 assesses AO1: perform dance through the application of physical, technical, interpretative and performance skills through two tasks, each marked out of 20 by an external AQA visiting examiner (visits normally March-May):

  • Assessment Task 1 - solo in the style of a specified practitioner: minimum two, maximum three minutes. The practitioner must be the choreographer of a prescribed set work or a named practitioner within one of the prescribed areas of study, from any area of study, and may be the same as or different from your Component 2 choices. The solo can be choreographed by you, your teacher, a dance artist, or a collaboration - but it must encapsulate the practitioner's style without being a reconstruction of existing repertoire.
  • Assessment Task 2 - performance within a quartet: minimum three, maximum four minutes, in any style relevant to the defined genres (modern, ballet, jazz). No practitioner is required; if you use one, it must differ from your solo practitioner. If fewer than four candidates are entered, the centre may add non-examined dancers (including students from other year groups) of appropriate standard and physical maturity.

Both tasks require a programme note of no more than 150 words: the solo note identifies the practitioner and reveals insight into the stylistic features explored; the quartet note references the style(s) and the dance idea(s) if applicable. The performance must take place in a live setting, formally risk assessed, with sufficient warm-up time, under teacher supervision for authentication, in the academic year of certification. Under-length or over-length performance incurs a timing penalty scaled to the severity of the infringement. Evidence: a single-camera, unedited recording from an audience perspective, name and candidate number at the start.

Choosing your solo practitioner strategically

Pick a practitioner whose stylistic vocabulary you can genuinely embody: Graham (contraction and release, floorwork), Alston (Cunningham-based clarity and speed), Bruce (theatrical synthesis of modern, ballet and folk colour), Bournonville (ballon and quick petit batterie) or Fosse (angled lines, isolations, hunched posture). The examiner marks style-embodiment in every column of the grid, so half-embodying a stylistically distant practitioner caps your mark.

In the quartet, casting is a performance decision: additional non-examined dancers must have the physical maturity and standard to complete the choreography, and examined dancers must rehearse sensitivity - matching timing, sharing weight safely, holding spacing - since the quartet grid credits spatial awareness including sensitivity to other dancers as its own column. A quartet of strong soloists who ignore each other cannot reach the top band.

Task logistics checklist
  • Externally set tasks: the solo and quartet tasks remain unchanged for the lifetime of the specification; the choreography task list is published on Centre Services by 15 September.
  • Live assessment in the academic year of certification; formally risk-assessed setting; sufficient warm-up; teacher supervision for authentication.
  • Evidence: single-camera, audience-perspective, start-to-finish, unedited recording; student named with candidate number; candidate record form signed.
  • Programme notes handed to the examiner at the start of the live assessment.
  • Timing penalties: applied for infringement of minimum/maximum duration, scaled to severity.

Physical and Technical Skills

The specification lists physical skills that underpin both tasks: flexibility, strength, cardiovascular endurance, muscular endurance, agility, neuromuscular coordination and kinaesthetic awareness. These are trained conditions - the examiner sees their product as precision and control. Build them through supplementary conditioning, class technique and progressive rehearsal, not on assessment day.

Technical skills credited in the grids:

  • Correct alignment - the stacking of head, ribcage, pelvis, knees and ankles that makes movement safe and efficient.
  • Flexion, extension, rotation - controlled joint actions executed with the range the style demands (turn-out maintained in ballet vocabulary; parallel in much modern work).
  • Whole body participation and/or isolation - integrated movement versus precise isolation of body parts (central to jazz technique).
  • Transference of weight - smooth, controlled shifts between support points; underpins partnering and falls.
  • Locomotion - travelling actions (walk, run, leap, slide) with clarity of direction and momentum.
  • Elevation - height in jumps and leaps, with controlled landing.
  • Balance - sustained stillness on one or few points of support, in the style's required alignment.

The grid language matters: top bands demand exceptional and safe demonstration of precision and control in relation to the specified practitioner (solo) or in relation to style/genre (quartet). Safety is embedded in the descriptor - a technically showy but unsafe performance cannot reach the top band.

Train each physical skill deliberately: flexibility through progressive stretching after class; strength through resistance and body-conditioning work (core for alignment, legs for elevation); cardiovascular and muscular endurance through sustained full runs of the assessed material; agility through rapid direction-change drills; neuromuscular coordination and kinaesthetic awareness through class technique, partner feedback and video review. The grid's top bands describe a sustained state, not a flash - conditioning underpins everything the examiner sees.

Pre-assessment skills audit

Check each grid column in rehearsal video: precision and control of technique; spatial control or awareness; dynamics; timing and musicality; focus, projection, emphasis, expression. Any column you cannot evidence in the footage is the one to drill - the grid marks columns separately, so one weak column caps the task.

Spatial, Dynamic and Expressive Skills

Spatial elements: the solo grid rewards spatial control (personal and stage space) - direction, levels, shape/form - meaning you command both your kinesphere and the stage picture. The quartet grid rewards spatial awareness including sensitivity to other dancers: maintaining formations and spacing, safe partnering, and adapting to the quartet in real time.

Dynamic elements (solo): rhythmic control and dynamic variation - command of attack, weight, flow and tempo change, matching the practitioner's dynamic signature (Fosse's controlled isolations against sudden bursts; Graham's percussive contraction against sustained release).

Interpretative/performance skills exist to communicate the dance idea(s) in a clear and considered manner - the specification lists projection, focus, expression, emphasis, timing and musicality:

  • Projection: energy reaching the audience; performing to the back row even in a small studio.
  • Focus: deliberate direction of the eyes - focal shifts shape meaning (inward focus for introspection, outward for confrontation).
  • Expression: facial and physical expression consistent with the dance idea and style.
  • Emphasis: giving certain moments weight so the audience reads what matters.
  • Timing: accurate response to the aural setting and to other dancers.
  • Musicality: sensitivity to the music beyond the beat - phrasing, texture, accent, silence.

Note the grid splits interpretative skills into two credited columns - timing and musicality, and focus, projection, emphasis and expression - so neglecting one pair caps the mark regardless of brilliance elsewhere.

Solo grid (20 marks) at a glance

Five columns, 4 marks each: physical/technical skills; spatial elements (personal and stage space); dynamic elements; interpretative skills (timing and musicality); interpretative skills (focus, projection, emphasis and expression). Bands: exceptional (4), highly articulate (3), proficient (2), adequate (1), nothing worthy of credit (0).

Quartet grid (20 marks) at a glance

Four columns, 5 marks each: physical/technical skills; spatial awareness (including sensitivity to other dancers); timing and musicality; focus, projection, emphasis and expression. Bands: exceptional (5), highly articulate (4), proficient (3), sound (2), adequate (1), nothing worthy of credit (0).

Train expressive skills as deliberately as technique: projection through performing phrases to a distant point and filming from the back of the room; focus through rehearsing deliberate focal shifts marked into the choreography; timing and musicality through clapping phrases, marking accents and dancing to silence. In the solo, match your interpretative choices to the practitioner: Fosse's fixed, inward gaze; Graham's dramatic intensity; Alston's clean musical phrasing. Expression inconsistent with style reads as decoration and caps the interpretative columns.

Rehearsal Process, Performance Environment and Safe Practice

Safe practice (spec 3.1.1) is examinable knowledge and a condition of assessment. Know all four requirements: awareness of correct alignment; technical accuracy (executing movement as intended - inaccurate technique is itself unsafe); appropriate dancewear and presentation of self (correct footwear or barefoot convention for the style, hair secured, no jewellery); and a healthy approach to training, including the lifestyle of a dancer (progressive warm-up and cool-down, hydration, nutrition, sleep, load management, injury reporting). The examiner will only conduct assessments where health and safety requirements are securely in place.

Rehearsal process converts skill into performance: learn material accurately, mark through for spacing and safety, build full runs to develop stamina, then rehearse in performance conditions - same floor, same aural setting, entrances and exits rehearsed. For the quartet, rehearse sensitivity deliberately: blind-spot awareness, counterbalance and lift progressions, and recovery from slips without breaking the ensemble picture.

Performance environment: the assessed performance happens in a live, formally risk-assessed setting, and the recording must be the performance being assessed - one take, single camera, audience perspective, unedited, in the academic year of certification. Adapt projection and focus to the venue: a studio assessment needs less force than a theatre, but never perform flatly. Treat the examiner as an audience member; do not perform to the examiner.

Finally, embodying a specified practitioner means evidencing their style in every credited column: alignment, rotation and balance executed as Graham, Alston, Bruce, Bournonville or Fosse would demand; dynamics matching their signature; timing and musicality inside their aural world; expression consistent with their choreographic intent. Study film of the practitioner's repertoire, take class in the style, and let the programme note signal precisely which stylistic features the examiner should expect to see.

The lifestyle of a dancer is named in the spec: sleep for recovery, hydration and nutrition for energy, load management to avoid overuse injury, and honest reporting of niggles before they become injuries. In the performance environment, plan the space: rehearse entrances, exits and spacing to the actual room, check the floor is risk assessed and fit for the style (sprung floor for elevation work), and agree how the aural setting will be played. Walk in as a performer: warm-up complete, dancewear and presentation right, focus already on the dance idea - the assessment begins the moment you enter the space.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: Assessment Task 1 (AQA 7237): perform a solo for a minimum of two minutes and a maximum of three minutes in the style of a specified practitioner, in a live performance setting. With reference to the solo performance assessment grid, explain how the solo is marked out of 20 and what distinguishes an exceptional performance from a proficient one. (20 marks)
Mark-scheme logic: Mark-scheme logic: (1) grid structure - five columns of 4 marks: physical/technical skills; spatial elements (personal and stage space); dynamic elements; interpretative skills (timing and musicality); interpretative skills (focus, projection, emphasis and expression); every column judged in relation to the specified practitioner. (2) Exceptional = safe demonstration of precision and control sustained throughout: technical skill executed with the practitioner's stylistic vocabulary; total command of personal and stage space; dynamic control matching the practitioner's signature; timing/musicality and focus/projection/expression that embody the dance idea(s) completely and convincingly. (3) Proficient (2) = secure but with lapses - occasional loss of alignment, momentary breaks in focus or projection, dynamics reproduced but not fully owned. (4) Timing: under 2 or over 3 minutes triggers a penalty scaled to severity; duration is checked before assessment. (5) The programme note (max 150 words) frames the examiner's expectation by naming the practitioner and the stylistic features explored. (6) Top-band strategy: choose a style you can embody in every column, embed safety, rehearse under assessment conditions.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Explain what is meant by correct alignment and why it underpins both safe practice and technical accuracy. (4 marks)
Answer: Alignment is the correct stacking of head, ribcage, spine, pelvis, knees and ankles (1). Safe practice: aligned joints place load on muscles rather than ligaments and joint surfaces, reducing injury risk in flexion, extension, rotation and landing (1-2). Technical accuracy: the style's vocabulary is built on alignment - turn-out, balance and elevation are only executed as intended when aligned, and the assessment grid embeds safety within its top descriptors (1-2).
Describe how a dancer demonstrates timing and musicality in a solo performance. (4 marks)
Answer: Timing: accurate response to the aural setting - landing accents with the music, holding silence, matching tempo changes (2). Musicality: sensitivity beyond the beat - shaping phrases to the music's phrasing, colouring movement with texture and accent, exploiting counterpoint or suspending movement against a sustained note (2). Both are separate credited columns in the solo grid, judged in relation to the specified practitioner's style.
Explain how spatial awareness differs between solo and quartet performance. (6 marks)
Answer: Solo: the grid rewards spatial control of personal and stage space - command of direction, levels and shape/form; you own the whole stage picture (2). Quartet: the grid rewards spatial awareness including sensitivity to other dancers - maintaining spacing and formations, safe partnering, adapting to others' timing, recovering from slips without breaking the ensemble picture (2). In quartet work your spatial decisions are relational: a gap, counterbalance or pathway only communicates in response to the other three dancers, so awareness replaces individual command as the priority (2).
Discuss how a performer embodies the style of a specified practitioner without reconstructing existing repertoire. (8 marks)
Answer: Embodiment means performing the practitioner's vocabulary, dynamics and intent, not copying a dance (2). Study film and written sources of the practitioner's repertoire to identify signature features - e.g. Graham's contraction/release and floorwork, Fosse's angled lines and isolations, Bournonville's ballon (2). Compose or commission original material that showcases those features: alignment, rotation and balance executed as the practitioner would demand (2). Match dynamics and aural world: attack, weight, flow, timing and musicality inside the practitioner's idiom (2). Top-band responses show the examiner consistent style-embodiment in every grid column, with expression consistent with the practitioner's choreographic intent.
State the requirements for the quartet performance task, including duration, programme note and the use of additional dancers. (6 marks)
Answer: Duration: minimum three, maximum four minutes in a live performance setting; timing penalties apply outside this range (1). Style: any style relevant to the defined genres; a practitioner is optional but must differ from the solo practitioner and need not be a reconstruction (1-2). Programme note: maximum 150 words referencing the style(s) and the dance idea(s) if applicable (1). Additional dancers: if fewer than four candidates are entered, non-examined dancers (including students from other year groups) of appropriate standard and physical maturity may be used (1-2). Evidence: single-camera, unedited recording from an audience perspective with name and candidate number at the start.
