Sociological Theories and Perspectives

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA, OCR, WJEC/Eduqas

Consensus, conflict, structural and action theories from Durkheim to Giddens, with evaluation, synoptic theory-and-methods links and 30-mark essay technique for AQA 7192, OCR H580 and Eduqas.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Classify perspectives along the structural/action and consensus/conflict axes (AQA 4.4.1; OCR; Eduqas AC1.1)
  • Set out functionalist, Marxist and feminist accounts of society with named theorists and precise concepts
  • Explain Weber's social action theory, symbolic interactionism, phenomenology, ethnomethodology and Giddens' structuration
  • Evaluate each perspective with counter-theorists, studies and postmodernist critique (AO3)
  • Deploy synoptic theory-and-methods links (MODs) in 10- and 30-mark answers
  • Match theoretical content to command words and reach justified conclusions

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Structural theory: An approach explaining behaviour through the organisation of society — institutions and social class shaping individuals from above; includes functionalism, Marxism and feminism.
Social action theory: An approach (Weber) starting from the subjective meanings individuals attach to their actions; society is the ongoing product of interaction rather than a determining structure.
Value consensus: In functionalism, general agreement in society about norms and values which integrates individuals and underpins social order.
Anomie: Durkheim: normlessness — the breakdown of social rules during rapid change, leaving individuals without moral regulation; also the starting point of Merton's strain theory.
False class consciousness: The Marxist claim that the proletariat misperceives its interests because ruling-class ideology presents inequality as natural, preventing revolutionary action.
Hegemony: Gramsci: ruling-class domination achieved through consent — ideas and institutions win voluntary acceptance — rather than force; contested until a counter-hegemony is built.
Patriarchy: A structure of male dominance over women operating through institutions (family, work, media); the core concept of feminist theory.
Verstehen: Weber's empathetic understanding: grasping the subjective meanings actors give to their behaviour before explaining it sociologically.
Typification: Schutz (phenomenology): shared common-sense categories people use to classify others and organise interaction, so social reality is constituted through them.
Structuration: Giddens (1984): structure and action are two sides of one process — structures enable and constrain action, while action reproduces or transforms structures.
Meta-narrative: Lyotard (1979): a grand explanatory story (e.g. Marxism, science-as-progress) claiming universal truth; postmodernists argue such narratives have lost credibility.

📌 Key Points

Functionalism: Durkheim (social facts, solidarity, anomie), Parsons (value consensus, functional fit, socialisation and social control), Merton (dysfunctions; manifest and latent functions).
Marxism: Marx (class conflict, exploitation, alienation, ideology), Althusser (ideological/repressive state apparatuses), Gramsci (hegemony); evaluated by Willis (1977).
Feminism: liberal (Oakley), radical (Millett, Firestone), Marxist (Ansley), difference and poststructural (Butler's performativity).
Action theories: Weber (verstehen, four action types), symbolic interactionism (Becker, Goffman), phenomenology (Schutz), ethnomethodology (Garfinkel), structuration (Giddens).
Postmodernism: Lyotard, Baudrillard; counters — it is internally contradictory and understates measurable inequality (Harvey; Best and Kellner).
AO3 rule: every claim needs a named study or counter-theory; adjectives alone never earn evaluation marks.

Mapping the field: structure versus action, consensus versus conflict

AQA 4.4.1 (Theory and Methods), OCR H580 and Eduqas AC1.1 expect you to classify every perspective along two axes. Structural vs action: structural theories (functionalism, Marxism, feminism) treat society as an external force shaping individuals from above; action theories (Weber, symbolic interactionism, phenomenology, ethnomethodology) begin with the meanings individuals give to their behaviour. Consensus vs conflict: consensus theories see order resting on shared agreement; conflict theories see order resting on the power of one group over another.

  • Macro/micro: functionalism, Marxism and feminism are macro theories; interactionism, phenomenology and ethnomethodology are micro theories.
  • Why it matters: most 30-mark questions name a perspective; the A* skill is locating it on both axes and letting that drive evaluation — e.g. functionalism is structural-consensus, so it struggles to explain conflict and power.
  • Synoptic link (MODs): structural theories pair naturally with quantitative methods (questionnaires, official statistics) because they seek general patterns; action theories pair with qualitative methods (unstructured interviews, participant observation) because they seek meanings — examiners explicitly reward such links.

The structural theories: functionalism, Marxism and feminism

Functionalism sees society as a system of interdependent parts, each performing functions that maintain order. Durkheim (1893) argued society exists over and above individuals — 'social facts' are external and constraining — and modern societies hold together through organic solidarity, with anomie the danger when regulation collapses. Parsons (1951) added the value consensus, functional fit (institutions adapt as society moves from traditional to modern) and two mechanisms of integration: socialisation and social control. Merton (1938) criticised from within: institutions can be dysfunctional, and functions may be manifest (intended) or latent (unintended).

Marxism sees society divided by class conflict. For Marx, the capitalist mode of production rests on exploitation of the proletariat, producing alienation and ruling-class ideology that makes inequality appear natural. Neo-Marxists refined this: Althusser (1971) distinguished Ideological and Repressive State Apparatuses, with education and media transmitting bourgeois ideology; Gramsci (1971) explained durable domination as hegemony — rule by consent — which revolutionaries must contest with a counter-hegemonic bloc. Willis (1977) supplied the classic evaluation: his 12 'lads' formed a counter-school culture, showing working-class pupils partially saw through ideology yet still drifted into manual work.

Feminism treats patriarchy as the fundamental division. Liberal feminism (Oakley) seeks reform within the system — equal pay, shared parenting; radical feminism (Millett, Firestone) sees patriarchy as the oldest and deepest structure demanding revolutionary change, including separatism; Marxist feminism (Ansley: women as 'takers of shit' absorbing male frustration) links women's subordination to capitalism; difference and poststructural feminism (Butler) reject a single category of 'women', emphasising diversity and the performativity of gender.

Action theories: Weber to Giddens

Weber operates on two levels: structural factors (class, status, party) set the context, but sociology must use verstehen — empathetic understanding of subjective meanings. He classified action into four ideal types: instrumentally rational, value-rational, traditional and affectual.

Meanings and symbols: symbolic interactionism (Blumer, Becker, Goffman) studies how meanings arise in interaction through symbols. Becker (1963) showed deviance is created by audience reaction and labelling, with 'moral entrepreneurs' applying labels; Goffman's dramaturgy analysed how actors manage impressions on a 'front stage'. Phenomenology (Schutz) argues actors experience the world through typifications — shared common-sense categories — so reality is socially constructed. Garfinkel's ethnomethodology (1967) demonstrated how fragile those categories are through breaching experiments, such as students acting as lodgers in their own homes.

Synthesis: Giddens' structuration (1984) dissolves the structure-action dualism: structures are both the medium and the outcome of action (the 'duality of structure') — rules and resources enable as well as constrain, and persist only because actors keep drawing on them. AO3: critics argue structuration is too abstract to research empirically; Margaret Archer insists structure and action must be analytically separated because structures pre-exist the individuals who reproduce them.

Evaluation: postmodernism and the limits of every perspective

Postmodernism (Lyotard 1979; Baudrillard) argues that in post-Fordist, media-saturated society the meta-narratives — Marxism's class story, functionalism's consensus story, even science-as-progress — have lost credibility; identity is now constructed through consumption and choice rather than class. AO3 response: critics (Harvey; Best and Kellner) argue postmodernism is itself a meta-narrative, is internally contradictory (making universal claims that there are no universal claims) and understates continuing structural inequality — class, gender and ethnic gaps remain measurable and material.

Functionalism — where it fails

Understates conflict and power; assumes a value consensus that may simply reflect dominant groups; explanations risk teleology ('institutions exist because they have functions') and circularity; Merton's own internal critique concedes dysfunctions. Yet it remains the benchmark for explaining integration and socialisation — e.g. Durkheim on education creating shared values.

Marxism — where it fails

Economic determinism (Weber: class is one dimension among several; feminists: gender matters too); the predicted revolution never arrived in advanced capitalism; Willis shows pupils resist ideology. Still useful: Althusser and Gramsci explain consent, and neo-Marxism remains the reference point for analysing power.

Feminism and action theories — where they fail

Radical feminism risks treating all men as uniformly privileged, while difference feminism can fragment into un-theorisable individualism. Action theories ignore structural constraints — a pupil's poverty shapes life chances regardless of meaning. Yet Becker (1963) and Garfinkel (1967) show meanings genuinely shape outcomes, so the strongest answers combine levels (Weber, Giddens).

  • Examiner tip: never evaluate a perspective as merely 'outdated' or 'too simplistic' — name the theorist making the counter-claim and the evidence they would deploy.

Exam technique: command words, structure and AO balance

On AQA 7192, theory appears in short item questions (4-6 marks), 10-mark 'outline and analyse two...' questions and 30-mark 'outline and evaluate...' essays; OCR asks 'assess'/'evaluate' style questions (up to 30 marks) and Eduqas uses extended 'discuss' essays. AO weighting: 30-mark essays are roughly AO1 8 / AO2 8 / AO3 14 — evaluation carries the most credit.

Command wordWhat it demandsAO focus
Outline and analyse twoTwo developed points, each applied to the questionAO1 + AO2
Outline and evaluateKnowledge plus sustained debate with a reached judgementAO1 + AO2 + AO3
Assess / discuss how farWeigh evidence for and against a claimAO3-heavy

30-mark skeleton: (1) define the perspective with its key concepts and theorists; (2) three or four developed points, each with a named study or application; (3) three or four evaluation points, each with a counter-theorist or study; (4) a conclusion answering the actual question with a weighed judgement — never 'both views have merit'.

Timing: 30 marks ≈ 30 minutes: plan for 4, write for 24, check for 2. Common pitfalls: describing theory at length then bolting on one evaluation; confusing Marx and Weber on class; writing 'society says' without naming who; omitting the synoptic theory-and-methods link that AO marks reward.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: 1. Outline and evaluate the contribution of action theories to our understanding of society. [30 marks] 2. Outline and analyse two similarities between functionalist and Marxist theory. [10 marks]
Mark-scheme logic: Q1 (30 marks): AO1 — accurate knowledge of Weber (verstehen, four action types), symbolic interactionism (Becker, Goffman), phenomenology (Schutz, typifications), ethnomethodology (Garfinkel's breaching experiments) and Giddens' structuration. AO2 — apply each to social topics (labelling in education and crime, everyday interaction). AO3 — evaluate with structuralist rebuttals (class and poverty constrain regardless of meaning), Archer's critique of structuration as too abstract, empirical successes (Garfinkel 1967; Becker 1963), and a reached judgement that action theories best explain micro-processes but need structural context. Q2 (10 marks): two developed similarities with application, e.g. both are structural theories explaining behaviour through society shaping individuals; both make socialisation central to maintaining order; both are macro, modernist claims to general truths. Developed application only — no extended evaluation required.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Outline and analyse two ways in which action theories differ from structural theories. [10 marks]
Answer: Mark-scheme logic: two developed points with application. (1) Starting point: action theories begin with actors' meanings (Weber's verstehen; Schutz's typifications) whereas structural theories begin with institutions shaping behaviour from above (Parsons' value consensus; Marx's mode of production). (2) Unit of analysis: action theories are micro, studying face-to-face interaction (Garfinkel's breaching experiments) whereas structural theories are macro, seeking general laws across societies (Durkheim's social facts). Accept any two, each worth up to 5 marks with development.
Outline and evaluate functionalist explanations of social order. [30 marks]
Answer: AO1: Durkheim — social facts, collective conscience, anomie; Parsons — value consensus, socialisation and social control as integration mechanisms; Merton — functions, dysfunctions. AO2: apply to order in education, religion, crime (boundary maintenance). AO3: Marxism argues order reflects ruling-class power not consensus (Gramsci's hegemony); interactionism shows order is negotiated moment to moment (Cicourel); functionalism underplays coercion and change; yet it usefully explains why most people conform most of the time. Conclusion must judge how far consensus explains order, with a weighed statement.
Outline and analyse two criticisms Marxism makes of functionalism. [10 marks]
Answer: (1) Consensus masks power: what functionalism calls value consensus is ruling-class ideology — schools and media transmit bourgeois values (Althusser's ideological state apparatuses), so order serves the capitalist class, not society as a whole. (2) Institutions reproduce inequality, not integration: the correspondence principle (Bowles and Gintis) shows schools mirror workplace hierarchy, so functionalism mistakes class reproduction for shared values. Each point developed to 5 marks with application.
Outline and evaluate the view that patriarchy is the most important form of inequality in society. [30 marks]
Answer: AO1: radical feminism (Millett, Firestone — patriarchy as primary and universal; the personal is political), supported by evidence on the domestic division of labour (Oakley 1974) and domestic violence (Dobash and Dobash 1980). AO2: apply across family, work and media representation. AO3: Marxist feminists argue capitalism is primary (Ansley); Weberians argue class and status interact; difference feminism warns against one universal 'women's' experience (Butler); measurable class gaps in wealth and life chances suggest class remains central. Conclusion must weigh patriarchy against class with a judgement.
Outline and analyse two reasons why postmodernists reject meta-narratives. [10 marks]
Answer: (1) Diversity and choice: in media-saturated consumer society (Lyotard 1979) no single class or progress story can capture fragmented identities and multiple realities. (2) Lost faith in total explanations: grand narratives such as Marxism and science-as-progress claim universal truth, but post-Fordist society produces competing, localised knowledges with equal claim to credibility (Baudrillard's hyperreality). Each developed with application to 5 marks.
