Methods in Context and Theory

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA, OCR, WJEC/Eduqas

PET evaluation of questionnaires, interviews, observations and documents applied to education for the AQA 7192 20-mark methods in context question and OCR H580/02, plus positivism, interpretivism and value freedom for the 30-mark theory-and-methods essays.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Apply practical, ethical and theoretical (PET) evaluation to questionnaires, interviews, observations and documents in education settings (AQA 4.1.4; OCR H580/02)
  • Write the 20-mark methods in context answer with sustained application to the named education topic (AQA Paper 1 Q5: AO1 + AO2 only)
  • Anticipate education-specific research problems: classroom access, gatekeepers, safeguarding, the Hawthorne effect and teacher-pupil power
  • Deploy education research studies (Willis, Lacey, Ball, Mac an Ghaill, Gillborn and Youdell) as applied evidence
  • Link method choice to positivism versus interpretivism and to debates over value freedom (AQA 4.4)
  • Plan 30-mark theory-and-methods essays on sociology as a science and value freedom (AQA 7192 Paper 3; synoptic theory essays on OCR H580/03)

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Methods in context: The exam task (AQA 4.1.4): applying knowledge of one named research method to a given education topic, weighted towards AO2 application; assessed similarly in the compulsory methods section of OCR H580/02.
PET evaluation: Framework for assessing a method by practical issues (time, money, access), ethical issues (consent, confidentiality, harm) and theoretical issues (validity, reliability, representativeness, positivism versus interpretivism).
Gatekeeper: Anyone controlling access to a research setting or population; in education: headteachers, local authorities, the DfE, ethics committees and parents (who consent on behalf of children).
Hawthorne effect: Behaviour change caused by awareness of being studied; in classrooms pupils perform for the observer, threatening validity — reduced by long immersion and participant observation, cured only by covert methods.
Overt observation: Open observation in which those studied know the researcher's identity and purpose; preserves consent but invites the Hawthorne effect.
Impression management: Goffman's term for the conscious shaping of how one is seen; teachers used to being asked questions may present a professional 'front' in interviews, lowering validity.
Performativity: Ball (2003): the institutional pressure to measure and display results; makes official education statistics (league tables, exclusions, attendance) potentially distorted by triage and hidden exclusions.
Value freedom: Weber's ideal that findings be free of the researcher's values, distinct from value-relevance (values legitimately choose the topic); attacked by Becker and Gouldner, who argue some standpoint is unavoidable.
Whose side are we on?: Becker (1967): complete neutrality is impossible because research is always conducted from a standpoint; sociologists should side with the underdog (pupils rather than schools).
Sociology as a science: Debate over whether sociology can be scientific: positivists (Comte, Durkheim) say yes via objective study of social facts; interpretivists object; Popper (falsification) and Kuhn (paradigms) qualify the claim; realists (Keat and Urry 1975) offer a middle position via open and closed systems.

📌 Key Points

AQA methods in context (Paper 1 Q5): 20 marks, AO1 + AO2 only — sustained application to the named education topic is what separates levels (AQA 4.1.4).
Every PET point must be APPLIED: 'registers give questionnaires a complete sampling frame' beats a generic list of strengths.
Education-specific strengths: registers as sampling frames, fixed timetables easing access, anonymous surveys drawing out honest pupil answers.
Education-specific limitations: gatekeepers (headteacher, DfE, parents), DBS and safeguarding checks, strong Hawthorne effect with children, teacher-pupil power distorting data.
Studies to deploy: Willis (1977) group interviews with the lads; Lacey (1970) Hightown Grammar; Ball (1981) Beachside; Mac an Ghaill (1988) teacher-researcher; Gillborn and Youdell (2000) Rationing Education.
30-mark theory-and-methods essays (AQA 4.4): positivism versus interpretivism underpins method choice; value freedom (Weber, Becker, Gouldner) supplies the evaluation.

The methods in context question: what examiners reward

Both boards test the same skill: applying methods knowledge to a given education topic, not reciting generic strengths. On AQA 7192 this is the compulsory final question of Paper 1 (spec 4.1.4): a short Item on an education topic (labelling, pupil subcultures, class differences) plus a 20-mark demand to evaluate the strengths and limitations of using a named method to investigate it. The 20 marks are assessed through AO1 (knowledge of the method) and AO2 (application to education) only — there is no separate AO3 theory evaluation, which is why examiner reports repeatedly warn against drifting into generic methods lists. On OCR H580/02 (Researching and understanding social inequalities) the compulsory methods section combines item-based short answers with an extended evaluation of a named method applied to a given education or inequality context; application again dominates the marks.

The drill: every PET point must name the classroom actors and settings — pupils, teachers, headteachers, parents, registers, timetables — and explain how the method's feature specifically helps or hinders research on this topic.

  • Structure that scores: brief intro (method + topic), 3-4 PET paragraphs each pairing a strength with a limitation, judgement-led conclusion.
  • Application beats volume: four tightly-applied points outscore eight generic ones.
  • Use the Item: quote phrases from it and hang method points on them — that is where AO2 credit sits.
  • Theory lives inside T: positivist versus interpretivist judgements about data quality count as the theoretical limb of PET (AQA 4.4).

Level descriptors reward range (several distinct strengths and limitations), application (each point tied to the education context) and quality of argument (an explicit overall judgement). Three common failures: describing the method instead of evaluating it; listing generic strengths with no reference to classrooms, pupils or teachers; and answering a different research focus than the one the item names — re-read the item before planning and quote its exact topic phrase in the intro.

Questionnaires in schools

Schools already run them: Ofsted-style pupil voice surveys and anti-bullying questionnaires. Response rates rise when teachers administer the survey, but that itself biases answers — pupils respond as they think the teacher wants (Goffman's impression management). Positivists prize the reliability and representativeness of large pupil samples; interpretivists reply that answers are artefacts of the instrument — vocabulary a Year 7 cannot read produces data that measures reading age, not attitudes.

Interviews with pupils and teachers

Willis (1977), Learning to Labour, used group interviews and observation with 12 'lads': the group format let the counter-school culture speak — but peer pressure produces bravado and conformity effects alongside it. Unstructured one-to-one interviews suit sensitive topics and powerful respondents: Ball, Bowe and Gewirtz (1994) interviewed headteachers on marketisation. Costs: time, and guarded 'official' answers from teachers skilled at being observed.

Observing classrooms

Lacey (1970) Hightown Grammar and Ball (1981) Beachside Comprehensive: overt participant observation by researchers with insider status (Lacey taught at the school). Jackson (1968), Life in Classrooms, non-participant observation of the 'hype' of classroom rewards. The Hawthorne effect is strongest with children, who perform for adult audiences; long immersion (Woods 1979 on pupil strategies) reduces it. Covert observation of pupils is ethically near-impossible: no meaningful consent, safeguarding breached.

Documents and official statistics

Personal documents (pupil diaries, letters) are rare and hard to authenticate. School documents split into public (prospectuses, websites, league tables) and private (pupil files — access heavily restricted). Official statistics (DfE attainment, exclusions, attendance) are free, up-to-date and cover whole cohorts, but Gillborn and Youdell (2000), Rationing Education, showed league-table pressure producing triage and hidden exclusions: the statistic measures institutional responses as much as pupil behaviour (AQA 4.2.1).

Experiments in education

Rare in schools but examinable. Field experiments suit the context: Rosenthal and Jacobson (1968) told teachers that randomly selected pupils were about to 'bloom' and measured a self-fulfilling prophecy in later attainment — cheap, natural setting, real classroom behaviour; but teachers were deceived, the sample was one school, and effects were modest. Laboratory-style studies of classrooms control variables well yet are artificial — pupils do not behave normally under controlled conditions, and access plus consent make them rare.

Education-specific traps: access, gatekeepers, Hawthorne and power

Four problems recur in examiner reports because they are unique to, or sharpest in, schools. Revise one applied paragraph on each.

  1. Classroom access. Schools are closed institutions: entry needs headteacher (often local authority or DfE) permission, DBS checks and staff cooperation, and timetables fix when research can happen. Insider status helps — Ball and Mac an Ghaill (1988) researched schools they taught in — but creates loyalty conflicts: Mac an Ghaill was simultaneously colleague to the teachers and researcher of their labelling practices.
  2. Gatekeepers and consent. Children cannot give legally meaningful consent alone: parents, schools and ethics committees (BSA code of practice) must agree, and any gatekeeper can veto the research or demand changes — for example forbidding questions on ethnicity — that bias the findings.
  3. The Hawthorne effect. Children are especially self-conscious before adult observers; classroom behaviour shifts when a researcher is present. Participant observation and long immersion reduce it; covert methods cure it but breach consent.
  4. Teacher-pupil power and presentation. Both actors manage impressions: teachers present a professional front in interviews (Goffman); pupils answer as they think the adult wants, or perform for attention — Willis's lads misbehaved regardless of observer control, evidence that some pupil cultures resist the researcher. Classroom power asymmetry means pupils often treat the researcher as just another teacher.

Sampling in schools: the register is a near-perfect sampling frame — a rare applied strength worth citing. But random samples still need parental consent forms returned, and response rates are lowest in hard-to-reach households, biasing samples towards cooperative families; the pupils most affected by sensitive outcomes (exclusion, off-rolling, persistent absence) are precisely the hardest to sample. Weigh this against the positivist appeal of large, representative pupil surveys.

Theoretical wrap: positivist designs (questionnaires, statistics) struggle with all four because they presuppose cooperation and stable, natural data; interpretivist designs trade reliability for validity precisely by working around them — this contrast is the core T-paragraph of any strong methods in context answer.

Theory links and the 30-mark essays

Methods in context is applied Theory and Methods: the theory half of the AQA spec (4.4) supplies the deep evaluation that lifts answers into top levels.

  • Positivism (Comte, Durkheim): social facts are external and measurable, so questionnaires, structured interviews and official statistics suit education research on patterns — class differences in attainment analysed the way Durkheim analysed Suicide (1897). Strength: reliable, representative, able to test hypotheses. Limitation: data are constructions of the research process (Cicourel 1968) and miss the meanings behind patterns — labelling, subcultures, pupil identities.
  • Interpretivism (Weber's verstehen; symbolic interactionism): education is built through meanings, so validity demands observation and unstructured interviews — Lacey (1970), Ball (1981), Hargreaves (1967). Strength: authentic actor's-eye accounts of classroom processes. Limitation: unrepresentative, unreliable, Hawthorne-prone, small-scale.
  • Value freedom: Weber distinguished value-relevance (values legitimately choose the topic — studying working-class underachievement is itself a value-laden choice) from value-freedom (objectivity in conduct and findings). Becker (1967) 'Whose side are we on?' and Gouldner's attack on the myth of the value-free sociology deny neutrality is possible; funding also shapes agendas — DfE-commissioned and charity-funded education studies (e.g. Sutton Trust reports) reflect their sponsors' priorities.
  • Sociology as a science: Popper's falsificationism says sociology qualifies where it produces testable hypotheses; Kuhn replies that sociology lacks a single paradigm; realists (Keat and Urry 1975) argue classrooms are open systems — no laws, but scientific study of underlying structures is still possible.

30-mark technique (AQA 7192 Paper 3): essays such as 'Evaluate the view that sociology can and should be value-free' or 'Evaluate the claim that sociology is a science' reward AO1 knowledge, AO2 (where an item is provided) and AO3 analysis. Structure: define the key term; three or four evaluative paragraphs (point, named study, counter-position, mini-judgement); an explicit final judgement. Reuse your methods in context PET points as evidence — the Hawthorne effect supports interpretivist claims that neutral observation is impossible; performativity (Ball 2003) supports the claim that official statistics are not objective facts.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: 1. Item C reports research on anti-school subcultures in a comprehensive school. Applying material from the item and your wider sociological knowledge, evaluate the strengths and limitations of using non-participant observation to investigate pupil subcultures in schools. [20 marks] 2. Evaluate the strengths and limitations of using official statistics to investigate class differences in educational achievement. [20 marks]
Mark-scheme logic: Q1 (20 marks): AO1 — method knowledge: non-participant observation means the researcher watches classroom events without joining in; can be overt or covert. AO2 (the differentiator — every point applied to schools and subcultures): access — headteacher permission, DBS checks and staff cooperation are needed, but fixed timetables make classrooms visible and easy to schedule (practical strength); Hawthorne effect — pupils perform for a visible adult, so subcultures may go underground (Woods 1979), threatening validity (theoretical limitation); unrepresentative single school, unrepeatable so unreliable, no generalisation to schools nationally (theoretical limitation); watching interaction directly captures subculture formation — differentiation and polarisation (Lacey 1970; Ball 1981) — which questionnaires cannot reach (theoretical strength); covert variants breach consent and safeguarding with children, rarely ethical (ethical limitation). PET structure required: 3-4 paragraphs each pairing a strength with a limitation, sustained application throughout. Conclusion: the validity of watching subcultures form outweighs access and ethics costs for a small-scale study, but generalisation to pupils nationally is weak. Q2 (20 marks): official statistics — practical: free, up-to-date, whole-cohort coverage, representative (strength); ethical: no consent issues at aggregate level, but identifiable individuals risk exposure (limitation); theoretical: reliable and positivist, enabling hypothesis-testing on class and attainment, BUT socially constructed (Cicourel), performativity-distorted (Ball 2003) and hiding off-rolling and internal exclusions (Gillborn and Youdell 2000), and they ignore meanings (interpretivist limitation). Judgement: strong for measuring patterns, weak for explaining them.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Applying material from the item and your knowledge, evaluate the strengths and limitations of using questionnaires to investigate pupils' attitudes towards education. [20 marks]
Answer: Intro: closed questionnaires measure attitudes via standardised items. Practical: cheap for large samples; registers give complete sampling frames; whole-class administration fits timetables (strength); but questionnaires cannot probe answers and impose the researcher's meanings (limitation). Ethical: anonymity encourages honest answers on bullying and satisfaction (strength); parental consent needed for minors and some pupils cannot meaningfully consent (limitation). Theoretical: reliable and representative — positivist claim (strength); young pupils misread vocabulary, joke or give sick answers, so validity is low; attitude scales measure how pupils respond to questionnaires, not attitudes (Cicourel-style critique) (limitation). Use the item's phrases throughout. Conclusion: good for breadth on attitudes, weak for depth; combine with interviews.
Evaluate the strengths and limitations of using unstructured interviews to investigate teachers' use of labelling. [20 marks]
Answer: Intro: unstructured interviews are informal, in-depth conversations guided by the interviewee's answers. Practical: time-costly per teacher; access via headteacher as gatekeeper; small samples (limitation); but flexible probing suits a complex, hidden process (strength). Ethical: labelling is sensitive — teachers fear exposure to employers and inspectors, so confidentiality is essential (limitation); informed consent and the right to withdraw apply (strength). Theoretical: valid verstehen — teachers reveal how they construct 'ideal pupil' stereotypes (Becker 1971), producing depth questionnaires lack (strength); interviewee effects and impression management — teachers present a professional front (Goffman), so data may be performances; unrepresentative, unreliable (limitation). Conclusion: the best single method for meanings behind labelling, but small, non-replicable and vulnerable to guarded answers.
Evaluate the strengths and limitations of using covert participant observation to investigate pupil subcultures. [20 marks]
Answer: Intro: covert participant observation means joining the group without revealing the researcher role. Practical: no access negotiations — avoids gatekeeper veto (strength); but researcher must sustain a false identity, cannot take notes openly, long immersion (limitation). Ethical: the decisive weakness — no informed consent, deception, and involvement in pupils' (sometimes harmful) behaviour breaches safeguarding and the BSA code; special justification is required for covert work with children (limitation); anonymity of the school can be preserved (weak strength). Theoretical: highest validity — the Hawthorne effect is eliminated, so genuine subculture behaviour (Willis 1977 lads) is observed (strength); unrepresentative, unreliable, and the researcher may 'go native' (limitation). Conclusion: validity gain is real but ethical costs with minors usually outweigh it — overt participant observation is the defensible alternative.
Outline and explain two ethical problems of using covert observation to investigate classroom behaviour. [10 marks]
Answer: Two developed points, each outlined then explained. 1. Lack of informed consent — those studied cannot agree to the research because they do not know it is happening; explained: this violates autonomy and the BSA code, and is especially serious with children, who additionally cannot legally consent for themselves. 2. Deception and harm — the researcher lies about their identity and may witness or join behaviour that could harm pupils if exposed or reported; explained: trust in institutions is damaged if deception is discovered, and pupils may be identifiable or adversely affected, breaching safeguarding duties owed to minors. (Alternative acceptable points: no right to withdraw; confidentiality difficulties.)
Evaluate the view that sociology can and should be value-free. [30 marks]
Answer: Plan: define value freedom (Weber: findings free of the researcher's values). For: Weber's own position — value-relevance in choosing topics, value-freedom in conduct; positivists (Comte, Durkheim) — objective study of social facts (Suicide 1897) as the scientific route to neutrality; Popper — falsification disciplines subjective commitment; practical argument — only neutral sociology wins funding and trust (Gomm). Against: Becker (1967) 'Whose side are we on?' — a standpoint is unavoidable, so side with the underdog; Gouldner — the 'myth of a value-free sociology' masks the values of those who fund and use research; interpretivists — all data are constructed through meanings (Cicourel), so perfect objectivity is impossible; Marxists and feminists see sociology as committed to exposing exploitation (Willis 1977; Mac an Ghaill 1988); funding shapes agendas (DfE, charities). Judgement: complete value-freedom is unattainable, but Weber's distinction remains useful — values may choose the topic while transparent, rigorous method disciplines how it is studied.
