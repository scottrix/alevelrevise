Sampling and Data

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level Edexcel

Sampling methods, data types, measures of location and dispersion, outliers, box plots and histograms - Edexcel 9ST0 Topics 2-3 revised with exam-style questions and mark-scheme logic.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Select and justify an appropriate sampling method for a given context, and criticise unsuitable methods for bias (AO2/AO3)
  • Draw a random sample using random numbers generated on a calculator or from tables (AO1)
  • Calculate the mean, median, mode, range, interquartile range and standard deviation from raw data and frequency tables (AO1)
  • Apply the 1.5 x IQR rule to identify outliers and justify whether they should be discarded (AO2)
  • Construct and interpret box plots and histograms, calculating frequency density for unequal class widths (AO1/AO2)
  • Compare two distributions in context, commenting on location, dispersion and skewness (AO2)

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Population: The entire set of items or individuals relevant to the statistical enquiry; every member must be identifiable to take a census or use a sampling frame.
Sampling frame: A numbered list of all members of the population from which a sample is drawn, e.g. an electoral register or class register.
Simple random sample: A sample in which every member of the population has an equal chance of selection, chosen using random numbers so there is no bias.
Systematic sample: Selecting every kth member of an ordered sampling frame after a random start, where k = N/n; simple but risks periodicity in the list.
Stratified sample: The population is split into strata that differ in a relevant way, and each stratum is sampled in proportion to its size: n_h = (N_h / N) x n.
Quota sample: A non-random sample where interviewers fill quotas of respondents in each group; needs no sampling frame but is prone to interviewer bias.
Cluster sample: Randomly selecting whole groups (clusters, e.g. schools or streets) and sampling within them; cheap for geographically spread populations but clusters may be unrepresentative.
Standard deviation: The root mean square deviation from the mean, s = sqrt( sum of (x - mean)^2 / (n - 1) ), measuring spread in the same units as the data.
Frequency density: For a histogram, frequency divided by class width; the area of each bar is proportional to frequency.
Outlier: A value lying more than 1.5 x IQR beyond the quartiles, i.e. below Q1 - 1.5 x IQR or above Q3 + 1.5 x IQR; discard only with a justifiable reason such as a recording error.

📌 Key Points

Stratified sample size for a stratum: n_h = (N_h / N) x n, rounded to whole people.
Edexcel 9ST0 standard deviation uses divisor (n - 1): s = sqrt(sum of (x - mean)^2 / (n - 1)).
Histogram: frequency density = frequency / class width; never plot raw frequency with unequal widths.
Outlier fences: Q1 - 1.5 x IQR and Q3 + 1.5 x IQR.
Mean is pulled by outliers and skew; median and IQR are resistant.

Sampling methods: choosing and justifying

A census measures every member of the population; a sample measures only a subset. Exams expect you to weigh these up: a census gives complete information and no sampling variation, but is expensive, time-consuming, and destructive testing (e.g. crash-testing cars) rules it out entirely. A sample is cheaper and quicker, but smaller samples give less precise estimates.

Random methods need a sampling frame. Simple random sampling uses random numbers from a calculator or tables to pick n members, giving equal selection probability and no bias; its weakness is the requirement for a complete, up-to-date frame, which is impractical for large or scattered populations. Systematic sampling picks every kth item after a random start (k = N/n); it is quick and spreads through the frame, but if the list has a periodic pattern the sample can be badly biased. Stratified sampling divides the population into strata that differ in a way relevant to the enquiry (e.g. year group, region) and samples each in proportion to its size, guaranteeing every stratum is represented and often improving precision.

Quota sampling is non-random: interviewers fill predetermined quotas (e.g. 20 men over 60). It needs no sampling frame and is fast, but the choice of respondents within a quota is left to the interviewer, introducing bias. Cluster sampling randomly selects whole clusters and samples within them, cutting travel costs, but a cluster may not resemble the population. Opportunity (convenience) sampling and self-selected samples (volunteers) are almost always biased towards particular types of respondent and attract easy marks when asked to criticise a design.

Worked stratified calculation

A school of 800 students has 300 in Year 12 and 500 in Year 13. For a stratified sample of 40: Year 12 gives (300/800) x 40 = 15 students; Year 13 gives (500/800) x 40 = 25 students. Check: 15 + 25 = 40. State the method, the formula, the arithmetic and the total - that sequence earns full marks.

Method-choice questions follow a decision order. Ask: does a sampling frame exist? If not, quota or opportunity sampling is forced. If yes, do distinct groups matter to the enquiry? If so, stratify. Otherwise, is the frame large and ordered? Systematic sampling may then be the efficient choice. Whatever you choose, the highest marks come from a two-part justification: a correct property of the method (e.g. random, unbiased, proportionally representative) linked to the specific context of the question. Also be ready to calculate k = N/n for a systematic sample and to explain why a random start is needed - without it the sample could systematically miss part of the frame.

Data types and data quality

Categorical (qualitative) data are descriptions (blood group, brand); if the categories can be ordered they are ordinal (exam grade, satisfaction rating). Quantitative data are numerical: discrete data take exact, countable values (number of siblings, goals scored), while continuous data arise from measurement and can take any value in an interval (height, time). Choosing the right chart and the right average depends on this classification, so state the type explicitly when asked.

Primary data are collected first-hand by the enquirer: fully relevant, defined for the purpose, and you control the method - but collection is slow and costly. Secondary data were collected by someone else (government statistics, published surveys): instantly available and often large-scale, but possibly out of date, collected for a different purpose, and of unknown reliability. Questions frequently ask for one advantage and one disadvantage of each - learn those pairs.

Three quality words carry precise meanings in 9ST0. Reliability: the method would give consistent results if repeated. Validity: the data actually measure what the enquiry intends to measure. Bias: systematic distortion in one direction, from a poor sampling frame, leading questions, non-response or unrepresentative sampling. A pilot study is a small-scale trial run used to check that questions are understood, test the timing of the collection process, and refine the design before the main enquiry - quote at least two of these purposes.

When asked to criticise a data collection method, scan a checklist: sample size too small; sampling frame incomplete or absent; non-random selection; leading or ambiguous question wording; low response rate; data recorded inaccurately; secondary data of unknown reliability or age. One developed criticism per mark, each tied to the specific context.

Two further ideas appear in 9ST0 questions on data quality. Accuracy concerns how close a measurement is to the true value, while precision concerns the level of detail recorded (e.g. heights to the nearest centimetre); more precision is not automatically more accurate. Data cleaning - removing impossible values, correcting mis-keyed entries, deciding what to do about missing responses - should be done before any calculation, and exam questions expect you to state a rule (e.g. discard if clearly a recording error, otherwise keep) rather than silently delete. Link every quality discussion back to its effect on conclusions: biased or invalid data make estimates untrustworthy however large the sample.

Measures of location and dispersion

The three averages have distinct jobs. The mean, sum of x divided by n, uses every data value and is needed for standard deviation, but is distorted by outliers and skew. The median is the middle value when ordered - resistant to outliers, so preferred for skewed data. The mode is the most frequent value, the only sensible average for categorical data. For a frequency table the mean is sum of fx divided by sum of f; for grouped data use class midpoints and state that the answer is an estimate.

Dispersion measures: the range uses only two values and is distorted by outliers; the interquartile range (Q3 - Q1) is resistant; the standard deviation uses every value and pairs with the mean. Edexcel 9ST0 uses the sample formula s = sqrt( sum of (x - mean)^2 / (n - 1) ) - the (n - 1) divisor is a mark-scheme point; calculator SD functions may default to a different divisor, so quote 3 significant figures and state the formula used.

Coding appears in exam questions: if y = (x - a)/b then the mean of y is (mean of x - a)/b and the SD of y is SD of x divided by the absolute value of b. Division affects spread; subtraction does not. Expect a question asking you to recover the original mean or SD from coded summary statistics.

Skewness is measured from the averages: if mean > median the distribution has positive (right) skew; if mean < median, negative skew. The formula 3(mean - median)/sd quantifies this, and the quartile measure (Q3 + Q1 - 2Q2)/(Q3 - Q1) works from a box plot. In an exam, describe skew in context - e.g. incomes usually show positive skew, so report the median.

Choosing the measure for an exam answer is a two-step decision. First, is the data categorical? Then the mode is the only option. Second, is the distribution skewed or does it contain outliers? Then pair the median with the IQR. Only for roughly symmetric data without outliers should the mean with standard deviation be reported. A quick sanity check that earns marks: compare mean and median - if they differ noticeably, the distribution is skewed and the median should be quoted. A typical exam item gives coded or summary statistics and asks which average is appropriate and why; the answer is graded on the reasoning, not the choice alone.

Outliers, box plots, histograms and comparison

An outlier is any value below Q1 - 1.5 x IQR or above Q3 + 1.5 x IQR. Show the fence calculations explicitly, then decide: discard an outlier only with a justifiable reason (mis-keyed data, impossible value), otherwise keep it and say why (a genuine extreme value still part of the population). A discarded outlier changes the mean and SD but not the median or IQR.

A box plot displays the five key values - minimum, Q1, median, Q3, maximum - with outliers marked as crosses beyond the whiskers. It makes location (position of the median), dispersion (width of the box = IQR) and skew (asymmetry of the box and whiskers) visible at a glance. Comparison questions are guaranteed marks if you write in context: state which group has the higher average (median), which is more consistent (smaller IQR or range), and comment on skew - never just quote numbers without interpretation.

Histograms represent continuous, grouped data. The vertical axis is frequency density = frequency / class width, and the area of each bar is proportional to frequency. With unequal class widths, plotting raw frequency is a classic error; with equal widths the densities are proportional to frequencies anyway. You can estimate how many observations lie in any interval by finding the corresponding area, and you should describe the shape: unimodal, symmetric, or positively/negatively skewed.

A high-mark comparison paragraph has a fixed skeleton: (i) compare medians in context - on average...; (ii) compare spread in context - the IQR/SD for... is smaller, so more consistent; (iii) comment on outliers or skew and what they imply for the choice of average. Practise writing all three sentences with the context nouns in every one.

Box plots can also be constructed from a cumulative frequency curve: read the quartiles at cumulative frequencies of n/4 and 3n/4 and the median at n/2, then draw the box plot from those five values. Edexcel marks quartiles from cumulative frequency graphs leniently for reading accuracy but strictly for method - plot points at class upper boundaries, join with a smooth curve, and draw horizontal and vertical construction lines. Finally, remember the two marks that most candidates drop in comparison questions: interpreting every number in context (the mark scheme usually rejects uninterpreted values), and stating which distribution is more consistent rather than only which has the greater spread.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: A town council records the daily journey times, in minutes, of 11 bus drivers on one route. The times are: 2, 4, 4, 5, 6, 6, 7, 8, 9, 9, 34. (a) Find the mean, standard deviation, median and interquartile range of the times. (4 marks) (b) Identify any outliers using the 1.5 x IQR rule and state, with a reason, whether the value 34 should be discarded. (3 marks)
Mark-scheme logic: (a) Sum = 94, so mean = 94/11 = 8.55 minutes (1). Sum of x squared = 1564, so s = sqrt((1564 - 94^2/11)/10) = sqrt(76.1) = 8.72 minutes (1). Median is the 6th value = 6 minutes (1). Q1 = 3rd value = 4, Q3 = 9th value = 9, so IQR = 5 minutes (1). (b) Upper fence = Q3 + 1.5 x IQR = 9 + 7.5 = 16.5; lower fence = 4 - 7.5 = -3.5 (1). Since 34 > 16.5 it is an outlier (1). It should be discarded only if there is a reason to believe it was recorded in error or is impossible for the route; if it is a genuine (e.g. breakdown-related) journey time it must be kept (1). Accept any justified decision; marks are for fences, comparison and justification.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

A school of 800 students has 300 in Year 12 and 500 in Year 13. A researcher takes a stratified sample of 40 students, stratified by year group. (a) State one advantage of stratified sampling over simple random sampling here. (b) Calculate how many Year 12 and Year 13 students should be sampled. (4 marks)
Answer: (a) It guarantees each year group is represented in proportion to its size, so both groups are definitely included (any valid advantage). (b) Year 12: (300/800) x 40 = 15 students; Year 13: (500/800) x 40 = 25 students. Working: n_h = (N_h/N) x n for each stratum; check total 15 + 25 = 40. (1 mark advantage, 1 mark each calculation, 1 mark total/check.)
A dataset of 11 measurements has mean 8.55, s = 8.72, Q1 = 4, median = 6 and Q3 = 9. A proposed additional value of 2.8 is added to the dataset. Without recalculating, state the effect on (i) the mean, (ii) the standard deviation, (iii) the median, (iv) the IQR. (4 marks)
Answer: 2.8 lies below the mean (8.55), so (i) the mean decreases (1). (ii) The standard deviation changes slightly - since 2.8 is close to the existing bulk of the data and within the fences (lower fence -3.5), the change is small; it is not an outlier so spread stays similar (1). (iii) With 12 values the median becomes the mean of the 6th and 7th ordered values, so it may change slightly; 2.8 is the smallest value so it cannot lower the position of the middle - the median stays 6 or changes only marginally (1). (iv) Q1 and Q3 may shift because quartile positions depend on n, but since 2.8 is not an outlier the IQR changes little; the key mark is recognising resistant vs non-resistant measures (1).
The times, t minutes, taken by 95 customers at a bank are grouped: 0 < t <= 10 (frequency 20), 10 < t <= 15 (frequency 25), 15 < t <= 20 (frequency 30), 20 < t <= 40 (frequency 20). (a) Calculate the frequency density of each class. (b) Estimate how many customers took more than 18 minutes. (4 marks)
Answer: (a) Frequency density = frequency / class width: 20/10 = 2; 25/5 = 5; 30/5 = 6; 20/20 = 1. (1 mark for method, 1 for all four values.) (b) More than 18 minutes spans the last 2 minutes of the 15-20 class plus the whole 20-40 class: area = 30 x (2/5) + 20 = 12 + 20 = 32 customers (estimated). (1 mark method - proportional area within the class, 1 mark answer 32.)
Box plots of the daily screen time (hours) of 50 boys and 50 girls show: boys - median 3.2, IQR 1.1; girls - median 3.9, IQR 2.4. Compare the screen times of boys and girls. (3 marks)
Answer: Location: the median for girls (3.9 hours) is higher than for boys (3.2), so on average girls spend more time on screens per day (1). Dispersion: the IQR for girls (2.4) is larger than for boys (1.1), so girls screen times are more varied and boys more consistent (1). Any valid comment on skew/outliers if shown, stated in context (1). Marks require the context words (screen time, boys, girls) with each comparison - bare numbers score zero.
A researcher stands outside one supermarket on a Tuesday morning and asks 50 shoppers to fill in a quota sample about the shopping habits of the whole town. Criticise the sampling method and suggest an improvement. (3 marks)
Answer: The sample is quota/opportunity based, so the respondents are chosen non-randomly by the interviewer and may not represent the town (1). The sample is biased by time and place: Tuesday-morning shoppers are disproportionately retired people and non-workers, and one supermarket misses other areas (1). Improvement: take a stratified random sample of the residents using a sampling frame (e.g. the electoral register), stratified by age group or district, selecting members with random numbers (1).
