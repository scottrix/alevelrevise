Historical Enquiry and Essay Skills

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level All Boards (AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC, CCEA) AQA

A-Level History revision: Historical Enquiry and Essay Skills. Learning objectives, key points, worked examples and practice questions across AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC and CCEA.

📌 Key Points

Key Fact: Good question: specific, debatable, evidence-based, manageable scope (not 'Why WWI?' but 'How far did German naval policy cause WWI?')
Key Fact: Essay structure: intro (thesis + line of argument), themed paragraphs (PEEL), conclusion (judgement + significance)
Key Fact: Evidence: select best, not all; deploy precisely (dates, stats, quotes); weigh (corroboration, provenance)
Key Fact: Judgement: 'ultimately', 'more significantly', 'on balance'; avoid fence-sitting; relative significance
Key Fact: Citation: footnotes (Chicago) or in-text; bibliography; primary/secondary distinction
Key Fact: NEA (coursework): 3,000-4,000 words, primary + secondary sources, independently formulated question, about 20% of A-Level

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Formulate focused, answerable historical questions
  • Plan and structure analytical essays with sustained argument
  • Select, deploy, and weigh evidence effectively
  • Write balanced conclusions with substantiated judgements
  • Reference and cite sources appropriately

💡 Worked Example

Exam-Style Question

Question: Write an essay introduction for: 'How far was economic crisis the main cause of the French Revolution?'

Model Answer:

The French Revolution (1789) resulted from a convergence of fiscal collapse, social tension, and Enlightenment ideas. While economic crisis -- state bankruptcy, grain shortages, regressive taxation -- provided the immediate catalyst, this essay argues that structural weaknesses of the Ancien Régime (privilege, absolutism, lack of representation) made revolution probable, with Enlightenment providing the intellectual framework. Economic crisis was the spark, not the fuel

Q3: Evaluate this enquiry question: 'Why did the First World War break out in 1914?' How could it be improved?

Show Model Answer

Answer: The question is too broad and largely descriptive: 'why' invites a list of factors rather than a debatable line of argument, and its scope spans the whole July Crisis and beyond. Improved: 'How far was German naval expansion responsible for the outbreak of war in 1914?' -- this is specific (Weltpolitik and the Anglo-German arms race), debatable (the Fischer thesis against the 'shared responsibility' orthodoxy), evidence-based (the Dreadnought race, the Schlieffen Plan, the July Crisis) and manageable within a 3,000-4,000 word NEA. A good enquiry question must therefore be narrow enough to argue, not merely narrate.

Q4: Write a concluding paragraph that reaches a nuanced judgement on the relative importance of long-term and short-term causes of the 1917 Russian Revolution.

Show Model Answer

Answer: In conclusion, the February Revolution of 1917 was ultimately caused by the interaction of long-term structural weakness and the short-term shock of war. The autocracy's refusal to share power, the unresolved land question and the grievances left unaddressed after 1905 created deep instability; yet it was the defeats and shortages of the First World War -- mutinies at the front and bread queues in Petrograd -- that triggered collapse in February 1917. On balance, therefore, the war was the more significant immediate cause, but only because the long-term structural failings had left the Tsarist system incapable of absorbing such a shock; neither factor was sufficient alone.

Q5: Explain how a historian should weigh two pieces of evidence that reach contradictory conclusions about the same event.

Show Model Answer

Answer: First, assess the provenance of each source -- author, date, purpose and context: a government memo written during a crisis carries different weight from a memoir written decades later for self-justification. Second, corroborate: test each claim against an independent third source and against the established factual chronology. Third, evaluate typicality and bias -- whether the evidence represents wider opinion or a fringe view. Finally, reach an explicit judgement that weighs the evidence ('more significant because...') and selects the better-supported conclusion while qualifying, rather than dismissing, the alternative; a nuanced answer weighs rather than merely lists.
