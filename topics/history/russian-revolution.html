Russia: Tsardom to Communism, 1917-1991

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC/Eduqas, CCEA

Exam revision for Russia 1917-1991: the fall of Tsardom, the 1917 revolutions, Lenin and Stalin's regimes, destalinisation, Gorbachev's reforms and the USSR's collapse, with the interpretations needed for 30-mark essays.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain the collapse of Tsardom: autocracy's rigidity, the 1905 Revolution and the role of the First World War
  • Analyse the causes and character of the February and October 1917 revolutions, including dual power and the Bolshevik seizure
  • Evaluate Lenin's and Stalin's state-building: War Communism, NEP, the Cheka, the Five-Year Plans, collectivisation and the Terror
  • Assess how far destalinisation under Khrushchev and the stagnation under Brezhnev changed the Stalinist system
  • Weigh the causes of the USSR's collapse in 1991: Gorbachev's reforms, economic failure and nationalism
  • Deploy the interpretations of Figes, Service, Pipes, Conquest, Getty, Brown and Kotkin as frames for 30-mark argument

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Autocracy: The tsarist system in which unlimited power was vested in one hereditary monarch, justified by Orthodoxy and enforced by bureaucracy and the Okhrana; Nicholas II pledged in 1894 to preserve it 'unbroken'.
Duma: The national legislature conceded by the October Manifesto (1905); its powers were curtailed by the Fundamental Laws (1906) and the 3 June 1907 electoral coup, leaving it subordinate to the tsar.
Dual power: The February-October 1917 stand-off between the unelected Provisional Government (formal authority) and the Petrograd Soviet (real power over the garrison and streets under Order No.1).
War Communism: The 1918-21 emergency regime: nationalisation of industry, ban on private trade, grain requisitioning (prodrazvyorstka) and militarised labour — ideological and siege-driven at once.
NEP: The New Economic Policy (March 1921): tax in kind replaced requisitioning, private trade and small business returned, while the party retained the 'commanding heights' — a strategic retreat from full socialism.
Collectivisation: The forced consolidation of peasant holdings into kolkhozes from 1929, accompanied by dekulakisation and requisition quotas; provoked the 1932-33 famine (c.5-7 million dead, including the Holodomor).
Glasnost: Gorbachev's policy of openness from 1986: relaxed censorship, admission of the Chernobyl disaster's scale and reappraisal of Soviet history — which destroyed the regime's ideological legitimacy.
Perestroika: Gorbachev's economic 'restructuring' from 1987 (Law on the State Enterprise, 1988 Cooperatives Law): partial market mechanisms within the command system, which worsened shortages without creating a market.

Tsardom, Modernisation and the 1905 Revolution

Nicholas II inherited an autocracy in 1894 and swore to preserve it 'unbroken': the tsar answered to no constitution, parliament or electorate, ruling through an appointed bureaucracy, the Orthodox Church and the Okhrana (secret police). Witte's 'Great Spurt' industrialised fast — the Trans-Siberian Railway, the 1897 gold standard, French loans — creating a strike-prone urban proletariat in Petrograd and Moscow alongside a land-hungry peasantry still bound to communes and redemption payments. Opposition spanned liberal zemstvo reformers (the Kadets from October 1905), the peasant-oriented Socialist Revolutionaries, and the Social Democrats, split in 1903 into Lenin's Bolsheviks and the Mensheviks.

The 1905 Revolution opened with Bloody Sunday (22 January 1905), when troops shot down Father Gapon's peaceful march to the Winter Palace, destroying the tsar's 'Little Father' image. Defeat in the Russo-Japanese War (Mukden, Tsushima, May 1905) exposed official incompetence; strikes spread, the battleship Potemkin mutinied and the St Petersburg Soviet (Trotsky) coordinated workers. Nicholas conceded the October Manifesto (17 October 1905) — civil liberties and a legislative Duma — splitting liberals from radicals, then reasserted 'supreme autocratic power' in the Fundamental Laws (April 1906). He dissolved the first two Dumas (1906, 1907) and, under Stolypin, rewrote the electoral law in the coup of 3 June 1907 to secure a tame third Duma. Stolypin's 'wager on the strong' (khutor farm consolidation) created a prosperous minority but solved neither land hunger nor poverty; his assassination (1911) and the Lena goldfields massacre (1912) exposed the regime's shallowness.

Interpretation matters here: Figes treats 1905 as a 'dress rehearsal' — the soviets first appeared and popular anger over land, autocracy and war was never resolved; Pipes sees the Manifesto as a tactical concession from an unreconciled autocracy, which is why constitutionalism never took root. By 1914 the Duma, the court (Rasputin) and the tercentenary pageantry (1913) masked a regime trusted by almost no organised group — and revolutionaries had learned the soviet weapon they would revive in 1917.

1917: February, Dual Power and the October Revolution

The First World War converted strain into collapse. Command failures and the Great Retreat (1915) cost millions of casualties; inflation and munitions shortages bit into urban life; in August 1915 Nicholas took personal command, attaching the monarchy to every defeat, while Alexandra and Rasputin presided over ministerial chaos. The Duma's Progressive Bloc demanded a government of public confidence and was refused — autocracy's rigidity eliminated the middle way.

February 1917 began spontaneously: on 23 February (8 March, new style) International Women's Day bread demonstrations merged with striking workers; on 26-27 February the Volynsky regiment and then the Petrograd garrison mutinied, and repression became impossible. The Petrograd Soviet formed immediately and issued Order No.1 (the garrison to obey the Soviet, not officers), while Duma leaders created a Provisional Committee; Nicholas abdicated on 2 March and Grand Duke Michael refused the throne. Dual power resulted: the unelected Provisional Government (Prince Lvov, later Kerensky) held formal authority, the Petrograd Soviet held real power over streets and army.

The Provisional Government destroyed itself by postponing every hard question: it fought on (the Milyukov note, April), deferred land reform to a future Constituent Assembly and delayed elections. Lenin returned on 3 April 1917 with the April Theses — no cooperation with the Provisional Government, 'All power to the soviets', 'Peace, land and bread' — giving radicalism a programme. The failed June offensive and the July Days were followed by the Kornilov affair (August), in which Kerensky's panic at his own commander-in-chief let the Bolsheviks arm the city and destroyed the right's credibility. By September the Bolsheviks won majorities in the Petrograd and Moscow soviets, and Trotsky's Military Revolutionary Committee prepared action: on 10 October Lenin won the Central Committee to insurrection ('history will not forgive us if we delay'); on 24-25 October the MRC seized key points and the Winter Palace, and the Second Congress of Soviets ratified the seizure with the Decrees on Peace and Land. The Constituent Assembly, elected in November, was dispersed after one day in January 1918 — the Bolsheviks would not share power.

Historiographical fault line: Pipes insists October was a coup d'etat by a conspiratorial minority exploiting state paralysis, not a popular revolution; Figes counters that radicalism from below — land seizures, soldier pacifism, soviet democracy — made Bolshevism's victory possible and shaped the regime it created; Service stresses Lenin's personal decisiveness and party discipline as the decisive variable. A* answers weigh all three against the evidence of dual power and the garrison's turn.

Lenin and Stalin: Consolidation, Industrialisation, Terror

Lenin consolidated before he built. The Cheka, founded in December 1917 under Dzerzhinsky, executed 'class enemies' without trial from the outset; the Red Terror (September 1918), announced after the attempt on Lenin's life, made hostage-taking and executions official policy. War Communism (1918-21) nationalised industry, banned private trade, militarised labour and requisitioned grain by prodrazvyorstka — ideology and siege logistics at once. Its collapse was sealed when Kronstadt's sailors, 'reddest of the red', mutinied in March 1921 for free soviets and were crushed by Trotsky. The NEP (March 1921) retreated: a tax in kind replaced requisitioning, private trade and small business returned, and by c.1926-27 output neared 1913 levels, while the party kept the 'commanding heights' and banned internal factions (Tenth Congress, 1921). The state was already a one-party dictatorship: opposition parties banned, the 1922-23 constitution confirmed, Stalin installed as General Secretary (1922).

Stalin won the succession (patronage through the General Secretaryship; 'Socialism in One Country' against Trotsky's 'Permanent Revolution') and then overturned NEP. The Great Turn (1928-29) launched the First Five-Year Plan (1928-32): crash industrialisation — Magnitogorsk, the Dnieper dam, Stakhanovites (1935), Gosplan targets and savage discipline — followed by the Second (1933-37) and Third (1938-41) Plans. Collectivisation (from 1929) forced peasants into kolkhozes, 'dekulakised' the better-off (deportation or death) and, with resistance and ruinous quotas, produced the famine of 1932-33: some 5-7 million dead, including the Ukrainian Holodomor. Industry and urbanisation soared; consumption and agriculture paid.

Terror peaked in the Yezhovshchina (1936-38). The Kirov murder (December 1934) supplied the pretext; the Moscow show trials (1936: Zinoviev and Kamenev; 1937: Pyatakov and Radek; 1938: Bukharin and Rykov) extracted confessions from Old Bolsheviks; the military purge (June 1937, Tukhachevsky) beheaded the Red Army on the eve of war. Archives record c.680,000 executions in 1937-38 and a Gulag swollen to millions. Conquest reads the Terror as deliberate totalitarian consolidation by a paranoid dictator; the revisionist Getty stresses chaotic escalation — centre-local dynamics, denunciation and momentum out of control; Kotkin frames it as violent state-building.

In war the costs were redeemed. The Nazi-Soviet Pact (August 1939) bought time and territory; Operation Barbarossa (June 1941) nearly destroyed the USSR, but scorched-earth defence, evacuation of industry beyond the Urals, Order No. 227 ('Not one step back', July 1942) and the battles of Moscow, Stalingrad (1942-43) and Kursk (1943) delivered victory by May 1945 at the cost of c.27 million Soviet dead. Victory became the regime's enduring legitimacy — and fixed the Cold War confrontation that drained Soviet economics for four decades.

From Destalinisation to Collapse, 1953-1991

Khrushchev won the succession (1953-55) and broke Stalin's spell in the Secret Speech (February 1956), denouncing the 'cult of personality' and launching destalinisation: prisoners returned, the Gulag shrank, the cultural thaw began — but its limits showed when Hungary (1956) was crushed. His record mixed the Virgin Lands, housing and space triumphs (Sputnik 1957, Gagarin 1961) with the Berlin Wall (1961) and Cuban Missile Crisis humiliation (1962); the Presidium retired him in 1964.

Brezhnev (1964-82) offered 'stability of cadres' — in practice gerontocracy, a softened Stalin image and repression abroad by doctrine (Prague Spring 1968 and the 'Brezhnev Doctrine'; Afghanistan 1979). Detente (SALT I 1972, Helsinki 1975) bought parity with the USA while the command economy stagnated (zastoi): growth fell from 1950s highs toward zero, oil revenue masked decline, and consumer shortage and corruption spread. This stagnation is the standard 'structural decline' evidence in collapse arguments.

Gorbachev (1985-91) began with uskoreniye (acceleration) — an investment and anti-alcohol drive that failed — then chose systemic reform: glasnost lifted censorship (the press admitted the Chernobyl disaster's scale in 1986 and reopened Soviet history), while perestroika restructured the economy through the 1987 Law on the State Enterprise and the 1988 Cooperatives Law without a real market, worsening shortages. Politically he ended the party's monopoly: the 1988 constitutional reforms and semi-contested 1989 Congress of People's Deputies elections reintroduced competition; the 'Sinatra Doctrine' abandoned Eastern Europe (1989); INF (1987) and 'new thinking' ended the Cold War. Nationalism then dissolved the Union: the Baltic states asserted sovereignty, Nagorno-Karabakh (1988) inflamed Armenia and Azerbaijan, and Yeltsin's Russia claimed its own laws. The failed August coup (1991) by the GKChP discredited the CPSU — Yeltsin's tank-top defiance and the ban on the party in Russia shifted power to the republics — and the Belovezha Accords dissolved the USSR on 25-26 December 1991.

Historians: Service argues Gorbachev's reforms were morally necessary yet strategically destabilising — freedom destroyed cohesion faster than markets could replace it; Archie Brown stresses ideas and individual choice (the 'Gorbachev factor'), insisting collapse was not inevitable; Pipes and the structural school point to the command economy's terminal stagnation and imperial overstretch; Kotkin emphasises elite defection — an exhausted apparat abandoning its own ideology. Judgement to aim for: structural decay made collapse possible; Gorbachev's choices made it actual in 1991 rather than a later, bloodier unraveling.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: To what extent were Gorbachev's reforms the main cause of the collapse of the USSR in 1991? Explain your answer. (30 marks)
Mark-scheme logic: Level 5 (25-30): a sustained, analytically structured argument with a substantiated judgement, a wide range of accurate AO1 detail and factors weighed against each other throughout. Required AO1 knowledge: uskoreniye and its failure; glasnost (press freedom, Chernobyl 1986, historical reappraisal) destroying ideological legitimacy; perestroika (1987 State Enterprise Law, 1988 Cooperatives Law) worsening shortages; political liberalisation (1988 constitutional reforms, semi-contested 1989 elections) ending the party monopoly in practice; the 'Sinatra Doctrine' and loss of Eastern Europe; nationalist surge (Baltic sovereignty, Nagorno-Karabakh 1988, Yeltsin's Russia); the failed August 1991 GKChP coup; the Belovezha Accords and dissolution (25-26 December 1991); background structural decline under Brezhnev (stagnation, oil dependence, Afghanistan). Argument FOR Gorbachev as main cause: no systemic crisis was irreversible in 1985; glasnost converted private doubt into open opposition; political liberalisation dismantled the coercive and ideological instruments that had preserved the Union in 1956 and 1968; the coup his reforms provoked finished the party. Argument AGAINST: terminal economic stagnation and imperial overstretch predated him; nationalism was organic and long-suppressed; the coup itself came from reform's opponents, showing the system was already divided. Historians to deploy: Service (reforms necessary yet destabilising), Archie Brown (the 'Gorbachev factor' — without his choices collapse was not inevitable), Pipes and the structural school (economic failure decisive), Kotkin (elite defection). Judgement: Gorbachev's reforms were the proximate cause — structural decay created the possibility, but his sequencing (openness before markets, politicisation before economics) made collapse happen in 1991. Answers that list events without weighing causes, or that omit a clear judgement, cap at Levels 3-4.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Assess the reasons for the February Revolution of 1917. (30 marks)
Answer: The decisive cause was the First World War's strain on a brittle system: military failure and the Great Retreat discredited Nicholas personally once he took command (August 1915); inflation, food and fuel shortages radicalised Petrograd workers; the garrison mutiny of 26-27 February made repression impossible and forced abdication (2 March). Underlying causes explain the system's brittleness — autocracy refused the Progressive Bloc's compromise, and land hunger and urban misery persisted despite 1905's concessions, which Figes reads as a 'dress rehearsal' never resolved. Immediate triggers (the women's day demonstrations) only ignited a crisis already formed. Judgement: the war was the sufficient condition; autocratic rigidity the permissive one — without the war, Nicholas likely survived as in 1905-14. Strong answers also weigh Pipes's stress on state paralysis from above against Figes's social radicalism from below.
To what extent was the October Revolution a coup d'etat rather than a popular revolution? (30 marks)
Answer: Evidence for a coup: insurrection was decided by a narrow Central Committee vote (10 October), planned and executed by Trotsky's MRC, and involved seizing a few key points with minimal resistance — hence Pipes's characterisation of a conspiratorial seizure by a minority. Evidence for popular revolution: the Bolsheviks rode genuine mass moods (peace, land, workers' control), won majorities in the Petrograd and Moscow soviets by September, and were tolerated by the garrison and streets because Order No.1 and dual power had gutted official authority — Figes's position. Service bridges them: Lenin's leadership and party discipline converted mood into power. Judgement: tactically a coup, sociologically a revolution — the seizure was conspiratorial, but it succeeded only because mass radicalism had paralysed the state. Pure 'coup' answers underplay the Provisional Government's self-destruction; pure 'popular' answers underplay the party's organisation.
To what extent did Lenin's economic policies compromise Bolshevik ideology? (30 marks)
Answer: War Communism (1918-21) matched ideology: nationalisation, grain requisitioning and the abolition of trade approximated the abolition of markets — though siege conditions were equally decisive, and its economic failure (industrial collapse, famine, the Kronstadt mutiny of March 1921) showed ideology outrunning feasibility. NEP (1921) openly compromised: private trade, a tax in kind and small capitalism returned under state control of the 'commanding heights' — Lenin's 'one step backward to take two steps forward', defended as strategic retreat; Bukharin's 'enrich yourselves' stretched it further. Judgement: NEP was a conscious ideological concession justified pragmatically — power was preserved while socialism was postponed, not abandoned; the simultaneous 1921 ban on factions shows the party's real priority was monopoly power, not doctrine. Weigh Conquest and Pipes (ideological retreat) against Fitzpatrick (pragmatic state-building).
Collectivisation was economically disastrous but politically successful. How far do you agree? (30 marks)
Answer: Economic disaster is a strong claim but defensible: the famine of 1932-33 killed some 5-7 million (including the Ukrainian Holodomor), livestock herds halved as peasants slaughtered animals rather than surrender them, and rural output recovered only slowly; extraction of grain and labour was achieved largely by coercion's sheer cost. Political success is clearer: the state captured the countryside, ending the rural independence NEP had restored; dekulakisation destroyed a class and terrorised the rest; kolkhozes and Machine Tractor Stations embedded control and supplied the workforce and grain for the Five-Year Plans' industrial surge. Judgement: 'economically disastrous' needs qualification — industrial growth and urban provisioning did proceed, which was the plan's core purpose; 'politically successful' is closer, but was bought by famine and terror that alienated the peasantry permanently. Cite Conquest (deliberate famine), Getty (chaotic escalation) and Kotkin (state-building through violence).
To what extent did Khrushchev's and Brezhnev's rule solve the problems created by Stalinism? (30 marks)
Answer: Khrushchev attacked the terror and the myth: the Secret Speech (February 1956), mass releases from the Gulag and the cultural thaw ended mass repression as a system — but not the party's monopoly (Hungary 1956 was crushed) or the command economy. Brezhnev resolved the succession problem through 'stability of cadres' and secured parity with the USA (SALT I 1972), yet institutionalised stagnation (zastoi): falling growth, consumer shortage, corruption and gerontocracy. Judgement: distinguish three Stalinist legacies — terror, largely solved; economic dysfunction, not solved; one-party rule, untouched — so 'solved' overstates, and both regimes left Gorbachev a repressible but unreformable system. Strong answers cite Archie Brown or Service on the limits of reform from above, and note that de-Stalinisation itself destabilised the bloc (Hungary 1956, Prague Spring 1968).
