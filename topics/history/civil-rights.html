USA: Civil Rights and the Making of Modern America

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC/Eduqas, CCEA

Exam revision for USA civil rights: Reconstruction and Jim Crow, the NAACP's legal strategy, Brown to Montgomery and Selma, the 1964/1965 Acts, Black Power and the key historiography for 30-mark essays.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain how Reconstruction's constitutional promises were reversed by Jim Crow laws, disenfranchisement and segregation
  • Evaluate the NAACP's legal strategy and the real impact of Brown v Board of Education (1954)
  • Assess the relative importance of grassroots protest, national leaders (King, Parks, SNCC) and the federal government from Montgomery (1955-56) to Selma (1965)
  • Compare nonviolent direct action and Black Power: aims, methods and results after 1965
  • Weigh the King-centred orthodoxy against 'long civil rights movement' revisionism in essay arguments
  • Reach substantiated judgements on the significance of the Civil Rights Acts (1964/1965) and King's assassination (1968)

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Reconstruction: The 1865-77 federal programme after the Civil War: Thirteenth, Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments, the Freedmen's Bureau and federal troops in the South — reversed by the Compromise of 1877 and white 'Redemption'.
Jim Crow laws: Southern state statutes from the 1890s enforcing racial segregation in transport, schools and public facilities, backed by disenfranchisement (poll taxes, literacy tests, grandfather clauses).
'Separate but equal': The doctrine from Plessy v Ferguson (1896) that segregation was constitutional if facilities were nominally equal; Harlan's dissent predicted its injustice. Overturned in education by Brown (1954).
De jure / de facto segregation: Segregation by law (the Jim Crow South) versus segregation in practice — northern housing, schools and employment, sustained by housing markets and white violence rather than statute.
NAACP: The National Association for the Advancement of Colored People (founded 1909 from Du Bois's Niagara Movement); its Legal Defense Fund under Charles Houston and Thurgood Marshall litigated the road to Brown.
Nonviolent direct action: Disciplined protest (boycotts, sit-ins, marches) designed to expose injustice, provoke brutal official reaction and win national media sympathy — the SCLC/SNCC method from Montgomery to Selma.
Black Power: The post-1965 slogan (Stokely Carmichael, Mississippi march, June 1966) for self-determination, racial pride and armed self-defence, rejecting integration rhetoric; embodied by the Black Panther Party (1966).
COINTELPRO: The FBI's counter-intelligence programme (1956-71) under Hoover: surveillance, infiltration and disruption of civil rights and Black Power groups, including King's SCLC and the Panthers.

Reconstruction to Jim Crow: The Baseline

Reconstruction (1865-77) promised legal equality: the Thirteenth Amendment (1865) abolished slavery, the Fourteenth (1868) conferred citizenship and equal protection, the Fifteenth (1870) barred racial voting restrictions; the Freedmen's Bureau and federal troops briefly sustained black political power, including black congressmen. The Compromise of 1877 withdrew federal troops, and white 'Redemption' reversed everything: sharecropping and debt peonage replaced slavery's economics; poll taxes, literacy tests, grandfather clauses and white primaries disenfranchised the South by 1900 — black registration in much of the Deep South collapsed to near zero by 1904; Plessy v Ferguson (1896) constitutionalised 'separate but equal' (Harlan's lone dissent); Jim Crow segregation hardened in every public sphere; lynching peaked in the 1890s (over 100 a year, contested by Ida B. Wells's anti-lynching campaign and journalism), and the second KKK revived (1915). The Great Migration (from c.1916) moved millions north — into ghettos, the Red Summer race riots (Chicago 1919), the Tulsa massacre (1921) and de facto segregation.

By the 1940s the context was shifting: the New Deal and the Second World War (the 'Double V' campaign against fascism abroad and racism at home) raised black expectations, Truman desegregated the armed forces (Executive Order 9981, 1948) and the black vote mattered in northern cities, while the NAACP's Crisis magazine and branches built a national base. The baseline for 1954 is therefore legal subordination in the South, economic marginality everywhere — and a small but effective organised black elite with access to the courts.

This baseline explains the movement's two strategic tracks: litigation through the NAACP and mass protest — neither alone sufficient. The protest track had precursors worth citing in essays: A. Philip Randolph's March on Washington Movement (1941) forced Roosevelt's Fair Employment Practices Committee; CORE (founded 1942) pioneered the nonviolent direct-action techniques — sit-ins, Freedom Rides — that the 1960s mainstreamed; wartime militancy (the Pittsburgh Courier's 'Double V') linked race politics at home to the Cold War image abroad that later shaped executive action. Knowing the baseline also sharpens essay judgements: 'progress' claims must be measured against how total the system of Jim Crow was, not against modern expectations, and 'failure' arguments about the movement's economic limits trace back to this structural position.

The NAACP and the Legal Road to Brown

The NAACP (founded 1909 from Du Bois's Niagara Movement) pursued litigation for four decades before Brown. Charles Hamilton Houston (Howard University Law School) and Thurgood Marshall built the strategy: attack graduate and professional education first, where 'separate but equal' was most expensive to sustain — Murray v Maryland (1936), Missouri ex rel. Gaines v Canada (1938), Sweatt v Painter and McLaurin v Oklahoma (1950) — then strike the whole doctrine. Marshall's record before the Supreme Court (winning all but three of his arguments) gave Brown its credibility; the campaign was funded by membership dues and pioneered impact litigation — picking cases, facts and plaintiffs (like the Topeka families) to maximise national effect. Brown v Board of Education (17 May 1954, Warren Court, unanimous) held separate schools 'inherently unequal', using social-science evidence (the Clark doll studies) to overturn Plessy in education; Brown II (1955) ordered integration 'with all deliberate speed' — a licence for delay.

Implementation, not law, was the battleground: Massive Resistance followed (the Southern Manifesto, 1956; Virginia's Prince Edward County school closures), and Eisenhower's hesitancy was forced at Little Rock (September 1957), when Faubus used the National Guard to block the Little Rock Nine and Eisenhower — his authority openly defied — sent the 101st Airborne to escort the students into Central High, the first federal troop use in the South since Reconstruction; Cooper v Aaron (1958) then asserted that states were bound by Brown. Eisenhower acted to defend federal authority against open defiance, not from integrationist conviction — he privately regretted appointing Warren — so the White House was dragged, not driving. The weak Civil Rights Act (1957) — a voting-rights bill gutted by Senate filibuster, though it created the Civil Rights Division and Commission — showed legislation's limits without protest. Montgomery's buses were actually desegregated by Browder v Gayle (November 1956), not the boycott alone. Judgement for essays: the legal campaign changed the law's legitimacy and supplied the victories other campaigns consumed; changing behaviour required protest and federal will — litigation was the foundation, not the whole building.

Protest Peak: Montgomery to Selma, 1955-1965

Montgomery Bus Boycott (1 December 1955 - 20 December 1956): Rosa Parks's arrest triggered a 381-day boycott organised by the Montgomery Improvement Association and a young Martin Luther King — car pools, mass meetings, King's house bombed. Nonviolence plus church organisation proved a national media weapon; the boycott ended with Browder v Gayle. The SCLC (1957) was founded to replicate it; the student wing emerged with the Greensboro sit-ins (1 February 1960), which spread to hundreds of towns and produced SNCC (April 1960). CORE's Freedom Rides (1961) broke interstate segregation despite the Anniston firebombing; the Kennedy administration sent marshals but preferred to 'cool off' direct action. Albany (1961-62) taught organisational lessons — police chief Pritchett's mass arrests without violence blunted the spectacle, showing King's method needed a brutal, camera-ready opponent; the Mississippi Freedom Summer (1964) and its murders (Schwerner, Chaney, Goodman) showed the same for voter registration.

Birmingham (Project C, April-May 1963) was chosen precisely for Police Commissioner Connor's expected brutality: dogs and fire hoses turned on children's marches, televised nationally; King's 'Letter from Birmingham Jail' framed the moral case. The campaign forced the Kennedy administration's civil rights bill and capped with the March on Washington (28 August 1963; c.250,000; 'I Have a Dream'). Johnson signed the Civil Rights Act (2 July 1964) — public accommodations desegregated, employment discrimination barred (Title VII), the poll tax in federal elections abolished by the 24th Amendment that January — then Selma delivered the voting-rights campaign: SNCC's groundwork in Dallas County, Bloody Sunday (7 March 1965, state troopers with clubs and gas on the Edmund Pettus Bridge), Johnson's 'we shall overcome' address and the Voting Rights Act (6 August 1965) — federal registrars, literacy tests abolished, southern black registration soaring within two years. King won the Nobel Peace Prize (1964) — its youngest laurel then, international recognition of the nonviolent method; SNCC-SCLC tension over Selma's leadership prefigured the coming split.

Counterpoint throughout: Malcolm X (Nation of Islam; 'the ballot or the bullet'; self-defence rather than integration) and his 1964-65 evolution toward political coalition, cut short by his assassination (February 1965), foreshadowed the movement's split. Weighing him sharpens A* judgements: by 1965 the nonviolent method had won law, not economics — which is where Black Power entered.

Black Power, 1968 and the Historiography

After the Voting Rights Act the movement's centre shifted north and its mood hardened: Watts (August 1965; 34 dead, thousands arrested) opened four summers of urban riots — the Kerner Report (1968) diagnosed 'two societies, one black, one white — separate and unequal'; Stokely Carmichael's call for 'Black Power' (Mississippi march, June 1966) rejected integration rhetoric, displacing John Lewis and pushing SNCC from interracial organising. The Black Panther Party (Huey Newton and Bobby Seale, Oakland, October 1966) fused Marxism, community programmes (free breakfasts, health clinics, police 'patrols') and armed self-defence under a Ten-Point Programme; COINTELPRO (Hoover's FBI) targeted it — infiltration, split and the killing of Fred Hampton (1969) — while urban risings and police shoot-outs replaced march politics.

King radicalised in parallel: against Vietnam (1967, losing liberal allies), for the Poor People's Campaign, and into the northern campaign — Chicago (1966) and the Cicero marches exposed de facto segregation's different mechanics, where no Bull Connor offered a televised villain — and was supporting the Memphis sanitation strike when James Earl Ray shot him on 4 April 1968; riots swept 100+ cities, and the Fair Housing Act passed on his body that month. Aftermath: affirmative action, Swann v Charlotte (1971) busing and Boston's anti-busing crisis (1974), and persistent economic inequality — deindustrialisation hit black workers hardest (Sugrue). The assassination matters in essays as turning point and symbol: it ended the movement's unifying figure and fixed its achievements in law (1964, 1965, 1968) while exposing how partial they were — legal equality without economic parity, a gap the Kerner Report had already named.

Historiography to deploy: the King-centred orthodoxy (Taylor Branch's trilogy; the standard Montgomery-to-Selma narrative of a 1954-65 consensus) versus the revisionists — Charles Payne (I've Got the Light of Freedom: the grassroots 'organizing tradition', local organisers in Mississippi who preceded and outlasted national campaigns), Adam Fairclough (Better Day Coming: local movements shaped the national wins), Jacquelyn Dowd Hall (the 'long civil rights movement': 1930s labour and left origins, truncated by the Cold War), Clayborne Carson (SNCC and indigenous leadership), Steven Lawson (federal politics and the presidency as the decisive arena) and Thomas Sugrue (the northern struggle continued past 1968). Gender and organisation add a further layer: Ella Baker (who founded SNCC in 1960 after leaving the SCLC) and Jo Ann Robinson (Montgomery's Women's Political Council) embodied the participatory, group-centred tradition Payne describes — against King's charismatic, leader-centred model. A* essays judge King against local movements and the federal government, citing these positions as frames — not decoration.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: 'The Civil Rights Act of 1964 owed more to the protests of 1963 than to any other factor.' Assess the validity of this view. (30 marks)
Mark-scheme logic: Level 5 (25-30): a sustained, analytically structured argument reaching a weighted judgement, with a wide range of accurate AO1 detail and factors weighed against each other throughout. Required AO1 knowledge: Birmingham (Project C, April-May 1963; Connor's dogs and fire hoses; televised children's marches; the 'Letter from Birmingham Jail'); the Kennedy administration's embarrassment and the June 1963 civil rights bill; the March on Washington (28 August 1963; c.250,000; 'I Have a Dream'); the Civil Rights Act's provisions (2 July 1964: public accommodations, Title VII employment, federal enforcement power). Other factors to weigh: the NAACP's legal campaign (Brown 1954; Browder v Gayle 1956) establishing the legal climate; Montgomery (1955-56) and the sit-ins and Freedom Rides (1960-61) building the protest repertoire; grassroots voter-registration work (SNCC, Mississippi Freedom Summer 1964) underpinning Selma and the 1965 Act; electoral timing — Johnson's 1964 landslide and Democratic majorities; Cold War image pressures shaping executive action since Truman (1948). FOR the 1963 protests: Birmingham's televised brutality converted northern opinion and forced the bill onto the agenda; the March demonstrated discipline and national scale. AGAINST: the Act was the product of a decade-long chain (Brown -> Montgomery -> sit-ins -> Freedom Rides -> Birmingham) — no single year was sufficient; the 1964 election mattered for passage; administrations acted only when protest made inaction costlier, as Kennedy's 'cooling-off' preference proves. Historians to deploy: Lawson (federal politics decisive), Fairclough and Payne (local movements built the pressure), Hall (the long civil rights movement). Judgement: the protests of 1963 — Birmingham above all — were the necessary catalyst, not the sufficient cause: the Act owed most to the accumulated pressure of the whole movement, with Birmingham the decisive escalation. Answers that narrate events without weighing factors, or lack a clear judgement, cap at Levels 3-4.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

To what extent did Brown v Board of Education (1954) transform the lives of black Americans by 1965? (30 marks)
Answer: Brown transformed the law, not behaviour: the Warren Court's unanimous ruling ('separate educational facilities are inherently unequal') destroyed Plessy's legitimacy and armed every later campaign — Little Rock (1957) and Cooper v Aaron (1958) enforced it against Massive Resistance (Southern Manifesto, 1956). But implementation was glacial: 'all deliberate speed' licensed delay, by 1964 barely 2% of southern black children attended integrated schools, and segregation persisted in buses (until Browder v Gayle, 1956) and public accommodations until the 1964 Act. Judgement: by 1965 Brown was the necessary foundation rather than a transformation of daily life — its importance lies in legitimising federal intervention and inspiring protest (which brought the Civil Rights Act and Voting Rights Act). Strong answers weigh the legal-revolution view against the implementation gap, citing the NAACP strategy and southern resistance.
Assess the importance of Martin Luther King to the success of the Montgomery Bus Boycott. (30 marks)
Answer: King mattered as symbol and organiser: as MIA president he gave the boycott its nonviolent rhetoric, its church base and its national media voice, sustaining morale through 381 days, house bombings and indictments — and Montgomery made him the movement's national figure, enabling the SCLC (1957). But he was not the sole cause: Parks's arrest activated years of NAACP groundwork (she was its trained secretary); Jo Ann Robinson's Women's Political Council produced the leaflets in hours; E.D. Nixon organised bail and leadership; the working-class walkers sustained the economic pressure; and desegregation came via Browder v Gayle (November 1956), a court victory built on NAACP litigation strategy. Judgement: King was the indispensable public leader of a movement he did not alone create — credit him for leadership and media impact, but weigh grassroots women, the car-pool organisation and the courts. Payne's and Fairclough's emphasis on local organising supports this balance.
To what extent did the rise of Black Power undermine the civil rights movement after 1965? (30 marks)
Answer: Undermining case: Carmichael's 'Black Power' call (June 1966) split the coalition — white liberals and organisations withdrew funding; SNCC and the SCLC diverged; urban riots (Watts 1965 onwards) fed white backlash, helping law-and-order politics (Nixon 1968); the Panthers' armed self-defence (Oakland, 1966) invited COINTELPRO repression and internal destruction (Fred Hampton, 1969); King's assassination (April 1968) removed the unifying figure. Counter-case: Black Power revived pride and self-reliance where nonviolence had won law but not economics; Panthers' breakfast and clinic programmes built community support; the radicalised climate pushed concessions — the Fair Housing Act (1968), affirmative action — and King himself moved toward the Poor People's Campaign. Judgement: Black Power did not cause the movement's decline — white resistance, the North's de facto segregation and economic stagnation did (Sugrue; Kerner Report 1968) — but it fragmented strategy and rhetoric at the moment unity mattered most. Weigh both; answers asserting simple 'undermined' or 'revived' miss the Kerner context.
Assess the view that the federal government was the main driver of civil rights progress, 1954-1965. (30 marks)
Answer: Federal action was decisive but reactive: Eisenhower sent the 101st Airborne to Little Rock (1957) and signed the weak 1957 Act only under pressure; Kennedy sent marshals to the Freedom Rides and Ole Miss (1962) but preferred to 'cool off' protest until Birmingham (1963) forced his bill; Johnson drove the Civil Rights Act (1964) and Voting Rights Act (1965) through Congress with real commitment and landslide majorities. Without federal law, local victories were reversible — hence Lawson's argument that national politics was the decisive arena. Against: federal action followed protest and litigation, it did not initiate them — the NAACP's cases (Brown, Browder v Gayle), Montgomery, the sit-ins, Birmingham and Selma made inaction costlier; grassroots organisers sustained campaigns between crises (Payne, Fairclough). Judgement: the federal government was the necessary enabler — it converted pressure into law — but the movement was the driver; without protest there was no pressure, without law there was no permanence. A* answers weigh both directions of causation rather than choosing one.
How far did Reconstruction fail black Americans? (30 marks)
Answer: Failure case is strong in outcome: the Compromise of 1877 withdrew federal troops; Redemption reversed black political power; sharecropping and debt peonage replaced slavery's economics; poll taxes, literacy tests and grandfather clauses disenfranchised the South by 1900; Plessy v Ferguson (1896) constitutionalised segregation, and lynching and the KKK enforced white supremacy. Counter-case: Reconstruction's constitutional foundation endured — the Thirteenth, Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments, though hollowed out, became the legal instruments of the NAACP's twentieth-century victories (Brown rested on the Fourteenth), and the experience of black officeholding and Freedmen's Bureau schooling left an organisational legacy. Judgement: Reconstruction failed in its own generation — by 1900 legal equality was dead in the South — but its amendments and institutional memory were the foundation on which the later movement rebuilt; 'failure' must be qualified by the long view. Weigh the contemporary-failure reading against the legacy reading, citing the NAACP strategy as evidence.
