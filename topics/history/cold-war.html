The Cold War

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC/Eduqas, CCEA

From Yalta to the fall of the Berlin Wall: containment, Berlin and Korea, Cuba, Vietnam, détente and the Gorbachev endgame, with Gaddis and the revisionists, plus 30-mark essay logic for AQA, Edexcel and OCR.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain the ideological and geopolitical origins of the Cold War: Yalta, Potsdam, the Iron Curtain (AO1)
  • Analyse containment's evolution: Truman Doctrine, Marshall Plan, Berlin Blockade, NATO and Warsaw Pact (AO1/AO2)
  • Assess the globalisation of the conflict: Korea 1950-53 and the Cuban Missile Crisis 1962 (AO2)
  • Evaluate Vietnam's impact and the achievements and limits of détente (SALT I/II, Helsinki) (AO1/AO2)
  • Explain the end of the Cold War: Afghanistan 1979, Reagan and SDI, Gorbachev's reforms, the revolutions of 1989-91 (AO1/AO2)
  • Deploy historiography (Gaddis, revisionists - Williams, Kolko, Alperovitz - and Zubok, Kotkin) as sustained argument (AO2)

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Containment: Kennan's strategy (Long Telegram, February 1946): resisting Soviet expansion wherever it appeared, implemented through the Truman Doctrine, Marshall Plan, alliances and, later, military intervention.
Iron Curtain: Churchill's phrase (Fulton, Missouri, 5 March 1946) for the Soviet-dominated divide across Europe 'from Stettin in the Baltic to Trieste in the Adriatic'.
Truman Doctrine: 12 March 1947: US pledge to support 'free peoples' resisting subjugation, with $400m for Greece and Turkey - the political arm of containment, globalising the commitment.
Marshall Plan: European Recovery Program (announced June 1947): ~$13bn of US aid 1948-52 rebuilding western European economies; Stalin refused participation and answered with Cominform (1947) and Comecon (1949).
MAD: Mutual assured destruction: deterrence logic in which both sides' secure second-strike capability makes attack suicidal; enshrined in the ABM Treaty (1972).
Détente: The relaxation of Cold War tension, c. 1969-79: SALT I and the ABM Treaty (1972), Helsinki (1975), SALT II (1979); ended by the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan (December 1979).
Glasnost and perestroika: Gorbachev's policies from 1985: 'openness' (permitted criticism, media liberalisation) and 'restructuring' (economic and political reform) - together dismantling the regime's legitimating myths.
SDI: Strategic Defense Initiative ('Star Wars', announced 23 March 1983): proposed space-based missile defence to break MAD; symbol of Reagan's pressure and of the USSR's inability to match US technology.

Origins and containment, 1945-49: ideology, Yalta, Potsdam, Berlin

Ideology framed the alliance's collapse: Marxism-Leninism prophesied inevitable conflict with capitalism; liberal self-determination could not absorb a Soviet security doctrine built on buffers. Yalta (February 1945) papered over the gap - the Declaration on Liberated Europe promised free elections, Germany was divided into four occupation zones, Poland's borders moved west (Curzon Line, Oder-Neisse) - but Potsdam (July-August 1945) exposed it: Truman, newly briefed on the atomic bomb (tested 16 July), demanded the promised elections; Attlee replaced Churchill mid-summit; reparations (Stalin's claim on Germany against US insistence on first exporting from current production) and Poland stayed unresolved. Stalin consolidated a buffer zone of front coalitions turning communist (Romania 1945, Poland 1947, Czechoslovakia's coup February 1948); Churchill's Iron Curtain speech (5 March 1946) named the divide, and Kennan's Long Telegram (February 1946) diagnosed 'neurotic' Soviet expansionism prescribing firm counter-pressure.

The quarrel was mirrored: Moscow's Novikov telegram (September 1946) cast the US as pursuing world supremacy through capital export - the Soviet mirror of Kennan - proof for essays that each side read the other's defensive consolidation as offensive. The flashpoints to command: Iran (withdrawal 1946), Greece (the civil war, 1946-49), Turkey (Straits pressure, 1946), Germany (Bizonia 1947, currency reform 1948) - each rehearsed the miscalculation that the Marshall Plan and Cominform turned into rival blocs.

Containment institutionalised: the Truman Doctrine (12 March 1947) - $400m for Greece and Turkey - pledged support for 'free peoples'; the Marshall Plan (June 1947) offered ~$13bn to all Europe, which Stalin forbade his sphere to take. Confrontation fixed on Germany: the Deutsche Mark (20 June 1948) triggered the Berlin Blockade (24 June 1948 - 12 May 1949); the western airlift (~2.3 million tonnes, ~278,000 flights) and a counter-blockade on East German exports broke it without firing. Its products: two German states (FRG May 1949, GDR October 1949), NATO (4 April 1949) and, after West German entry, the Warsaw Pact (May 1955). Historiography: the orthodox school (Feis, Schlesinger) blamed Soviet expansionism; revisionists (Williams's 'Open Door', Kolko, Alperovitz's 'atomic diplomacy') indicted US economic expansionism and nuclear coercion; Gaddis - post-revisionist, then the 'new Cold War history' after Moscow archives opened (We Now Know, 1997) - restored Stalin's agency while crediting both sides' security dilemmas.

Global confrontation, 1949-62: Korea and Cuba

The Soviet bomb (August 1949) and Mao's victory (October 1949) transformed containment: NSC-68 (April 1950) militarised it, and Korea tripled US defence spending. Korea (25 June 1950 - 27 July 1953): Kim Il-sung's invasion crossed the 38th parallel (the 1945 division) with Stalin's conditional approval; MacArthur's Inchon landing (September 1950) drove north, and Chinese 'volunteers' (~300,000 from October 1950) pushed the UN forces - 90% American, fighting unvetoed only because Moscow was boycotting the Security Council over China's seat - back to the parallel. The armistice, not peace: some three million dead, most of them Korean civilians; the DMZ persists today. Korea seeded the domino logic and made containment global and military.

Khrushchev's Berlin gambit: his 1958 ultimatum demanding western withdrawal from West Berlin within six months, then the Berlin Wall (13 August 1961) - built to stop the exodus of ~2.7 million East Germans (1949-61) - stabilised the front and, as Kennedy noted, was 'better than a war'. Cuban Missile Crisis (October 1962): Castro's revolution (1959) and the Bay of Pigs fiasco (April 1961) drove Cuba to Moscow; a U-2 flight photographed R-12 medium-range missiles under construction (14 October); Kennedy chose quarantine (announced 22 October) over air strike on EXCOMM's advice; Stevenson confronted Zorin at the UN with the photographic evidence; the deal - Soviet missiles out of Cuba, US Jupiter missiles quietly withdrawn from Turkey - came on 27-28 October, the closest approach of nuclear war. The hotline (1963) and the Partial Test Ban Treaty followed; MAD became the stabilising logic.

Vietnam and détente, 1962-79

Vietnam: escalation from 16,000 advisers (1963) through the Gulf of Tonkin resolution (August 1964) to 543,000 troops (1969) and Operation Rolling Thunder (1965-68); the Tết Offensive (January 1968) was a tactical failure for the communists but destroyed the US domestic consensus; Nixon's Vietnamisation and the Paris Peace Accords (January 1973) ended US combat; Saigon fell (30 April 1975). Costs: ~58,000 Americans, 1.5-3 million Vietnamese, over $100bn; the War Powers Act (1973) and the 'Vietnam syndrome' checked US intervention for a generation. Historiography: the debate is winnable-or-doomed - Summers's military case versus Karnow and Logevall (missed opportunities of 1963, political-strategic failure) and Westad, who reads the war as US Third World ideology mislabelling nationalism as communism.

Détente: the Sino-Soviet split (Ussuri border clashes, 1969) opened triangulation - Nixon's China visit (February 1972); SALT I (May 1972) froze strategic launchers and the ABM Treaty limited missile defence to two sites (later one), enshrining MAD; the Helsinki Accords (August 1975) traded recognition of Europe's borders (Basket I) for human rights commitments (Basket III) that dissidents - Charter 77, Sakharov - weaponised against Moscow; SALT II (June 1979) capped MIRVed launchers but died with Afghanistan. Détente's balance sheet for essays: it stabilised arms control and legitimised the status quo, but its critics (neo-conservative and orthodox) called it a one-way concession that normalised Soviet control of Eastern Europe; revisionists (Cohen) lament its abandonment as a lost path to ending the Cold War early. Note too the Brezhnev Doctrine (articulated after Czechoslovakia, August 1968): Moscow reserved the right to intervene where socialism was threatened - the guarantee Gorbachev would withdraw in 1989, making 1968 the essential contrast point in end-of-Cold-War essays.

The end of the Cold War, 1979-91: Afghanistan, Reagan, Gorbachev, 1989

The Soviet invasion of Afghanistan (December 1979) - supporting the PDPA against the mujahideen - killed détente: Carter's grain embargo, the Moscow Olympics boycott (1980) and the Carter Doctrine (January 1980) committed the US to the Persian Gulf. Reagan re-armed: defence spending rose from ~$134bn (1980) to ~$290bn (1988); the 'evil empire' speech (March 1983) and the Strategic Defense Initiative (23 March 1983) - space-based missile defence aimed at breaking MAD - raised pressure; Pershing II and cruise deployments in Europe (November 1983) answered the SS-20s, and the Able Archer exercise scare (November 1983) showed how close miscalculation came. The arms-race-won-it thesis for essays: SDI and deployments strained a stagnating Soviet economy - but the claim needs qualifying, since Soviet growth had stalled under Brezhnev before Reagan. Poland sharpened the pressure: Solidarity (founded August 1980, ~10 million members at its peak, Gdansk under Wałęsa) exposed the regime's hollow legitimacy; Jaruzelski's martial law (December 1981) suppressed it without Soviet invasion - a rehearsal, in reverse, of Moscow's 1989 restraint.

Gorbachev (general secretary from March 1985) chose reform over repression: glasnost and perestroika, underpinned by novoe myshlenie (new thinking) - security as mutual, not zero-sum. The INF Treaty (8 December 1987) abolished an entire class of missiles (2,692 - SS-20s and Pershing IIs) with on-site inspection; Reagan stood at the Brandenburg Gate (12 June 1987): 'Mr Gorbachev, tear down this wall'. 1989: Moscow renounced the Brezhnev Doctrine (the 'Sinatra doctrine'); Hungary opened its Austrian border; Leipzig's Monday demonstrations grew; the Berlin Wall fell (9 November 1989) after Schabowski's improvised press conference; Prague's Velvet Revolution and Romania's violent December followed. German reunification (3 October 1990) via the Two Plus Four Treaty; the Paris Charter (November 1990); the USSR itself dissolved after the failed August 1991 coup - Gorbachev resigned 25 December 1991. Historiography: triumphalists credit Reagan's arms race; Zubok stresses Gorbachev's ideas and illusions; Kotkin isolates the 'Gorbachev factor' - the USSR fell to its own leader's choices, not external pressure; Gaddis holds that a system could not compete economically and ideologically. A* weighs all three: external pressure (SDI, deployments), internal reform (glasnost destroying legitimacy), and contingency (the accidental timing of 1989).

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: 'Soviet expansionism, rather than American economic imperialism, was responsible for the outbreak of the Cold War.' How far do you agree? [30 marks]
Mark-scheme logic: 30-mark historiographical argument logic: deploy schools as frameworks, not name-drops. FOR Soviet expansionism (orthodox - Feis, Schlesinger; post-1991 Gaddis): the broken Yalta promise of free elections, the buffer zone 1945-48 (Romania 1945, Poland 1947, Czech coup February 1948), Cominform (1947)/Comecon (1949), the Berlin Blockade (1948-49) - expansion beyond the agreed spheres. FOR American responsibility (revisionists - Williams's 'Open Door', Kolko, Alperovitz): the Marshall Plan as economic penetration of Europe ($13bn, conditions favouring US trade), 'atomic diplomacy' (bomb unbriefed at Yalta, deployed at Potsdam), the Truman Doctrine's polarising universalism, NATO (1949) entrenching division. Weigh: post-revisionism (Gaddis) - neither side solely guilty; a security dilemma in which each defensive move (Marshall Plan, Cominform; NATO, Warsaw) read as offensive; Stalin's paranoia and US confidence in an open world economy were structurally incompatible. Judgement: Soviet expansionism was primary within Europe (physical seizure of ground), US economic statecraft primary in globalising the conflict (dollar diplomacy, Greek-Turkish commitment); A* requires the orthodox-revisionist-post-revisionist triad explicitly, a hierarchy, and a supported final judgement.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

'The Berlin Blockade (1948-49) was the most dangerous moment of the Cold War before 1962.' How far do you agree? [30 marks]
Answer: FOR: the first armed superpower confrontation - the airlift (~2.3m tonnes, ~278,000 flights) risked war at every collision; the future of Germany, and so of Europe's balance, hung on it; its outcomes (NATO April 1949, two German states) entrenched the division. AGAINST: Korea (1950-53) - actual war, some three million dead, Chinese entry, MacArthur advocating nuclear use; Cuba (1962) came closest to catastrophe (nuclear-armed missiles 90 miles from Florida, quarantine, 27-28 October). Judgement: the Blockade was the most dangerous structurally - it decided Europe's map - but Korea was the most lethal and Cuba the closest to nuclear war; a hierarchy, not a flat denial, earns the A* mark.
'The Cuban Missile Crisis was a triumph for Kennedy's policy of containment.' How far do you agree? [30 marks]
Answer: FOR: the quarantine worked without escalation; Soviet missiles left Cuba; Kennedy's calibrated EXCOMM process (rejecting the air-strike option) and Stevenson's UN evidence showed policy discipline; the hotline (1963) and Test Ban Treaty followed - crises managed, not war fought. AGAINST: the secret Jupiter withdrawal from Turkey was a real concession - revisionists read the 'victory' as a public-relations artefact; Castro remained in power and the Bay of Pigs (1961) had already shown rollback impossible; containment in the western hemisphere survived, but the crisis proved US economic and military pressure could not dictate outcomes inside the Soviet sphere. Judgement: defensive containment vindicated, but at a price the public record concealed - A* sets Gaddis's deterrence reading against Allison's bureaucratic models and the revisionists' concession thesis.
'US failure in Vietnam was primarily a military failure.' How far do you agree? [30 marks]
Answer: FOR: attrition ('search and destroy') never matched guerrilla warfare; Rolling Thunder (1965-68) failed to break Hanoi's will; the Tết Offensive (January 1968) was a US tactical victory that still exposed the impossibility of measuring progress (Westmoreland's 'light at the end of the tunnel'). AGAINST: politics first - Diem's overthrow (November 1963) removed a partner the US had helped destabilise; the Saigon regime's corruption and illegitimacy meant no local base; the home front - Tết destroyed consensus, Johnson's withdrawal (31 March 1968), Congress cutting funds and the War Powers Act (1973); ideology - Westad: the US misread Vietnamese nationalism as Soviet expansion. Judgement: primarily political-strategic, with the military defeat downstream of it - Summers's 'winnable' thesis is the minority position; Logevall's missed opportunities of 1963 is the A* counterweight.
'Gorbachev's reforms were the main reason for the collapse of Soviet control in Eastern Europe by 1989.' How far do you agree? [30 marks]
Answer: FOR: glasnost legitimised criticism of the whole system; the renunciation of the Brezhnev Doctrine meant no Soviet tanks in 1989 - the decisive contrast with Hungary 1956 and Czechoslovakia 1968; perestroika's failure stripped Moscow of the economic capacity to subsidise COMECON; the 'Sinatra doctrine' left regimes to fend for themselves. AGAINST: long-run structural decay - Brezhnev-era stagnation, the arms burden (SDI debate), and nationalism already organised underground (Solidarity in Poland 1980-81); external pressure - Reagan's deployments and SDI raised a cost Moscow could not pay; contingency - Schabowski's press conference, Hungary's border opening, and the speed of the Wall's fall (9 November 1989) owed much to accident. Judgement: Gorbachev's reforms were necessary - he withdrew the guarantee that had held Eastern Europe since 1945 - but not sufficient; Kotkin's 'Gorbachev factor' versus Zubok's ideas-and-illusions and the structural school gives the A* its nuance.
How useful are Khrushchev's memoirs and a US State Department telegram from October 1962 for a historian studying the Cuban Missile Crisis? [20 marks]
Answer: NATURE: retrospective memoir versus real-time diplomatic telegram - each proves a different layer. ORIGIN: Khrushchev writes after his 1964 fall, self-justifying - missiles as defensive protection of Cuba, his personal control of Anadyr (the missile deployment) stressed; US telegrams institutionalise Washington's threat perception (U-2 photographs of 14 October, quarantine options) but reflect bureaucratic framing, not the full picture. PURPOSE: the memoir shapes a legacy - defensive claims must be tested against the build-up's scale; the telegram informs decision-makers, so its contemporaneity is high and its hindsight zero. Utility: neither complete - the memoir reveals Soviet intentions and miscalculation, the telegram the US calculus; corroboration with Kennedy's White House tapes, EXCOMM minutes and post-1991 Soviet archives (Gaddis, Allison) tests each. A* concludes: a self-justifying source can still be highly useful for attitudes - here, Khrushchev's need to appear a peacemaker.
