Costing and Budgeting

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA

A* costing and budgeting for AQA 7127 (3.9-3.12): cost classification, marginal costing and break-even, absorption costing with under/over absorption, cash budgets, and standard costing variance analysis with causes and interdependence.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Classify costs as fixed, variable, semi-variable or stepped, and direct or indirect, and split semi-variable costs using the high-low method (AQA 7127, 3.10/3.12) (AO2).
  • Calculate contribution, break-even output and revenue, margin of safety and target-profit output, and evaluate the uses and limitations of break-even analysis (3.10) (AO2/AO3).
  • Calculate overhead absorption rates, absorbed overhead and under/over absorption, and reconcile marginal and absorption costing profits when inventory levels change (3.12) (AO2/AO3).
  • Prepare cash budgets with receipts and payments timed correctly, and evaluate the purposes of budgeting including behavioural effects such as budgetary slack (3.9) (AO2/AO3).
  • Calculate materials, labour and overhead variances against a flexed budget, and explain plausible causes and interdependence (3.11) (AO2/AO3).

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Contribution: Selling price per unit minus variable cost per unit: the amount each unit contributes towards fixed costs and then profit. Total contribution = units × contribution per unit.
Break-even output: The output at which total revenue equals total cost and profit is nil: fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit (or fixed costs ÷ C/S ratio for revenue).
Margin of safety: Actual or budgeted output minus break-even output: how far sales can fall before the business makes a loss; express in units or as a percentage of sales.
Semi-variable cost: A cost with a fixed element and a variable element (e.g. telephone line rental plus call charges); split using the high-low method: variable cost per unit = change in cost ÷ change in output.
Overhead absorption rate (OAR): Budgeted overhead ÷ budgeted activity (units, machine hours or labour hours): the rate used to charge fixed production overheads to units under absorption costing.
Under/over absorption: The difference between overheads absorbed (OAR × actual activity) and overheads actually incurred; under-absorption is an expense adjustment, over-absorption a credit.
Flexed budget: The original budget adjusted to the actual level of output, so that variances compare like with like: comparing actual results with the unflexed budget measures nothing useful.
Budgetary slack: Deliberately understating budgeted revenue or overstating budgeted costs to make targets easier to achieve - a behavioural flaw of imposed or loosely reviewed budgets.

📌 Key Points

Break-even units = fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit; margin of safety = actual − break-even; target-profit units = (fixed costs + target profit) ÷ contribution per unit.
Marginal costing values inventory at variable production cost and writes off fixed overheads as period costs; absorption costing carries fixed overhead in inventory - profits differ when inventory levels change.
OAR always uses BUDGETED overhead ÷ BUDGETED activity. Under-absorption = actual overhead > absorbed overhead.
Cash budgets forecast cash, not profit: sales on credit become receipts next month; purchases are paid a month after buying; depreciation never appears.
Variances compare ACTUAL with FLEXED budget. Materials: price (paid vs standard price) and usage (used vs standard for actual output). Labour: rate and efficiency.

Cost classification, marginal costing and break-even (AQA 7127, 3.10)

Cost behaviour - not cost type - drives every management accounting decision in Paper 2. Fixed costs (rent, salaries, insurance) do not move with output within a relevant range; variable costs (materials, piece-rate labour, sales commission) move in direct proportion; semi-variable costs (electricity with a standing charge) contain both; stepped costs (a second supervisor hired once output passes a threshold) jump at activity points. Separately, direct costs trace to a unit (materials, direct labour); indirect costs (overheads) do not. A cost can be direct and fixed (a dedicated supervisor for one production line) - the two classifications answer different questions, and confusing them is a classic error.

The high-low method splits a semi-variable cost: take the highest and lowest activity levels (not the highest and lowest costs), compute variable cost per unit = change in cost ÷ change in units, then fixed cost = total cost at either point − variable element. With output of 4,000 units costing £38,000 and 9,000 units costing £78,000: variable = 40,000/5,000 = £8 per unit; fixed = 38,000 − (4,000 × 8) = £6,000; predicted cost at 7,000 units = 6,000 + (7,000 × 8) = £62,000. Two cautions for evaluation marks: the method uses only two observations, so outliers distort it; and it assumes linearity and a stable fixed element, so it is an estimate, not a law.

Worked example: make-or-buy and limiting factors

Make-or-buy. A component costs £18 per unit to make: materials £7, direct labour £6, variable overhead £3, plus £2 of absorbed fixed overhead. A supplier quotes £16. Compare the RIGHT figures: the variable cost of making is £16 (7 + 6 + 3) - the £2 absorbed fixed overhead continues whatever you choose. Making and buying are cost-identical on variable cost, so decide on capacity and quality; had the quote been £17, buying saves £1 per unit of contribution while capacity is freed for other work. The exam rule: exclude fixed costs that do not change, include any that do.

Limiting factor. Products A and B: contribution per unit A £12, B £9 - A looks better. But machine hours are scarce: A needs 4 hours, B needs 2. Contribution per machine hour: A £3.00, B £4.50 - B ranks first. Rank by contribution per unit of the scarce factor, allocate capacity accordingly, then calculate the optimal production plan and total contribution. The mistake AQA watches for is ranking by contribution per unit; the evaluation point is that the 'correct' plan maximises total contribution, not unit contribution.

Multi-product questions use the C/S ratio differently: with a constant sales mix, weighted-average contribution per unit (or weighted C/S ratio) feeds the same formulas - break-even revenue = fixed costs ÷ weighted C/S ratio. If the mix shifts towards lower-contribution products, break-even rises even when total sales hold - a subtlety worth one sentence in any mixed-mix answer. Sensitivity language to reuse: 'each £1 fall in selling price raises break-even by fixed costs ÷ new contribution' - concrete and computable rather than vague.

Where does management accounting data come from, and how reliable is it? Estimates of variable and fixed splits come from past records (hence high-low), engineering studies, or supplier quotes; they carry sampling error and may reflect a cost structure that has changed (a new energy contract, automation replacing labour). Strong answers acknowledge the evidence base behind the numbers before recommending action - 'the £8 variable cost per unit is an estimate from two observations; before committing to the 12,000-unit plan, management should re-estimate using regression or supplier quotations' - and that habit converts a calculation answer into an evaluation answer.

Contribution = selling price − variable cost per unit, and it is the engine of short-run decisions. Break-even output = fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit; break-even revenue = fixed costs ÷ C/S ratio, where the C/S (contribution/sales) ratio = contribution per unit ÷ selling price; margin of safety = budgeted output − break-even output (in units, or ÷ budgeted output × 100 as a percentage); target-profit output = (fixed costs + target profit) ÷ contribution per unit. On a break-even chart, total revenue and total cost lines cross at break-even; the contribution chart plots contribution against fixed costs and shows profit building only after the fixed line is covered. Learn to describe the chart as well as compute it - AQA asks for both.

Worked example: full break-even analysis

Fixed costs £84,000; selling price £24; variable cost £14; budgeted output 11,000 units; target profit £36,000.

  • Contribution per unit = 24 − 14 = £10.
  • Break-even output = 84,000 ÷ 10 = 8,400 units.
  • Break-even revenue = 8,400 × 24 = £201,600 (check: C/S ratio = 10/24 = 41.67%; 84,000 ÷ 0.4167 = £201,600).
  • Margin of safety = 11,000 − 8,400 = 2,600 units, or 2,600/11,000 × 100 = 23.6% of budgeted sales.
  • Target profit £36,000: units = (84,000 + 36,000) ÷ 10 = 12,000 units.

Interpretation: each unit sold beyond 8,400 adds £10 of profit; 12,000 units × £10 − 84,000 = £36,000. If variable cost rose to £16, contribution falls to £8 and break-even jumps to 10,500 units - the sensitivity examiners ask you to comment on.

Decisions using marginal costing: make-or-buy - compare the buying price with the marginal cost of making (only relevant variable costs; fixed costs usually continue, so include them only if they change); special orders below normal price - accept if contribution is positive and spare capacity exists, but consider the reaction of regular customers; limiting factors - when a scarce resource (machine hours, materials) caps output, rank products by contribution per unit of the scarce factor, not contribution per unit. All are short-run tools: in the long run fixed costs must be covered and prices must deliver a normal profit, which is the standard evaluation point.

The limitations of break-even analysis, ready to deploy in a 12-marker: it assumes linear costs and revenue (no bulk discounts, no economies of scale, one price for all units); it assumes all output is sold at the same price (ignores stock-building and price discrimination); fixed costs are constant over all output ranges (in truth they step); it handles one product or a constant mix only; and it is a static model - demand uncertainty, competitor response and cost inflation sit outside it. Its strengths are equal: transparent, cheap, communicates risk through the margin of safety, and gives a first-pass target for pricing and capacity. The A* judgement: break-even analysis is a planning aid to be stress-tested (what if price falls 5%? variable cost rises 10%?), not a decision machine.

Draw and label the chart if the question asks: horizontal axis output, vertical axis £; fixed costs as a horizontal line starting on the vertical axis; total cost rising with the gradient of variable cost per unit; total revenue rising from the origin with the gradient of the selling price; the intersection labelled break-even; margin of safety as the horizontal gap between budgeted output and break-even. A contribution chart replaces the total-cost line's separation by plotting a contribution line rising at contribution per unit against a horizontal fixed-cost line - profit exists only where contribution exceeds fixed costs, and the crossing point is break-even. Describing these features precisely (gradients, starting points, the meaning of the gap) earns method marks in many structured questions, so practise sketching both once under time pressure.

Absorption costing and ABC (AQA 7127, 3.12)

Absorption costing exists because financial statements need inventory values that include production overhead. The mechanism runs in stages. Allocation assigns whole overhead items to the departments that caused them (a production supervisor's salary to that production department). Apportionment splits shared items on a sensible basis (rent by floor area, power by machine hours). Reapportionment then charges service departments (maintenance, canteen) to production departments - by repeated distribution or the step method, questions will state the basis. Finally an overhead absorption rate charges overheads to units: OAR = budgeted overhead ÷ budgeted activity, with activity usually machine hours (machine-intensive), labour hours (labour-intensive) or units.

Worked example: OAR and under/over absorption

Budgeted overhead £180,000; budgeted machine hours 24,000; actual overhead £176,000; actual machine hours 22,000.

  1. OAR = 180,000 ÷ 24,000 = £7.50 per machine hour.
  2. Overhead absorbed = 7.50 × 22,000 = £165,000.
  3. Under-absorption = 176,000 − 165,000 = £11,000 - added to production cost in the statement of profit or loss.

Diagnose the cause: hours fell 2,000 below budget (activity effect: 2,000 × 7.50 = £15,000 under), while spending came in £4,000 under budget (spending effect: −4,000). Net −£11,000. Two causes, two directions - the decomposition examiners reward.

Over-absorption (absorbed exceeds actual) is credited; under-absorption is charged. Both arise from two sources: actual activity differing from budget, or actual spending differing from budget. The conceptual criticism AQA expects: arbitrary rates distort unit costs when products consume overheads unevenly - which is the gap ABC was invented to close.

Worked example: reapportionment of a service department

Production departments: Machining (overhead £60,000, 20,000 machine hours) and Assembly (£40,000, 10,000 labour hours); service department Maintenance (£20,000), which works 60% for Machining and 40% for Assembly.

  1. Reapportion Maintenance: Machining +£12,000 → £72,000; Assembly +£8,000 → £48,000.
  2. Machining OAR = 72,000 ÷ 20,000 machine hours = £3.60 per machine hour.
  3. Assembly OAR = 48,000 ÷ 10,000 labour hours = £4.80 per labour hour.

Choosing the base follows the cause: machine hours where machines dominate the work, labour hours where people do. Where a question offers both bases and asks which is more appropriate, it wants the causal justification, not a coin flip - and where a service department serves other service departments too, use repeated distribution until the residuals become immaterial, as the question directs.

Activity-based costing pools overheads by activity (set-ups, inspections, ordering, machine running) and charges products through cost drivers (number of set-ups, number of inspections). The logic: products cause activities, activities cause costs. ABC improves accuracy where overheads are high and product diversity is real (a complex product that needs many set-ups absorbs its true cost instead of being subsidised by simple high-volume lines). Its costs: data collection is expensive, drivers are not always measurable, and the choice of driver is still a judgement. For 12-mark evaluation: ABC gives better product-level decisions (pricing, dropping products) in modern overhead-heavy environments; traditional absorption is cheaper to run and adequate where overheads are low or products are homogeneous - the classic 'set-ups' versus 'machine hours' contrast.

Marginal versus absorption costing is a guaranteed comparison question. Both treat selling and administration overheads as period costs; they differ only on fixed production overhead: marginal costing expenses it as incurred; absorption costing defers it inside inventory values. So when inventory increases, absorption shows higher profit (some fixed overhead rides in closing inventory into next period); when inventory falls, absorption shows lower profit; when inventory is unchanged, profits are identical. The reconciliation: difference in profit = change in inventory units × fixed overhead per unit. Example: fixed overhead per unit £6; inventory rises from 1,000 to 2,500 units; absorption profit = marginal profit + 1,500 × 6 = marginal profit + £9,000. State also the arguments: marginal costing aids short-run decisions (contribution logic) but understates inventory value against IAS 2, which absorption satisfies; absorption smooths reported profit and matches total production cost to units sold, but can let managers hide cost overruns in inventory build-ups - a link to creative accounting in topic 5.

When the comparison is asked as a 12-marker, the frame that scores: define both methods' treatment of fixed production overhead (AO1); compute or reconcile the profit difference via the inventory change (AO2); then evaluate on three axes - decision relevance (marginal/contribution logic wins for short-run choices), external reporting compliance (absorption satisfies inventory valuation rules), and behavioural risk (inventory build-ups can defer overhead into stock and flatter profit - absorption invites it, marginal exposes it). Close with the purpose judgement: a business should manage by marginal costing and report by absorption costing; the 'difference' is a reporting convention, not a dispute about facts.

Structure your comparison answers as a table in your head - inventory value, fixed overhead treatment, decision usefulness, external reporting - then a judgement on purpose: management decision-making favours marginal costing; statutory statements require absorption. Questions often set a small profit calculation under both methods for the same data; practising the reconciliation by the change-in-inventory shortcut saves five minutes and removes the arithmetic risk of building both statements in full.

Two refinements complete 3.12. First, non-production overheads (selling, distribution, administration) are period costs under both methods - never absorbed into units - so a unit cost that includes advertising has misclassified. Second, norms for the exam: fixed overhead per unit for reconciliations uses only fixed PRODUCTION overhead divided by normal (budgeted) output; variable costs per unit are identical under both methods; and if asked for 'the profit under marginal costing', build it as sales − variable cost of sales − all fixed costs, in that order, rather than adapting an absorption statement - fewer steps, fewer errors. Under time pressure, the inventory-change shortcut (difference = change in units × fixed overhead per unit) converts one full statement into one line of arithmetic.

Worked example: ABC versus traditional absorption

Set-up costs £60,000. Product X: 2,000 units, 10 set-ups; Product Y: 8,000 units, 90 set-ups. Traditional (machine hours, 50,000 total): rate £1.20; X absorbs 2,000 × 2 hours × 1.20 = £4,800 (per unit £2.40); Y absorbs 8,000 × 5 hours × 1.20 = £48,000 (per unit £6.00). ABC (driver = set-ups, 100 total): rate £600 per set-up; X = 10 × 600 = £6,000 (£3.00 per unit); Y = 90 × 600 = £54,000 (£6.75 per unit). Y, the complex product, was under-costed by traditional absorption - simple X subsidised it. Decisions that follow: Y's pricing and any 'drop Y' analysis were built on wrong costs. That single contrast is the standard way to earn the ABC evaluation marks.

Budgeting, cash budgets and variance analysis (AQA 7127, 3.9 and 3.11)

A budget is a quantified plan for a period, and budgeting earns its place in the specification through purposes you must be able to evaluate: planning (forcing management to look forward), coordination (aligning production, purchasing, sales and finance), communication and motivation (targets that stretch but are attainable), control (variances flag where reality departs from plan), resource allocation, and authorisation of spending. The master budget gathers the functional budgets (sales, production, purchases, labour, overheads) into budgeted financial statements plus a cash budget. Evaluation angle: budgets only deliver these benefits if targets are realistic and accepted - unattainable targets demotivate; too-easy targets (budgetary slack) invite complacency; participative budgeting (managers set their own targets) improves commitment but invites slack, a tension worth stating in any 25-marker.

The cash budget is where marks are won or lost on timing. Rules: credit sales become receipts one or more months later (per the stated terms); purchases are paid the month after buying (or per terms); wages, rent and overheads follow their stated patterns; depreciation is never in a cash budget (no cash moves); capital purchases hit cash when paid; VAT and discounts only if specified. Work from receipts − payments = net cash flow, add opening balance, get closing balance - which becomes next month's opening balance.

Worked example: two-month cash budget

Opening cash January: £5,000. Sales: December £40,000, January £50,000, February £60,000 - half for cash, half collected one month later. Purchases: December £24,000, January £30,000, February £32,000 - paid one month after purchase. Wages £10,000 and overheads £6,000 per month, paid in the month. Equipment £15,000 paid in February.

Read it as management would: February closes at £4,000 after the equipment purchase - tight but positive; a 10% sales shortfall would tip February negative, so a modest overdraft facility is prudent. That interpretive sentence is the difference between a calculation answer and a management answer.

Preparation sequence matters for full-question marks: the sales budget comes first (it drives everything), then production budget = budgeted sales + budgeted closing inventory − opening inventory, then purchases, labour and overhead budgets, and finally the cash budget and budgeted statements. A question asking 'state the order of preparation and why' is testing that dependency chain - sales is the principal budget factor in most businesses, though a scarce resource (limited machine capacity, scarce skilled labour) can cap sales instead, in which case the limiting factor leads. Link this back to the limiting-factor ranking in section 1: the same scarce resource logic governs both the operating plan and the budget structure.

Standard costing and variance analysis (3.11) compares actual results with a flexed budget - the original budget restated at the actual output level. Variance sign convention: favourable (F) raises profit against the flexed budget; adverse (A) reduces it. The core calculations:

VarianceFormula
Materials price(Standard price − actual price) × actual quantity purchased/used
Materials usage(Standard quantity for actual output − actual quantity) × standard price
Labour rate(Standard rate − actual rate) × actual hours
Labour efficiency(Standard hours for actual output − actual hours) × standard rate
Fixed overhead expenditureBudgeted fixed overhead − actual fixed overhead
Fixed overhead volume(Actual output − budgeted output) × fixed overhead per unit (absorption costing)
Sales price(Actual price − standard price) × actual units sold
Sales volume (profit)(Actual units − budgeted units) × standard profit/contribution per unit
Worked example: a full variance set

Standard: 3 kg of material at £5 per kg (£15 per unit); 1.5 hours of labour at £12 per hour (£18 per unit). Budgeted output 4,000 units. Actual output 4,000 units; materials used 12,400 kg costing £59,520; labour 6,200 hours costing £74,400.

  • Materials price: actual price = 59,520 ÷ 12,400 = £4.80; (5.00 − 4.80) × 12,400 = £2,480 Favourable.
  • Materials usage: standard for actual output = 4,000 × 3 = 12,000 kg; (12,000 − 12,400) × 5 = £2,000 Adverse.
  • Labour rate: actual rate = 74,400 ÷ 6,200 = £12.00; variance nil.
  • Labour efficiency: standard hours = 4,000 × 1.5 = 6,000; (6,000 − 6,200) × 12 = £2,400 Adverse.

Causes that fit the pattern: cheap material (£4.80 vs £5) of lower quality - explaining BOTH the price saving and the excess usage and slow work; a possible training or machine-calibration problem behind the efficiency loss. The interdependence sentence - 'the favourable price variance was purchased at the cost of adverse usage and efficiency' - is precisely what analysis marks are for. Note also: with output at budget, the flexed budget equals the original; with output at 4,500 units, every usage/efficiency standard would first be restated at 4,500 units.

Evaluation of standard costing: it quantifies control, supports responsibility accounting and highlights exceptions worth managing attention - but standards age (price lists, wage awards change), over-precise targets can drive dysfunctional behaviour (buying cheap material to 'win' the price variance), and some variances are interdependent so must be read together. In the exam: calculate precisely, then explain each variance with a cause that is consistent with the others, and close with the interdependence point. If asked to 'evaluate the variance analysis for management', add that only controllable variances warrant action, and that uncontrollable adverse variances (a market-wide price rise) need re-forecasting, not blame.

Worked example: flexing before analysing

Budget: 4,000 units, sales revenue £96,000, variable costs £56,000, fixed costs £30,000, profit £10,000. Actual: 4,500 units, revenue £105,000, variable costs £63,500, fixed costs £30,400.

  • Flexed budget at 4,500 units: revenue 4,500 × 24 = £108,000; variable costs 4,500 × 14 = £63,000; fixed £30,000; profit £15,000.
  • Sales price variance: (24.00 − 105,000/4,500) × 4,500 = (24 − 23.333) × 4,500 = £3,000 Adverse.
  • Sales volume (contribution) variance: (4,500 − 4,000) × standard contribution 10 = £5,000 Favourable.
  • Variable cost variance: 63,000 − 63,500 = £500 Adverse; fixed overhead expenditure: 30,000 − 30,400 = £400 Adverse.

Against the UNFLEXED budget, revenue is £9,000 favourable - but that mixes selling more units with selling them cheaper. Flexing isolates the two effects: more units (good) at a discounted price (bad). That is the entire point of the technique and a one-sentence explanation AQA repeatedly rewards.

Close the topic with the exam pattern: a 20-mark question typically gives a budget and actuals, asks for three or four variances (6-8 marks), the flexed budget (3-4 marks), then causes and interdependence (5-6 marks); a 25-marker may add 'evaluate the usefulness of the standard costing system'. Whatever the wrapper, the sequence is fixed: flex, calculate with F/A labels, explain consistently, evaluate with behaviour and controllability. Practise writing the explanation sentence in the same breath as each calculation - 'the adverse usage variance of £2,000 reflects the cheaper material buckling on the press, which also explains the £2,400 adverse efficiency variance' - because that habit is what turns variance analysis from arithmetic into management accounting.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: A company makes a single product. Fixed costs are £84,000 per year, selling price £24 per unit, variable cost £14 per unit. Budgeted output is 11,000 units. (a) Calculate the break-even output, the margin of safety (units and %), and the output needed for a target profit of £36,000. (b) Evaluate the usefulness of break-even analysis to the managers of this business. (12 marks)
Mark-scheme logic: (a) AO2: contribution = 24 − 14 = £10; break-even = 84,000 ÷ 10 = 8,400 units; margin of safety = 11,000 − 8,400 = 2,600 units = 2,600 ÷ 11,000 × 100 = 23.6% of budgeted sales; target profit output = (84,000 + 36,000) ÷ 10 = 12,000 units. (b) AO3 evaluation: usefulness - simple and transparent; quantifies risk via the 23.6% margin of safety (sales can fall 23.6% before losses); supports pricing decisions (a £2 price cut raises break-even from 8,400 to 10,500 units); provides a target (12,000 units) for capacity and sales planning; the break-even chart communicates the cost/revenue structure to non-specialists. Limitations - assumes linearity (no economies of scale or bulk discounts), constant fixed costs at all outputs (they step), all output sold at one price, a single product or constant mix, and a static demand environment; it is short-run because fixed costs must eventually be covered. Judgement: useful as a first-pass planning and risk tool, particularly for the margin of safety, but should be stress-tested for price and cost changes and supported by market evidence before committing to the 12,000-unit target.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Fixed costs are £84,000. Selling price £24, variable cost £14, budgeted output 11,000 units. Calculate: contribution per unit; break-even in units and in revenue; margin of safety in units and as a percentage; output required for a target profit of £36,000. (8 marks)
Answer: Contribution = 24 − 14 = £10 per unit. Break-even = 84,000 ÷ 10 = 8,400 units; break-even revenue = 8,400 × 24 = £201,600 (C/S ratio 10/24 = 41.67%; 84,000 ÷ 0.4167 = £201,600). Margin of safety = 11,000 − 8,400 = 2,600 units; as % of budgeted sales = 2,600 ÷ 11,000 × 100 = 23.6%. Target profit output = (84,000 + 36,000) ÷ 10 = 12,000 units. Mark logic: contribution (1), break-even units (2), revenue (1), MoS units and % (2), target output (2). Show formulas - method marks survive small arithmetic slips.
Total costs at 4,000 units were £38,000; at 9,000 units they were £78,000. Using the high-low method, calculate the variable cost per unit and the fixed cost, and predict total cost at 7,000 units. (6 marks)
Answer: Variable cost per unit = (78,000 − 38,000) ÷ (9,000 − 4,000) = 40,000 ÷ 5,000 = £8. Fixed cost = 38,000 − (4,000 × 8) = 38,000 − 32,000 = £6,000. Check at 9,000 units: 6,000 + 72,000 = £78,000. Predicted cost at 7,000 units = 6,000 + (7,000 × 8) = £62,000. Mark logic: change in cost over change in ACTIVITY (not cost) levels (2); fixed cost (2); prediction (2). State the assumption: cost behaviour linear between the two points.
Budgeted overhead £180,000; budgeted machine hours 24,000. Actual overhead £176,000; actual machine hours 22,000. Calculate the OAR, the overhead absorbed, and the under/over absorption, explaining its causes. (6 marks)
Answer: OAR = 180,000 ÷ 24,000 = £7.50 per machine hour. Absorbed = 7.50 × 22,000 = £165,000. Under-absorption = 176,000 − 165,000 = £11,000, charged as an expense. Causes: activity effect - 2,000 fewer machine hours than budget left overhead unabsorbed (2,000 × 7.50 = £15,000 under); spending effect - overheads came in £4,000 below budget (a favourable spending variance). Net: −15,000 + 4,000 = −£11,000 under-absorbed. Mark logic: OAR from BUDGETED figures (2); absorbed using ACTUAL hours (1); under-absorption direction (1); two-cause decomposition (2).
Standard: 3 kg at £5 per kg and 1.5 labour hours at £12 per hour per unit. Actual output 4,000 units; materials used 12,400 kg costing £59,520; labour 6,200 hours costing £74,400. Calculate the materials price and usage variances and the labour rate and efficiency variances, and suggest one consistent explanation. (10 marks)
Answer: Materials price: actual price = 59,520 ÷ 12,400 = £4.80; (5.00 − 4.80) × 12,400 = £2,480 Favourable. Materials usage: standard for actual output = 12,000 kg; (12,000 − 12,400) × 5 = £2,000 Adverse. Labour rate: actual rate = 74,400 ÷ 6,200 = £12.00; (12.00 − 12.00) × 6,200 = nil. Labour efficiency: standard hours = 6,000; (6,000 − 6,200) × 12 = £2,400 Adverse. Consistent explanation: cheaper, lower-grade material (F price) caused more waste (A usage) and slower working (A efficiency) - the variances are interdependent, so the £2,480 saving was partly consumed by £4,400 of usage and efficiency losses. Mark logic: each variance calculated correctly with F/A label (7); interdependent cause explanation (3).
Prepare the cash budget for January and February. Opening cash 1 January £5,000. Sales: December £40,000, January £50,000, February £60,000 - half cash, half collected one month later. Purchases: December £24,000, January £30,000, February £32,000 - paid one month later. Wages £10,000 and overheads £6,000 per month paid when incurred. Equipment £15,000 paid in February. State each closing balance. (10 marks)
Answer: January: receipts = 25,000 (Jan cash sales) + 20,000 (Dec credit) = £45,000; payments = purchases 24,000 + wages 10,000 + overheads 6,000 = £40,000; net +5,000; opening 5,000; closing £10,000. February: receipts = 30,000 (Feb cash sales) + 25,000 (Jan credit) = £55,000; payments = purchases 30,000 + wages 10,000 + overheads 6,000 + equipment 15,000 = £61,000; net −6,000; opening 10,000; closing £4,000. Mark logic: correct timing of credit receipts and purchase payments (4); cash sales in month (2); equipment in February only (1); compounding balances month to month (2); no depreciation anywhere (1). Comment: February's £4,000 is thin after the equipment outlay - flag overdraft headroom for management.
