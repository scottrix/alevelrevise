AC1: Financial Statements, Ratio Analysis and Costing

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level All Boards (AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC, CCEA) AQA

Double-entry bookkeeping, income statements, statements of financial position, marginal costing, break-even, variance analysis.

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Double-Entry System: Every financial transaction affects at least two accounts with equal and opposite Debit and Credit entries (Assets = Liabilities + Equity).
Break-Even Point: The level of output where total revenue equals total costs: Fixed Costs / Contribution per unit.
Statement of Profit or Loss (Income Statement): Revenue − Cost of Sales = Gross Profit; Gross Profit − Expenses (admin, distribution, finance costs) = Profit for the Year. Cost of sales = Opening Inventory + Purchases − Closing Inventory. Matching (accruals) concept: costs are matched to the period in which revenue is earned, not when cash is paid.
Statement of Financial Position (Balance Sheet): Snapshot at a point in time: Non-current assets (depreciated) + Current assets (inventory, receivables, cash) = Equity (capital, retained earnings) + Non-current liabilities + Current liabilities (payables, overdraft). Depreciation: straight-line (cost − residual) / useful life or reducing balance % × net book value; it spreads cost over useful life (matching concept).
Liquidity and Efficiency Ratios: Current ratio = Current Assets / Current Liabilities (benchmark ~1.5–2:1); Acid test = (Current Assets − Inventory) / Current Liabilities (benchmark ~1:1); Inventory turnover = Cost of Sales / Average Inventory; Receivables days = Receivables / Credit Sales × 365; Payables days = Payables / Credit Purchases × 365. Working capital = Current Assets − Current Liabilities.
Profitability and Gearing Ratios: Gross profit margin = Gross Profit / Revenue × 100; Operating (net) margin = Operating Profit / Revenue × 100; ROCE = Operating Profit / Capital Employed × 100 (Capital Employed = Equity + Non-current liabilities); Gearing = Debt / (Debt + Equity) × 100 (high gearing > 50% = risky in downturns because interest must be paid). Use comparatives and industry benchmarks for meaningful interpretation.
Marginal vs Absorption Costing: Marginal costing values inventory at variable production cost only and treats fixed overheads as period costs; contribution per unit = Selling price − Variable cost. Absorption costing shares fixed overheads over units produced (OAR = Budgeted overhead / Budgeted activity). Under/over-absorption arises when actual activity differs from budget. Marginal costing is better for short-run decisions (make-or-buy, special order); absorption costing is required by accounting standards (IAS 2) for external statements.

🔍 Key Principles & Specification Requirements

  • Financial statements: Statement of Profit or Loss (revenue - cost of sales = gross profit; - expenses = net profit); Statement of Financial Position.
  • Working capital = Current Assets - Current Liabilities; Acid Test ratio = (Current Assets - Inventory) / Current Liabilities.
  • Costing techniques: Marginal costing (variable costs assigned to products, fixed costs treated as period costs) vs Absorption costing.

💡 Worked Example Question

Exam-Style Question

Question:

A firm has fixed costs of £60,000, selling price of £25/unit, and variable costs of £10/unit. Calculate break-even output and margin of safety if sales are 5,000 units.

Model Solution & Mark Scheme:

Contribution per unit = £25 - £10 = £15.
Break-even output = £60,000 / £15 = 4,000 units.
Margin of Safety = Actual Sales - Break-even = 5,000 - 4,000 = 1,000 units (or 20%).

Q3: A company reports the following at 31 December: Revenue GBP 800,000; Cost of sales GBP 480,000; Operating expenses GBP 160,000; Inventory GBP 40,000; Receivables GBP 70,000; Cash GBP 30,000; Current liabilities GBP 70,000; Equity GBP 350,000; Non-current liabilities GBP 150,000. Calculate the gross profit margin, current ratio, acid test ratio and ROCE, and interpret each result.

Show Model Answer

Answer: Gross profit = Revenue − Cost of sales = 800,000 − 480,000 = GBP 320,000. Gross profit margin = 320,000 / 800,000 × 100 = 40%. For every GBP 1 of sales, 40p remains to cover expenses and profit — meaningful only against prior years and industry benchmarks; a fall could indicate price competition or rising input costs. Current ratio = Current assets / Current liabilities = (40,000 + 70,000 + 30,000) / 70,000 = 140,000 / 70,000 = 2:1 — within the traditional 1.5–2:1 benchmark: liquid enough to meet short-term obligations, but not so liquid that assets are idle. Acid test = (Current assets − Inventory) / Current liabilities = (140,000 − 40,000) / 70,000 = 100,000 / 70,000 = 1.43:1 — above the 1:1 benchmark, so the company could pay its current liabilities without relying on selling inventory, indicating strong liquidity. ROCE = Operating profit / Capital employed × 100. Operating profit = Gross profit − Operating expenses = 320,000 − 160,000 = GBP 160,000. Capital employed = Equity + Non-current liabilities = 350,000 + 150,000 = GBP 500,000. ROCE = 160,000 / 500,000 × 100 = 32%. Evaluation: ROCE of 32% is strong if the cost of borrowing is well below it (compare with interest rate on the GBP 150,000 debt); however, ratios alone are insufficient — they use historical cost data, are distorted by window dressing (e.g. delaying payables before the year end), and ignore cash flow timing; ROCE should also be compared with the previous year's ROCE and with industry averages before concluding on performance, and the acid test's high value may partly reflect slow-paying receivables (check receivables days).

Q4: A firm produces a single product with fixed costs of GBP 90,000 per year, selling price GBP 30 per unit and variable cost GBP 18 per unit. Budgeted output is 9,000 units. (a) Calculate the break-even output and the margin of safety. (b) Calculate the budgeted profit using marginal costing. (c) Evaluate the usefulness of break-even analysis for decision-making.

Show Model Answer

Answer: (a) Contribution per unit = Selling price − Variable cost = 30 − 18 = GBP 12. Break-even output = Fixed costs / Contribution per unit = 90,000 / 12 = 7,500 units. Margin of safety = Budgeted output − Break-even output = 9,000 − 7,500 = 1,500 units (or 1,500 / 9,000 × 100 ≈ 16.7% of budgeted sales) — sales can fall by 16.7% before the firm makes a loss. (b) Marginal costing profit: Total contribution = 9,000 × 12 = GBP 108,000. Profit = Contribution − Fixed costs = 108,000 − 90,000 = GBP 18,000. (Check: at break-even 7,500 units, contribution exactly covers fixed costs, profit = 0; each unit beyond break-even adds GBP 12 profit, and 1,500 × 12 = GBP 18,000. Consistent.) (c) Evaluation: break-even analysis is useful — it is simple to calculate and communicate, identifies the target output for profitability, supports pricing decisions (how a price change moves break-even), and the margin of safety quantifies risk for lenders and managers; it can be shown on a break-even chart with TR and TC lines crossing at 7,500 units. Limitations: it assumes costs and revenues are linear (no economies of scale or bulk-buy discounts), assumes all output is sold at one price (ignores price discrimination and demand uncertainty), treats fixed costs as constant over all output levels, and is only valid for a single-product firm or constant sales mix; it is a short-run, static model that ignores changes in technology or cost structure. Overall, break-even analysis is a valuable first approximation for planning and risk assessment, but decisions should be supported with market research and sensitivity analysis rather than relied on in isolation.
