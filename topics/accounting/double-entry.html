Double Entry and the Ledger

Year 1 / ASYear 2 / A-Level AQA

A* revision of the double entry model for AQA 7127 (3.3-3.4): debits and credits, books of prime entry, trial balance, suspense accounts, control accounts and bank reconciliation, with exam-style error correction and the benefits and costs of the double entry system.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Apply the accounting equation and the dual effect of every transaction to decide the correct debit and credit entries for any transaction (AQA 7127, 3.3) (AO1/AO2).
  • Select the correct book of prime entry for each transaction type and post accurately to the ledger using folio references (3.3) (AO2).
  • Prepare and interpret a trial balance, naming the six error types it fails to detect and justifying why it is not proof of accuracy (3.4) (AO1/AO3).
  • Open, use and clear a suspense account through the journal, and assess the effect of each correction on profit for the year (3.4) (AO2/AO3).
  • Prepare sales and purchases ledger control accounts, reconcile them to lists of balances, and reconcile the cash book to the bank statement (3.4) (AO2).
  • Evaluate the benefits and costs of operating a double entry bookkeeping system, using business examples (3.1/3.3) (AO3).

📋 Key Definitions and Core Concepts

Accounting equation: Assets = Capital + Liabilities, restated internationally as Assets = Equity + Liabilities. It must hold after every transaction because of the dual effect.
Capital: The owner's claim on the business: opening capital plus profits minus drawings; called equity in company accounts.
Book of prime entry: The first place a transaction is recorded before posting to the ledger - sales day book, purchases day book, returns books, cash book or general journal.
Trade discount: A reduction off list price given to trade customers; deducted before the transaction is recorded, so it never appears in the ledger accounts.
Settlement (cash) discount: A discount for prompt payment, recorded only when taken: discount allowed (expense) for the seller, discount received (income) for the buyer.
Trial balance: A list of all ledger balances at a date with debit and credit columns; it tests the arithmetic accuracy of double entry only, not completeness or correctness of classification.
Suspense account: A temporary account holding the difference when the trial balance fails to agree (or when a one-sided error is found before it is corrected); cleared by journal entries and closed before final accounts are prepared.
Control account: A summary account for trade receivables or trade payables in the nominal ledger, built from day books and the cash book; its balance is checked against the sum of the individual personal accounts.

📌 Key Points

Debit: expenses, assets, drawings increase. Credit: liabilities, capital/equity, income increase. Revenue received in advance is a liability, not income.
Balanced errors (complete omission, same wrong amount both sides, wrong account, reversal, principle, compensating) never break the trial balance; only one-sided errors do.
Only one-sided errors need a suspense account. A correction made with equal debit and credit via suspense never changes profit; corrections touching income or expense accounts do.
Prepare control accounts from day books and the cash book only - never from the personal ledgers - so the reconciliation actually tests them.
Bank reconciliation: update the cash book first (charges, interest, standing orders, direct debits and credits, dishonoured cheques), then adjust for timing: subtract unpresented cheques, add outstanding lodgements.

The accounting equation, debits and credits, and books of prime entry (AQA 7127, 3.3)

Every transaction has a dual effect: it changes at least two items, and the accounting equation survives each change. If Suneja opens a business with £20,000 from savings, assets (bank) rise £20,000 and capital rises £20,000. If she then buys a van for £6,000 cash, one asset rises and another falls - the equation still balances. If she buys inventory for £2,000 on credit, assets rise £2,000 and liabilities (payables) rise £2,000. The equation is not a formula to memorise for one question: it is the logic that generates every debit and credit.

Account categoryTo increaseTo decrease
Assets (non-current and current)DebitCredit
Expenses (including purchases)DebitCredit
DrawingsDebitCredit
LiabilitiesCreditDebit
Capital / equityCreditDebit
Income (sales, rent receivable, discount received)CreditDebit

A four-step routine decides any entry. Step 1: identify the two (or more) accounts affected. Step 2: classify each as asset, liability, capital, income or expense. Step 3: decide whether each increases or decreases. Step 4: apply the table. Test it: pay wages £800 in cash. Wages (expense) increase, so debit wages £800; cash (asset) decreases, so credit cash £800. Receive £500 rent from a sub-tenant: debit bank (asset up), credit rent receivable (income up). Owner takes goods worth £150: debit drawings £150, credit purchases £150 - goods drawn are recorded at cost, credited to purchases, not sales.

The Pell settlement shows the settlement discount rule: only the discount actually taken is recorded, split so the payables account is cleared in full. A trade discount, by contrast, would simply mean the purchases were recorded at, say, £2,280 rather than list price.

Transactions are first entered in a book of prime entry, which keeps the ledger clean, records supporting details once, and creates an audit trail. The sales day book lists credit sales invoices (total posted to debit sales, each invoice posted to the credit of the individual customer). The purchases day book does the mirror image for credit purchases. Returns inwards and returns outwards books handle credit notes and debit notes. The cash book records all bank and cash movements and is both a book of prime entry and a ledger account, often extended with discount allowed and discount received columns. The petty cash book records small cash payments under analysed heads, using the imprest system: the float is topped up to a fixed amount by the exact value of vouchers, so the petty cashier is automatically checked. The general journal records everything else: opening entries, purchases and sales of non-current assets on credit, corrections of errors, irrecoverable debts written off, and the year-end transfer entries.

  • Sales day book - credit sales invoices; source: copies of sales invoices.
  • Purchases day book - credit purchases; source: suppliers' invoices.
  • Returns inwards / outwards books - credit notes / debit notes.
  • Cash book - receipts and payments; source: till rolls, counterfoils, statements.
  • Petty cash book - small payments; source: petty cash vouchers.
  • General journal - everything not covered above; source: instructions, schedules.

The ledger is split for control: the sales (receivables) ledger holds one account per credit customer, the purchases (payables) ledger one per supplier, and the nominal (general) ledger everything else, including the sales ledger control and purchases ledger control accounts. Every posting carries a folio reference (e.g. SL 14, CB 7) cross-referencing book to account. Folios look cosmetic but they are examinable: they prove each entry was cross-checked, they let any figure be traced both ways, and they are part of the internal control story you must be able to tell in extended answers about the benefits and costs of the double entry system (3.1, 3.3). The costs are real: a full double entry system needs trained staff, books or software, and time; for a micro-business with few transactions the cost can exceed the benefit, which is why incomplete records techniques exist (3.14).

Command-word discipline matters here. For state or identify, give the entry only. For explain, give the entry and why (account classification plus the dual effect). For prepare the journal entries, show date, accounts, amounts, and a narration - AQA mark schemes award for narrations because they demonstrate the transaction, not the habit, has been understood.

Push the dual effect one step further, because examiners love the cases where the equation survives something that looks one-sided. A customer pays £1,200 in December for work to be done in January: bank (asset) rises, but the credit is to deferred income (revenue received in advance) - a liability - because no income has been earned yet. The business receives an electricity bill for £300 but will pay next month: the expense is debited now and the credit sits in accruals, a liability. In both cases the equation holds because a liability absorbs the difference. Candidates who credit sales or debit expenses directly have destroyed the matching that the ledger is supposed to preserve, and the error surfaces in the adjustments section of Paper 1. Similarly, when a non-current asset is bought partly on credit, the debit is the asset but the credits split between bank and the supplier's personal account - two credits against one debit is perfectly valid double entry, since 'two entries' means at least two, not exactly two.

Worked example: discounts - trade versus settlement

Ridge sells goods list price £1,000 to Doherty, allowing 10% trade discount and offering 2.5% settlement discount. Invoice raised: £1,000 less 10% = £900. Entry: Dr Doherty £900, Cr Sales £900 - the trade discount is buried in the figure and never appears separately. If Doherty pays within terms, the settlement discount is £22.50 (2.5% of £900): Dr Bank £877.50, Dr Discount allowed £22.50, Cr Doherty £900. Ridge's books show discount allowed £22.50 (expense). From Doherty's side the mirror image: Dr Ridge £900, Cr Bank £877.50, Cr Discount received £22.50 (income). If Doherty pays late, nothing further is recorded - settlement discounts are recognised only when taken, unlike trade discounts which are recognised by never recording them.

Posting runs in totals with only the personal accounts getting individual attention. If the sales day book for March lists 40 invoices totalling £46,300, the nominal ledger receives one entry - Dr Sales ledger control £46,300, Cr Sales £46,300, folios referencing SDB 3 and NL 22 - while each of the 40 customers' personal accounts is credited with its own invoice amount. This is why the sales ledger control account in section 3 below can be rebuilt from the day books alone: the control account receives exactly the same totals the day book produced. Understanding this flow answers a classic exam question - state the source of each entry in a control account - and stops candidates inventing entries (such as irrecoverable debts written off) that never pass through the day books but only through the journal.

Multiple-choice and short-answer items on 3.3 are usually one-line decisions - state the double entry for paying a supplier by standing order (Dr payables, Cr bank) - so speed comes from classification, not memory. The extended questions reward narrative control: knowing that the cash book doubles as a ledger account, that the journal is the book of corrections, and that every figure in the financial statements can be traced through folio references back to a source document. Hold the whole chain in mind - source document, book of prime entry, ledger, trial balance, financial statements - because every later topic in the specification (adjustments in 3.6, company accounts in 3.16, even the data trail questions on technology) assumes you can move along it in either direction without hesitation.

Balancing the ledger, the trial balance and suspense accounts (AQA 7127, 3.3 and 3.4)

At the reporting date each ledger account is balanced: total both sides, insert the smaller total as balance c/d on the larger side, carry it down as balance b/d on the opposite side. Debit balances (assets, expenses, drawings) and credit balances (liabilities, capital, income) are then listed in a trial balance. Because every transaction was recorded with equal debits and credits, the two columns must agree. If they do not, something is wrong - and the difference is parked in a suspense account so final accounts can be drafted while the error is traced. Suspense is a device, not an account with meaning: a trial balance must agree, and the suspense account must be cleared, before financial statements are finalised.

The crucial A* distinction is between the six error types a balanced trial balance cannot detect and the errors it can. All six undetectable types are balanced errors: both sides were recorded, just wrongly.

Error typeExampleRevealed by TB?
Complete omissionA £400 credit sale never recorded at allNo
Same wrong amount both sidesSale recorded as £540 instead of £450 in both accountsNo
Error of commissionDebited the wrong customer's accountNo
Error of principleMotor expenses debited to motor vehiclesNo
Reversal of entriesSale debited to sales, credited to customerNo
Compensating errorsSales overcast £100, wages overcast £100No
One-sided errors (posting one side only; casting one account wrongly)Cash debited, receivables never creditedYes - TB fails

So a trial balance that agrees is not evidence that the records are correct: any of the six balanced errors could be sitting inside it. That is why verification continues beyond the trial balance with control accounts, bank reconciliation and physical checks (3.4). Conversely, a trial balance that fails tells you a one-sided error exists but not which - hence the suspense account.

Balancing itself is examinable. In the wages account for a month, total debits (wages paid) sit on the left; the balance c/d goes to the right at the period end, and comes back as balance b/d ready to start the next period. Accumulate it over the year and the closing balance transfers to the statement of profit or loss. Permanent accounts (assets, liabilities, capital) carry their balances forward as balance b/d into the next period and appear in the statement of financial position; temporary accounts (income and expenses) are closed off to the statement of profit or loss and start each new period at nil. Knowing which accounts are which tells you instantly where a balance belongs in the final accounts - and it explains why drawings is a debit-balance account deducted from capital rather than an expense.

When the trial balance disagrees, open the suspense account with the difference on the side that is short. If debits exceed credits by £100, suspense carries a debit balance of £100, restoring apparent agreement. Then correct each error through the journal. For each correction ask two questions. First, does this error involve an account missing an entry on one side only (or a wrong amount on one side)? If yes, the suspense account absorbs the imbalance. Second, do the correcting entries touch income or expense accounts? If yes, profit for the year changes.

Worked example: clearing a suspense account and measuring the profit effect

A business's trial balance shows debits £96,000 and credits £95,900, a difference of £100. The suspense account is opened with a debit balance of £100. Three errors are then found:

  1. A cheque of £100 received from a credit customer was debited to the bank account but never credited to the customer's account. Correction: Dr Suspense 100, Cr Trade receivables 100. The missing credit is supplied; suspense is cleared. No profit effect - the error sat entirely within balance-sheet items.
  2. A credit sale of £400 to D Fell was completely omitted. Correction: Dr D Fell 400, Cr Sales 400. No suspense needed - the error was balanced. Profit increases by £400 (revenue previously understated).
  3. Motor expenses of £600 were debited to motor vehicles (error of principle). Correction: Dr Motor expenses 600, Cr Motor vehicles 600. No suspense needed. Profit decreases by £600 (expenses understated, asset overstated).

Net effect on profit: +£400 − £600 = −£200. Note the pattern examiners reward: the correction that used the suspense account (error 1) changed no profit figure, because suspense entries are always equal debits and credits by construction. Profit effects come only from balanced errors corrected through the statement of profit or loss accounts. Also state the balance sheet effects: trade receivables were overstated by £100, motor vehicles overstated by £600 before correction.

Common one-sided errors to watch for in data: a casting error in a single account (e.g. sales day book totalled £9,860 instead of £8,960, posted as £9,860); a posting made to one side of one account only; two debits with no credit; the wrong figure on one side. Common balanced errors that trap candidates: writing off an irrecoverable debt but crediting the customer's account and forgetting the receivables control account interplay, or treating drawings of goods as at selling price (it is at cost, credited to purchases). Reversals (type 4) are corrected by doubling the wrong entry: if a £250 receipt was reversed, debit and credit the original accounts again with £250 to cancel, then post correctly - a two-step journal worth stating explicitly for full marks.

Remember the statement of financial position consequences as well as the profit consequences, because part (b) of these questions almost always asks for both. An uncorrected error of principle (expenses sitting in motor vehicles) overstates both profit and non-current assets; an uncorrected omission of a credit sale understates profit, trade receivables and revenue. And a suspense account must be cleared before the financial statements are published: a suspense balance in a published statement of financial position signals uncorrected records and would prevent the accounts from showing a true and fair view. In exam answers, finish every suspense question by writing 'the suspense account now has a nil balance' - it is a marking point in its own right.

For the extended-answer variant - discuss whether a trial balance that agrees proves the accounts are accurate - structure as: (i) purpose of the trial balance (arithmetic check of double entry); (ii) the six undetectable error types with one example each; (iii) what does provide assurance (control accounts reconciled, bank reconciliation, physical stock counts, internal check segregation); (iv) judgement that a balanced TB is necessary but far from sufficient. That fourth move - the justified conclusion - is what separates the top band from mid band in AQA's 25-mark Section C questions.

Worked example: the imprest petty cash system

Petty cash float £200, restored by imprest. During the week vouchers totalled £87 (postage £34, cleaning £28, stationery £25). The cashier reimburses exactly £87, restoring the float to £200. Check: float £200 = vouchers £87 + cash in box £113. The verification is built in - if cash plus vouchers does not equal the imprest amount, something is missing, and the analysis columns feed the nominal accounts (Dr postage, Dr cleaning, Dr stationery, Cr cash) without cluttering the main cash book.

Finally, link the model to the accountant's role (3.1). Double entry is not paperwork for its own sake: it is what allows the trial balance to self-check the books, the financial statements to be prepared from ledger balances rather than reconstructed by guesswork, and managers to interrogate any figure back to its source document. When you are asked to evaluate the double entry system, anchor each benefit to this mechanism and each cost to the resources it consumes - that is the difference between assertion and analysis.

Verification: control accounts and bank reconciliation (AQA 7127, 3.4)

Verification goes beyond the trial balance. The three named mechanisms in 3.4 are control accounts, the bank reconciliation and checks on the personal ledgers. Their shared logic: build a figure independently from summary records, then compare it with the detailed records. Any difference must be explained and corrected. In an exam this is one of the highest-yield preparation areas because the formats are fixed and the marks are mechanical.

A sales ledger control account summarises everything owed by credit customers; the purchases ledger control account summarises everything owed to suppliers. Critical rule: build them only from the day books, returns books and cash book - never from the individual personal accounts - otherwise the check is circular. Items are posted in totals, with the other side of each entry to the nominal account named in brackets.

The purchases ledger control is the mirror image: debits for payments, discounts received, returns outwards and contras; credits for opening balances and credit purchases. A contra (set-off) arises where the business is both customer and supplier of the same firm: the two balances are netted, Dr Purchases ledger control, Cr Sales ledger control, with no cash movement. Reconciling the control account to the list of personal balances: prepare the control account, list and total the individual balances, then investigate the difference. Typical causes: a transaction omitted from the list of balances but in the control account (or vice versa); a casting error in either; a posting to the wrong personal account (commission - affects the list total, not the control account); or an error in the control account itself. Explain each difference, correct it, and show that both now agree.

Control accounts deliver control benefits you should be able to evaluate for AO3: they localise errors (the discrepancy is inside one ledger, not the whole system); they deter fraud because a different clerk maintains the personal ledgers from the one who prepares the control account (internal check through segregation of duties); they give the trial balance a quick receivables/payables figure; and for large businesses they allow the sales ledger to be split across clerks without losing overall control. The cost is duplication of bookkeeping work and the need for competent, independent staff.

The bank reconciliation follows the same compare-and-explain logic between the cash book and the bank statement. Always in this order. Step 1: update the cash book for items on the statement not yet in the books - bank charges and interest, standing orders and direct debits, direct credits (customers paying directly), dishonoured cheques, and any bank errors. The corrected cash book balance is the figure that appears in the statement of financial position. Step 2: list timing differences, which need no entry: cheques paid in but not yet credited (outstanding lodgements) and cheques issued but not yet presented. Step 3: start from the updated cash book balance and adjust for timing, or start from the statement balance and work back - both must land on the same number.

Worked example: full bank reconciliation

Cash book shows a debit balance of £4,320. The bank statement shows £4,880. The statement reveals bank charges £50, a direct credit from a customer of £200, unpresented cheques £1,150, and a lodgement of £740 not yet credited.

Mark-scheme pattern: the £50 and £200 go through the cash book with double entries (Dr/Cr bank); the £1,150 and £740 appear in no ledger account at all - they are pure timing. Candidates who 'correct' the cash book for unpresented cheques throw marks away every year.

Two reconciliation wrinkles appear regularly. A dishonoured cheque from a customer (insufficient funds, or a technical error) reverses the original receipt: Dr Trade receivables, Cr Bank - the customer owes again, so it appears on the debit side of the sales ledger control account and the customer's personal account. A bank error (for example the bank debiting your account with another customer's direct debit) is corrected by notifying the bank; it appears only in the reconciliation statement, never in your books, until the bank corrects it. Remember too that the figure reported in the statement of financial position is the corrected cash book balance - if that is negative, the overdraft is a current liability.

The purchases ledger control account is the exact mirror image, and being able to state its sources is a standard short-answer demand.

To reconcile either control account with its list of balances: prepare the control account from the summary books; list and total the individual balances from the memorandum ledger; then explain and correct any difference line by line. Typical causes, each with its correcting treatment: a transaction in the control account but omitted from the list (add it to the list); a casting error in one personal account (correct that account); a posting to the wrong personal account - an error of commission - which distorts the list total but not the control account (transfer the amount between the two personal accounts); an entry missing from the control account itself (post it); or a debit balance among the payables (an advance payment) listed as a negative. The discipline of explaining each difference, not just adjusting, is what the mark scheme pays for.

Tie it together for the extended question on the benefits and costs of operating a double entry bookkeeping system (3.3, with links to 3.1). Benefits: it self-checks (trial balance), it detects errors and deters fraud through control accounts and reconciliation, it produces reliable financial statements and management information for decisions, it supports tax compliance and audit, and it permits performance comparison over time. Costs: staffing and training; software purchase and maintenance; data security investment; time taken per transaction; risk that over-complex systems generate information nobody uses. The evaluation point AQA rewards: the benefits grow with the size and complexity of the business, so the optimal system differs between a market trader (incomplete records may suffice) and a limited company (full double entry is legally required). Conclude with a judgement, not a list.

In Paper 1 this material typically appears as a 20-mark structured question (prepare a control account, then reconcile, then explain one cause of difference) or inside a Section C scenario asking you to evaluate internal control. Whatever the wrapper, the mechanics are identical: know the source of every control account line, know which items enter the cash book and which are pure timing, and be ready to state double entries for the adjustments (dishonoured cheque, contra, write-off). Drill the formats until you can produce a balanced control account in under five minutes - the time saved funds the evaluation paragraphs that decide the top grades.

💡 Worked Example

Exam-style: Explain the purpose of a trial balance, and discuss whether a trial balance that agrees proves that the ledger accounts are free from errors. Support your answer with named error types and examples. (12 marks)
Mark-scheme logic: AO1 (knowledge, ~4 marks): the trial balance lists every ledger balance at a date in debit and credit columns; because each transaction carries equal debits and credits, the columns should agree, so it tests the arithmetic accuracy of the double entry system. AO2 (application, ~2 marks): reference to what happens when it fails - the difference is placed in a suspense account until corrected by journal. AO3 (analysis/evaluation, ~6 marks): a balanced trial balance does NOT prove accuracy because balanced errors never disturb it. Must name and illustrate at least four of: complete omission (transaction never recorded); error of commission (correct amounts, wrong account of the same class); error of principle (correct amounts, wrong class of account, e.g. capital expenditure to purchases); original entry error (wrong figure on both sides); reversal of entries; compensating errors. Conclude with judgement: the trial balance is a necessary but insufficient check; assurance requires control account reconciliations, bank reconciliation, stock counts and internal check (segregation of duties). Top answers structure as argument, not list, and end with a supported conclusion.

❓ Practice Questions & Mark Schemes

Prepare journal entries with narrations for each of the following transactions of a sole trader: (i) bought goods on credit from Pell Ltd £2,400; (ii) sold goods on credit to Kaur £3,100; (iii) returned goods to Pell Ltd £180; (iv) Kaur paid the amount due by cheque; (v) the owner took goods costing £150 for personal use. (10 marks)
Answer: (i) Dr Purchases £2,400, Cr Pell Ltd £2,400 (goods bought on credit from Pell Ltd). (ii) Dr Kaur £3,100, Cr Sales £3,100 (credit sale to Kaur). (iii) Dr Pell Ltd £180, Cr Purchases returns £180 (goods returned to supplier). (iv) Dr Bank £3,100, Cr Kaur £3,100 (receipt from customer clears the personal account). (v) Dr Drawings £150, Cr Purchases £150 (goods withdrawn by owner at cost). Mark points: correct accounts named (5), correct debit/credit sides (5); narration required for full marks. Common traps: (v) credited to Sales or valued at selling price - drawings of goods are at cost and credited to purchases.
A trial balance shows debit balances totalling £96,000 and credit balances totalling £95,900. (a) Open the suspense account. (b) The following errors are then found: a cheque for £100 received from a credit customer was debited to bank but never credited to the customer's account; a credit sale of £400 to D Fell was completely omitted; motor expenses of £600 were debited to motor vehicles. Show the journal entries to correct each error and clear the suspense account, and state the effect of each correction on profit for the year. (12 marks)
Answer: (a) Suspense account opened with a DEBIT balance of £100 (debits exceed credits by £100), so the trial balance now appears to agree. (b) Error 1 (one-sided): Dr Suspense £100, Cr Trade receivables £100 - suspense cleared (Dr 100 cancelled by Cr 100); no effect on profit. Error 2 (complete omission): Dr D Fell £400, Cr Sales £400 - profit INCREASES by £400 as revenue was understated; no suspense involved. Error 3 (principle): Dr Motor expenses £600, Cr Motor vehicles £600 - profit DECREASES by £600 as expenses were understated and assets overstated; no suspense involved. Net effect on profit: +£400 − £600 = −£200. Mark logic: suspense side/direction (2); each correction Dr and Cr correct with narration (6); profit effects identified per error plus net figure (4). Key principle: only one-sided errors use suspense, and suspense corrections never change profit because they are equal debits and credits.
From the following, prepare the sales ledger control account for March: opening balance of trade receivables £18,400; credit sales £96,500; cash and cheques received from customers £88,200; discounts allowed £1,300; irrecoverable debts written off £950; returns inwards £1,150; contra with the purchases ledger £700. State the closing balance. (8 marks)
Answer: Debit side: balance b/d £18,400; credit sales £96,500 (total £114,900). Credit side: cash and cheques received £88,200; discounts allowed £1,300; irrecoverable debts written off £950; returns inwards £1,150; contra (Dr purchases ledger control / Cr sales ledger control) £700; balance c/d £22,600 (total £114,900). Closing balance £22,600 carried down as balance b/d on the debit side (asset). Mark points: each correct item on the correct side (6); correct balancing and carried-down figure (2). Common trap: putting the contra on the debit side - the set-off reduces what customers owe, so it is a credit.
The cash book shows a debit balance of £4,320 but the bank statement shows £4,880. The statement shows bank charges of £50, a direct credit from a customer of £200, unpresented cheques of £1,150 and an outstanding lodgement of £740. Prepare the adjusted cash book and reconcile it to the bank statement. (8 marks)
Answer: Adjusted cash book: balance b/d £4,320, less bank charges £50, add direct credit £200, giving corrected balance £4,470 (Dr). Bank charges: Dr Bank charges £50, Cr Bank £50. Direct credit: Dr Bank £200, Cr Trade receivables £200. Reconciliation: statement balance £4,880 less unpresented cheques £1,150, add outstanding lodgement £740 = £4,470, agreeing with the adjusted cash book. Mark logic: charges and direct credit routed through the cash book with double entries (4); timing differences NOT entered in the cash book, only used in the reconciliation (2); both sides reach £4,470 (2). The £4,470 is the figure reported in the statement of financial position.
Evaluate the benefits and costs to a growing sole trader of introducing a full double entry bookkeeping system with control accounts, instead of continuing with incomplete records. (12 marks)
Answer: AO1: a double entry system records the dual effect of every transaction in books of prime entry and the ledger; control accounts independently summarise receivables and payables and are reconciled to the personal ledgers. AO2 applied to a growing sole trader: growth means more customers, suppliers and transactions, more chance of error and fraud, and rising need for finance and management information. Benefits (developed): arithmetic self-checking via the trial balance; errors localised by control accounts and internal check (segregation even within a small team); reliable statement of profit or loss and financial position supports loan applications and tax returns; benchmarking over time possible. Costs (developed): training or hiring a bookkeeper; software purchase/maintenance; time per transaction; data security costs. Evaluation: benefits rise with transaction volume and external scrutiny (bank, HMRC), while costs are largely fixed once systems exist - so as the business grows the balance tips toward full double entry; for a very small trader the cost may still exceed the benefit. Judgement with justification required for the top band (e.g. adopt a computerised double entry package now, before growth makes the changeover costly).
